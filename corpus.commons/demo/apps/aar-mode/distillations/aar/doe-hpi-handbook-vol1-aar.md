# US DOE, Human Performance Improvement Handbook Vol. 1, After-Action Review Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Department of Energy (2009). *Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 1: Concepts and Principles*. DOE-HDBK-1028-2009. Distribution Statement A, approved for public release. Licence: **Public domain (US Government work, 17 USC §105)**; DOE asks for acknowledgement in reuse. **Scope:** open. Third-party material inside the handbook (Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model, Mager and Pipe's gap analysis, INPO-derived lists, and the tests and decision tree it adapts from Reason and Johnston) is paraphrased here, never quoted.

## After-Action Review Relevance

The handbook is not an AAR manual; it is DOE's account of why people err and how organisations should respond. Two of its moves are exactly what an AAR facilitator needs and what the rest of the demo corpus carries only in part. First, it gives an **error taxonomy with a matching remedy for each type**: slips and lapses in the skill-based mode, misapplied rules in the rule-based mode, and mistakes from an inaccurate mental model in the knowledge-based mode, with a direct statement that retraining is the wrong fix for the first, may fix the second, and that the third needs analysis first (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 1*, Ch 2, pp. 2-27 to 2-28). Second, it gives an **event model** (the Anatomy of an Event) that runs from organisational values and processes, through latent organisational weaknesses, to error precursors and flawed controls, to the initiating action and the event, and it tells investigators to work that model backward to show the "line of sight" from organisational weaknesses to consequences (Ch 1, pp. 1-14 to 1-15; Ch 3, pp. 3-22 to 3-23).

Around those two moves the handbook supplies a just-culture apparatus (blame cycle, error-versus-violation balance, foresight and substitution tests, and a culpability decision tree adapted from Reason, with the estimate that about 90 percent or more of unsafe acts are blameless), a defence-in-depth account of why severity tracks controls rather than errors, and a named instance of normalisation: the Columbia foam strikes, "normalized to the point where they were simply viewed as a “maintenance” issue" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-6). In AAR work it fires hardest in Phase 2 (contributory factors), Phase 3 (just-culture sorting) and Phase 4 (action design), with lighter use in Phases 1 and 5. It complements TC 25-20, LFUO 2024 and the NHS guide; where it differs from LFUO (it keeps a "root cause" vocabulary), see Integration.

## Key Concepts for After-Action Review

1.  **Error, slip, lapse, mistake, violation.** "Error is behavior without malice or forethought; it is not a result" [V]. Slips are physical actions that fail to achieve the immediate objective; lapses are failures of memory or recall; mistakes come from "an inadequate plan to achieve the intended outcome" [V] (Source: Ch 2, p. 2-8). A violation is "a deliberate, intentional act to evade a known policy or procedure requirement" [V] (Ch 1, p. 1-14), and "Most violations are well intentioned, arising from a genuine desire to get a job done according to management’s wishes" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-10). For an AAR, naming which of these happened is a precondition for choosing an action, not a verdict on the person.

2.  **Performance modes and their error modes.** Skill-based work (practised, automatic) fails by inattention; rule-based work (IF-THEN rules) fails by misinterpretation, "applying the wrong response to a work situation, or applying the correct procedure to the wrong situation" [V]; knowledge-based work (no rule applies) fails by "an inaccurate mental model of the system, process, or facility status" [V] (Ch 2, pp. 2-22 to 2-26). The handbook adds that knowledge-based mode "might better be called “lack of knowledge” mode" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-25), so expert work is not knowledge-based just because experts did it.

3.  **Match the action to the mode.** Retraining a skill-based slip "is a waste of time and is an insult to the worker" [V]; rule-based errors "may be corrected through retraining" [V]; and "Corrective action to reduce knowledge-based mistakes is more complicated" [V], needing an analysis of what went wrong first (Ch 2, pp. 2-27 to 2-28). For skill-based errors the handbook sends the reviewer to observation and one-on-one inquiry into tools, procedures, distractions, interruptions, fatigue and stress, because "Those conditions can be controlled" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-27).

4.  **Anatomy of an Event.** Latent organisational weaknesses create error precursors and flawed controls; an initiating action, correct, in error or in violation, then produces the event; "Breaking the linkages may prevent events" [V] (Ch 1, p. 1-14). Investigators can work it backward in four areas of fact (consequences; initiating actions and the precursors that provoked them; flawed controls; the organisational weaknesses behind each) so that "the analysis should clearly show the causal links (line of sight) from the organizational weaknesses to the event consequences" [V] (Ch 3, pp. 3-22 to 3-23).

5.  **Active versus latent errors.** Active errors have immediate, visible consequences and are mostly made by the people who touch equipment; latent errors "result in hidden organization-related weaknesses or equipment flaws that lie dormant" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-9), can be made by anyone from managers to workers, and accumulate: "Once they are created they do not fade away, but rather they accumulate in the system" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-16). An NRC study the handbook cites found 81 percent of 270 errors in 35 events were latent [BT] (Ch 2, p. 2-9).

6.  **Error precursors and error-likely situations.** Precursors are "unfavorable conditions embedded in the job site that create mismatches between a task and the individual" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-31), in four categories: task demands, individual capabilities, work environment, human nature. An error-likely situation needs a specific action inside those conditions (Ch 2, p. 2-30). For an AAR timeline, precursors are what was present at the moment of the initiating action.

7.  **Severity is set by controls, not by the error.** "Whereas human error typically triggers an event, it is the number of controls and the weaknesses of those controls that dictate the severity of the event" [V]; focusing only on error reduction is "a bad strategy" [V], since it only lengthens the time between events (Ch 3, p. 3-3). Engineered controls are more reliable than administrative ones, and "If it is imperative to prevent error, then physical, engineered controls are more appropriate" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-5).

8.  **What investigation should ask.** Root cause analysis "should focus on what could have prevented the event rather than simply concentrating on who caused an event" [V], and should note which controls kept it from being worse (Ch 3, p. 3-22). "“Inattention to detail” and “not following procedures” are not root causes" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-22). Through Dekker, the handbook adds that hindsight distorts investigations and that the analyst should establish, for each person, their goals, their focus and what they knew at critical points [BT] (Ch 3, p. 3-22).

9.  **The blame cycle.** Driven by the belief that people err because they lack motivation, the cycle runs from error through counselling or discipline to reduced trust, less communication, less management awareness of job-site conditions, persisting latent weaknesses and more error [AP] (Ch 4, p. 4-23). "Events will continue as long as event investigations stop prematurely at the active human error" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-23).

10.  **Just culture: balance, intent, culpability.** "A just environment is all about getting the balance right between how willful violations and unintentional errors are addressed in the organization" [V], with zero tolerance for reckless conduct and honest errors unpunished (Ch 4, p. 4-24). Judging people "based on the consequence of their action, not the intent of the action" [V] is the failure (Ch 4, p. 4-23). The foresight test, the substitution test and a culpability decision tree adapted from Reason help sort individual acts; the handbook cites experience that about 90 percent or more of unsafe acts fall in the blameless category [BT] (Ch 4, pp. 4-24 to 4-30).

11.  **Normalisation and drift.** The bald-tire attitude is "A belief that past performance is justification for not changing (improving) existing practices or conditions" [V]; its example is Columbia, where "Learned attitudes about foam strikes diminished management’s wariness of their danger" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-6). At-risk behaviours are rarely penalised, become automatic, and lead people to underestimate risk until an event occurs (Ch 2, p. 2-7). Through resilience-engineering research, the handbook names a drift toward failure that precedes major events as controls erode under production pressure and change [BT] (Ch 5, p. 5-16).

12.  **Team errors.** "Competence does not guarantee positive control" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-15). The handbook names the halo effect, pilot/co-pilot deference (Air Florida Flight 90, where the co-pilot flagged the engine readings at least five times but did not oppose the take-off), free riding (Bhopal), groupthink and diffusion of responsibility, the last illustrated by a DOE pump replacement in which a group of supervisors bypassed work control under schedule pressure [AE] (Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15).

## Questions to Ask During After-Action Review

### Phase 0: Scoping

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Will people report honestly? | Do participants believe their errors will be punished? If so, the handbook's warning applies: "information related to those errors will likely remain obscure" [V]. What has been said, and by whom, about how admissions will be used? | Ch 4, p. 4-13 |
| Is the reporting culture in place, or only the just culture? | Beyond a no-blame statement, does the review offer de-identification or confidentiality, protection from discipline for honest errors, separation of the learning function from discipline, feedback, and an easy way to tell a story? | Ch 4, Att. C, p. 4-31 |
| Is a near miss being skipped because nothing was damaged? | Is this event being dismissed as minor? The handbook treats a near miss as a situation where safeguards may have been breached without loss, and holds that even small failures are windows into the system. | Glossary, p. vi; Ch 4, p. 4-10 |

### Phase 1: Timeline and Local-Rationality Reconstruction

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What did each person know? | For each person at each critical point: what were they trying to accomplish, what were they paying attention to, and what did they know then? | Ch 3, p. 3-22 (via Dekker) |
| What was present at the moment of the action? | Which precursors were in play when the initiating action happened: time pressure, high workload, unfamiliarity, distractions, departure from routine, confusing displays, stress, habit, assumptions? | Ch 2, pp. 2-31 to 2-32 |
| Which mode was the person in? | Was the action practised and automatic (skill-based), a rule being applied (rule-based), or problem-solving with no rule (knowledge-based)? | Ch 2, pp. 2-19 to 2-27 |
| Did someone see it and not speak? | Did anyone notice the problem and defer to a senior person, assume someone else was checking, or keep quiet to preserve harmony? | Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15 |
| Is hindsight shaping the accounts? | Are we describing facts known only after the event as if they were knowable before it? | Ch 3, p. 3-22 |

### Phase 2: Contributory-Factor Analysis

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Has the analysis stopped at the active error? | Having named the initiating action, have we gone on to the precursors, the flawed or missing controls, and the organisational weaknesses behind each? Can we draw the line of sight? | Ch 3, pp. 3-22 to 3-23; Ch 4, p. 4-23 |
| Which controls failed or were missing, and which worked? | What controls should have prevented the error or limited its consequence? Which ones did limit it? | Ch 1, p. 1-15; Ch 3, p. 3-22 |
| Why was the consequence this large? | Was the initiating error ordinary, with severity set by weak or missing controls? | Ch 3, p. 3-3 |
| Is a latent error in play? | Was a weakness introduced earlier (a design, a procedure, a mislabel, a decision) and lying dormant until this action exposed it? Who could have created it, including managers and engineers? | Ch 2, p. 2-9 |
| Has a deviation been normalised? | Has this condition or practice happened before without harm, so that it came to be seen as acceptable or "maintenance"? | Ch 2, pp. 2-6 to 2-7 |
| Is "inattention to detail" or "not following procedures" on the list? | If so, what made inattention likely here, and what made the procedure unused or unusable? | Ch 3, p. 3-22 |
| Did the team's dynamics contribute? | Was a check skipped because the performer was trusted, a junior deferred, someone free-rode, or a group took a risk no individual would have? | Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15 |

### Phase 3: Just-Culture Sorting

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Error or violation? | Was the departure unintended (slip, lapse, mistake) or a deliberate departure from a known rule? If deliberate, was it well intentioned, aimed at getting the job done? | Ch 1, p. 1-14; Ch 2, pp. 2-8 to 2-10 |
| Is the judgement following consequence or intent? | Would we be having this conversation about the same act if it had caused no harm? | Ch 4, pp. 4-23 to 4-24 |
| Would a peer have done the same? | Could a well-motivated, equally competent and similarly qualified person have made the same error in the same circumstances as they were perceived at the time? If not, were there deficiencies in training, selection or experience? | Ch 4, p. 4-25; Att. B, pp. 4-29 to 4-30 (adapted from Reason and Johnston) |
| Were the expectations workable? | If a rule was broken, was it available, intelligible, workable and correct for the situation? | Ch 4, pp. 4-26, 4-29 |
| Is one act being judged at a time? | With several unsafe acts in the sequence, is each being assessed separately, by a small team rather than one manager? | Ch 4, Att. B, p. 4-29 |
| Has the organisation looked at itself? | Have we asked how the organisation failed the individual, and what flaws in processes, policies or procedures allowed the error? | Ch 4, p. 4-26 |

### Phase 4: Action Design

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Does the action fit the error type? | Is retraining proposed for a skill-based slip or lapse? If so, what would change the conditions instead: tools, interruptions, distractions, job aids, fatigue? | Ch 2, p. 2-27 |
| Is a rule-based error getting a rule-based fix? | Does the action improve the rule, its clarity, or the person's understanding of where it applies? | Ch 2, pp. 2-27 to 2-28 |
| Has a knowledge-based mistake been analysed before acting? | Was it a gap in knowledge, or adequate knowledge undone by weak problem-solving, team error or poor communication? | Ch 2, p. 2-28 |
| Does the action reduce frequency, severity, or both? | Is every action an error-reduction measure (reminders, care, attention), or does at least one strengthen a control? | Ch 1, p. 1-16; Ch 3, p. 3-3 |
| Could an engineered control replace an administrative one? | Where preventing the error is imperative, is there a physical or design control (interlock, forcing function, separation) instead of another procedure step? | Ch 3, p. 3-5 |
| Does the action mark the critical step? | Is the step where error causes irreversible harm identified as such in the procedure and the briefing? | Glossary, p. ii; Ch 3, p. 3-7 |
| Does a latent weakness need leadership? | Which contributors sit in management processes or values, and who owns them? | Ch 1, p. 1-15; Ch 3, p. 3-11 |

### Phase 5: Learning-Loop Closure

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Will the people who reported see change? | What will the people who gave accounts see change, and when? The handbook holds that "the worker must see appropriate changes in response to his or her feedback" [V]. | Ch 3, p. 3-19 |
| Will closure be verified? | Who will check that each action was completed and worked, independently of the people who did it? | Ch 3, pp. 3-23 to 3-24 |
| Is this a recurrence? | Has this pattern of error appeared before? Recurrent errors are indicators of latent organisational weaknesses. | Ch 4, p. 4-10 |
| Are procedure problems being fed back? | Did the review surface procedure flaws (two actions in one step, unmarked critical steps, a slow revision process)? Who will change them? | Ch 3, pp. 3-7 to 3-8 |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| The action list says "retrain the operator" for a slip on a familiar task | Remedy mismatched to error type: retraining a skill-based slip "is a waste of time and is an insult to the worker" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-27) | Ask what in the conditions made the slip likely (interruptions, identical controls, fatigue) and act there |
| The finding is "inattention to detail" or "did not follow procedure" | A label in place of a cause; the handbook says these "are not root causes" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-22) | Ask what made attention hard and what made the procedure unused; work back through precursors and controls |
| A severe outcome, an ordinary error | Severity set by controls (Ch 3, p. 3-3) | Map the controls that should have stopped the consequence; ask why each failed or was absent |
| "We've always done it this way and it's been fine" | Bald-tire attitude; normalised deviation (Ch 2, p. 2-6) | Ask how long the practice has run, how often it came close, and what kept it from causing harm until now |
| Everyone on the timeline touched equipment; no one decided anything earlier | Latent errors left out (Ch 2, p. 2-9) | Add the earlier decisions, designs and procedure changes that set the conditions |
| Someone knew and said nothing | Team error: pilot/co-pilot deference, free riding, or groupthink (Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15) | Ask what made speaking up costly; treat the answer as a contributory factor |
| Discipline is proposed only because harm resulted | Consequence, not intent, is driving the judgement (Ch 4, p. 4-23) | Run the substitution question; ask whether the same act without harm would have been noticed |
| Action items are all reminders and briefings | Error reduction only; frequency without severity (Ch 1, p. 1-16) | Add at least one action that strengthens or adds a control, preferring engineered over administrative where prevention is imperative |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- The review needs to name what kind of error happened, and the proposed action needs to fit it (especially when retraining is on the table).
- The analysis has stopped at the person who acted, and the facilitator needs a model (Anatomy of an Event) to work backward through precursors, controls and organisational weaknesses.
- The outcome was severe and the error ordinary, and the group needs a frame for why controls, not errors, set severity.
- A long-tolerated practice or condition is in the timeline, and the group needs language for normalisation.
- A just-culture call needs the error-versus-violation distinction and the balance between them, alongside the NHS tree.

Do not use it as the AAR procedure (TC 25-20 and LFUO 2024 carry that) or in place of the NHS five-test tree for an individual-action decision; the handbook's own tree is an adaptation of Reason's and is paraphrased here. Its setting is DOE nuclear and laboratory facilities; carrying it to other work is this projection's move.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored; nothing in the handbook concerns software. A data team reviews an incident in which an engineer ran a cleanup script against the production database instead of staging, deleting a day of customer records; restoration took nine hours. The engineer had two terminal windows open with identical prompts, had run the same script against staging several times that week, and was interrupted by a chat message just before running it. Running scripts by hand against production had been the team's practice for two years "and had always been fine". Backups were taken nightly but restores had never been tested.

**Phase 1.** The facilitator asks what the engineer was trying to do, what they were paying attention to, and what they knew at that moment (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 1*, Ch 3, p. 3-22, via Dekker). The account places the action in the skill-based mode: a practised command, run from habit, after an interruption. The precursors are on the handbook's common list: repetitive action, distraction or interruption, identical displays, habit pattern (Ch 2, p. 2-32).

**Phase 2.** Someone proposes "inattention to detail" as the root cause. The facilitator notes the handbook's position that such labels "are not root causes" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-22) and works the Anatomy of an Event backward: the initiating action (a slip), the precursors (identical prompts, interruption), the flawed or missing controls (no separation of production credentials, no confirmation step, untested restores), and the organisational weaknesses behind them (two years of hand-run scripts that had become normal). The nine-hour restore, not the slip, set the severity:

> Whereas human error typically triggers an event, it is the number of controls and the weaknesses of those controls that dictate the severity of the event. [V] (Source: Ch 3, p. 3-3)

The "always been fine" practice is read as the bald-tire attitude (Ch 2, p. 2-6).

**Phase 3.** A manager asks whether the engineer should face consequences. The facilitator asks the substitution question: could a similarly skilled colleague, with two identical windows and the same interruption, have done the same? The team says yes. The handbook's tree treats that as an organisationally induced error, and the facilitator reads out its reframing:

> Events triggered by human error are often symptomatic of a system failure. Instead of asking how the individual failed the organization, the question 'how did the organization fail the individual?' would be more appropriate. [V] (Source: Ch 4, p. 4-26)

**Phase 4.** The first draft of actions reads "engineer to complete database-safety training". The facilitator points to the handbook's line on skill-based errors:

> But, retraining workers to do work that is already basically memorized and automatic, performed with little conscious thought because of the nature of the work, is a waste of time and is an insult to the worker. [V] (Source: Ch 2, p. 2-27)

The team replaces it with actions aimed at the conditions and the controls: distinct production prompts and credentials, a confirmation step that names the target database (an engineered control, preferred because "If it is imperative to prevent error, then physical, engineered controls are more appropriate" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-5)), and a quarterly restore test, which reduces severity rather than frequency (Ch 1, p. 1-16). In this scenario the review closes with three owned actions and no disciplinary referral, and the facilitator books a check that the restore test actually ran (Ch 3, pp. 3-23 to 3-24).

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Retraining the slip.** Signal: a skill-based error on a familiar task gets a training action. Diagnosis: remedy mismatched to error type (Ch 2, p. 2-27). Follow-up: find the conditions that made the slip likely and change those.
- **Stopping at the active error.** Signal: the timeline ends at the person who acted. Diagnosis: the blame cycle; "Events will continue as long as event investigations stop prematurely at the active human error" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-23). Follow-up: work the Anatomy of an Event backward to the organisational weaknesses.
- **Labels as causes.** Signal: "inattention to detail", "did not follow procedure", "human error" as findings. Diagnosis: the handbook rejects the first two as root causes (Ch 3, p. 3-22). Follow-up: ask what made each likely.
- **Frequency-only actions.** Signal: every action asks people to be more careful. Diagnosis: error reduction alone, which only lengthens the time between events (Ch 3, p. 3-3). Follow-up: add a control that limits severity.
- **"Pay attention" as the fix.** Signal: an action that tells people to concentrate. Diagnosis: the handbook says avoiding error "is not as simple as telling someone to “pay attention.”" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-19). Follow-up: reduce interruptions and distractions instead.
- **Judging by outcome.** Signal: the same act draws discipline only when it caused harm. Diagnosis: consequence, not intent, driving judgement (Ch 4, p. 4-23). Follow-up: apply the substitution question and the error-versus-violation distinction.
- **Treating normalised risk as safe.** Signal: "it has always been fine". Diagnosis: the bald-tire attitude, of which Columbia is the handbook's example (Ch 2, p. 2-6). Follow-up: count the near misses the practice has had.
- **Asking for feedback and not acting.** Signal: earlier review findings or survey results went nowhere. Diagnosis: people stop cooperating when they see no change (Ch 3, pp. 3-19, 3-23). Follow-up: show the reporters what changed.

## Citation and Source-Integrity Notes

**Borrowed-through gaps.** The handbook draws heavily on authors the demo corpus does not hold: James Reason (GEMS, latent conditions, violation categories, the culpability decision tree, reporting-culture features), Jens Rasmussen (SRK), Sidney Dekker (the goals-focus-knowledge questions and the Fitts cockpit study), Neil Johnston (substitution test), Tom Gilbert (Behavior Engineering Model), Mager and Pipe (gap analysis), Weick and Sutcliffe (HRO mindfulness), Hollnagel and Woods (resilience engineering, drift), and INPO publications. Where this distillation uses their ideas it does so through the handbook's paraphrase, marked as such; none of them is quoted.

**Named limits of the source.** The handbook is written for DOE facilities (operators, technicians, engineers, laboratory staff) and its examples are mainly nuclear, industrial and aviation; the software scenario above is the projection's. It states that it "focuses on managing human error" rather than violations (Ch 2, p. 2-11). It keeps a root-cause vocabulary that LFUO 2024 rejects. Its error-rate and error-share figures are cited from industry studies, not produced by DOE. Its culpability decision tree is an adaptation of Reason's, and its box text survives in the converted source without the arrows, so the distillation names the questions and outcomes but not every branch.

**Evidence-marker continuity.** The four blockquotes and every inline `[V]` fragment are copied from the deep reference's verified quotations (all checked against the converted source by `scripts/check-verbatim.py`). Within the `[V]` blockquote from Ch 4, p. 4-26, the source's curly inner quotation marks are rendered as single quotes. The foresight and substitution test questions, the culpability-tree walkthrough and the reporting-culture features are paraphrased, not quoted, because the handbook adapts them from Reason and Johnston. Lens gate: the four demo lenses (builder, agentic-builder, pm-bounded-by-ba-role, chris-gagne-consultant-coach) were each judged clear no for this distillation; retrieval-time fallback covers a lens-specified query.
