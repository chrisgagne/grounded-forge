# U.S. Marine Corps, MCDP 1: Warfighting, After-Action Review Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Marine Corps (1997). *MCDP 1: Warfighting*. Headquarters United States Marine Corps, Department of the Navy. Foreword signed by General C. C. Krulak, Commandant, 20 June 1997; Preface by General A. M. Gray (Ret.). PCN 142 000006 00. Supersedes FMFM 1 *Warfighting* (1989). Licence: **US Government public-domain doctrinal publication** (17 U.S.C. § 105). **Scope:** open. The source never uses the term *after-action review*; its word for reviewing an event after the fact is *critique* (Ch 3, "Training").

## After-Action Review Relevance

MCDP 1 is the Marine Corps' statement of warfighting philosophy, not an AAR manual. The Foreword says so: "This book contains no specific techniques or procedures for conduct. Rather, it provides broad guidance in the form of concepts and values. It requires judgment in application." [V] (Source: U.S. Marine Corps, *MCDP 1: Warfighting*, Foreword). Its one passage on reviewing an event after the fact is the critique paragraph in Ch 3, and that paragraph is squarely on task:

> Critiques are an important part of training because critical self-analysis, even after success, is essential to improvement. Their purpose is to draw out the lessons of training. As a result, we should conduct critiques immediately after completing training, before memory of the events has faded. Critiques should be held in an atmosphere of open and frank dialogue in which all hands are encouraged to contribute. We learn as much from mistakes as from things done well, so we must be willing to admit mistakes and discuss them. Of course, a subordinate's willingness to admit mistakes depends on the commander's willingness to tolerate them. Because we recognize that no two situations in war are the same, our critiques should focus not so much on the actions we took as on why we took those actions and why they brought the results they did. [V] (Source: U.S. Marine Corps, *MCDP 1: Warfighting*, Ch 3, "Training")

The paragraph gives an AAR facilitator a timing rule (straight after the event, and after successes as well as failures), a climate rule (open, all hands, with the senior person's tolerance of mistakes setting how much gets admitted), and a focus rule (why each action was taken and why it had the result it did, over a list of what was done). Three other parts of the source carry the rest of the projection. Ch 3 "Professionalism" sets a stance on errors that treats inaction as an error in its own right: leniency for errors of overboldness, severity for errors of inaction or timidity, and no licence to act stupidly or recklessly. Ch 4 "Commander's intent" gives a standard for reviewing a departure from a plan: the task says what to do, the intent says why, and "the intent is predominant" [V] (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"). Ch 1's treatment of uncertainty, friction and complexity frames timeline and contributory-factor work: people act on incomplete information, an organisation makes some of its own friction, and results come from many parts acting on local conditions.

In AAR work the source fires in Phase 0 (timing and climate), Phase 1 (intent and the reasons behind each action), Phase 2 (many forces; self-made friction), Phase 3 (which kind of error is on the table), Phase 4 (actions that add control or equipment where method needs to change) and, lightly, Phase 5 (revised procedures that keep room for judgement). It complements TC 25-20, LFUO 2024 and the NHS guide and replaces none of them. Its critique is not the critique TC 25-20 sets against the AAR; see Integration.

## Key Concepts for After-Action Review

1.  **The critique: MCDP 1's review after the event.** Held "immediately after completing training, before memory of the events has faded" [V], in "open and frank dialogue in which all hands are encouraged to contribute" [V], after success as well as failure; its purpose is "to draw out the lessons of training" [V] (Source: Ch 3, "Training"). The source's setting is training. Carrying the critique over to incidents and operational events is this projection's move, not the source's.

2.  **Why over what.** Because "no two situations in war are the same" [V], the critique should "focus not so much on the actions we took as on why we took those actions and why they brought the results they did" [V] (Source: Ch 3, "Training"). For an AAR, the reasons behind each action, and the link from each action to its result, are the unit of the review.

3.  **The senior's tolerance sets how much is admitted.** "a subordinate's willingness to admit mistakes depends on the commander's willingness to tolerate them" [V] (Source: Ch 3, "Training"). In AAR terms, the stance of the most senior person present toward mistakes is part of the review's climate, and the facilitator has to bring it into the open before accounts are given.

4.  **No zero defects, within limits.** The source calls "errors by junior leaders stemming from overboldness" [V] "a necessary part of learning" [V], then sets out both the leniency and its limits (Source: Ch 3, "Professionalism"):

   > We should deal with such errors leniently; there must be no "zero defects" mentality. Abolishing "zero defects" means that we do not stifle boldness or initiative through the threat of punishment. It does not mean that commanders do not counsel subordinates on mistakes; constructive criticism is an important element in learning. Nor does it give subordinates free license to act stupidly or recklessly. [V] (Source: Ch 3, "Professionalism")

   Leniency here comes with counselling, and it stops short of stupid or reckless acts.

5.  **Inaction is an error too.** "we should deal severely with errors of inaction or timidity. We will not accept lack of orders as justification for inaction" [V] (Source: Ch 3, "Professionalism"). Read with "Risk is equally common to action and inaction." [V] (Ch 1, "Uncertainty"), this puts waiting and not acting inside the review's scope. The source states the pairing as institutional discipline for how leaders deal with errors; it is not a review procedure, and an AAR run blameless uses the distinction to decide what to examine, not what to punish (see Anti-patterns).

6.  **Candour before the decision, support after.** "Until a commander has reached and stated a decision, subordinates should consider it their duty to provide honest, professional opinions even though these may be in disagreement with the senior's opinions." [V] Once the decision is made, juniors support it as their own, and ready compliance for personal advancement will not be tolerated (Source: Ch 3, "Professionalism"). On an AAR timeline this is a question about the key decisions: was a differing view voiced before the call, and was it heard?

7.  **Task and intent.** "The task describes the action to be taken while the intent describes the purpose of the action." [V] Intent is provided so that subordinates can exercise judgement and initiative, "to depart from the original plan when the unforeseen occurs" [V], in a way consistent with their seniors' aims, and "the intent is predominant" [V] (Source: Ch 4, "Commander's intent"). For an AAR, a departure from the plan or procedure is reviewed against the intent as well as the task.

8.  **The burden of understanding intent is shared.** "The burden of understanding falls on senior and subordinate alike." [V] Seniors must make their purposes clear without inhibiting initiative; subordinates "should understand the intent of the commander at least two levels up" [V]; and a subordinate not given a clear purpose "should ask for one" [V] (Source: Ch 4, "Commander's intent"). When an AAR finds an intent gap, it has two halves to examine: how the purpose was stated, and whether it was sought.

9.  **Action on incomplete information.** "all actions in war will be based on incomplete, inaccurate, or even contradictory information" [V] (Source: Ch 1, "Uncertainty"). Uncertainty cannot be removed; the source's means for fighting despite it are "developing simple, flexible plans; planning for likely contingencies; developing standing operating procedures; and fostering initiative among subordinates" [V] (Ch 1, "Uncertainty"). For an AAR, the timeline reconstructs what each person knew at the time, including what was missing, wrong or contradictory in it.

10.  **Self-induced friction.** "Friction is the force that resists all action and saps energy." [V] Some of it is self-induced, from factors such as "lack of a clearly defined goal, lack of coordination, unclear or complicated plans, complex task organizations or command relationships, or complicated technologies" [V]; the stance is to minimise it, while "the greater requirement is to fight effectively despite the existence of friction" [V] (Source: Ch 1, "Friction"). For contributory-factor work, the source's examples are prompts for friction the organisation made for itself.

11.  **Many forces; small causes, large effects.** "Each episode is the unique product of myriad moral, mental, and physical forces." [V] (Source: Ch 1, "Conclusion"). Nonlinearity: "Minor incidents or actions can have decisive effects." [V] (Ch 1, "Uncertainty"). Complexity: war "emerges from the collective behavior of all the individual parts in the system interacting locally in response to local conditions and incomplete information" [V] (Ch 1, "Complexity"). [AR] Read together for an AAR: expect several contributors, and do not assume a large effect needs a large cause.

12.  **More control and more equipment are not the whole fix.** "Better equipment is not the cure for all ills; doctrinal and tactical solutions to combat deficiencies must also be sought" [V], and "Advanced information technology especially can tempt us to try to maintain precise, positive control over subordinates, which is incompatible with the Marine Corps philosophy of command." [V] (Source: Ch 3, "Equipping"). "We cannot rightly expect our subordinates to exercise boldness and initiative in the field when they are accustomed to being oversupervised in garrison." [V] (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command"). For action design: test whether a proposed action adds control that trains people out of the initiative the event called for.

## Questions to Ask During After-Action Review

### Phase 0: Scoping

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is the review timed while memory is fresh? | Can the review be held straight after the event, "before memory of the events has faded" [V], even if it has to be shorter than the ideal? | Ch 3, "Training" |
| Is a success being skipped? | The event went well: is a review planned anyway, given that "critical self-analysis, even after success, is essential to improvement" [V]? | Ch 3, "Training" |
| Has the senior person's stance on mistakes been stated? | Before the first account, has the most senior person present said how admitted mistakes will be treated? Candour depends on it (Concept 3). | Ch 3, "Training" |
| Can all hands contribute? | Do the attendance list and the format let everyone who took part speak, not only the leads? | Ch 3, "Training" |

### Phase 1: Timeline and Local-Rationality Reconstruction

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What was the intent, and who knew it? | What purpose (the *in order to* clause behind the task) did the people acting believe they were serving? How far up the chain did they know it, and where did they get it? | Ch 4, "Commander's intent" |
| Why did each action make sense? | For each key action: why was it taken, and why did it bring the result it did? | Ch 3, "Training" |
| What did people know at the time? | What was missing, inaccurate or contradictory in the information each person had when they acted? | Ch 1, "Uncertainty" |
| Whose account sits nearest the point of decision? | Where accounts diverge, which come from the people at the point of decision, and which from people further away? The source holds that a competent subordinate at the point of decision "will naturally better appreciate the true situation than a senior commander some distance removed" [V]. | Ch 4, "Philosophy of command" |
| Was dissent voiced before the call? | Before the key decision, did anyone hold a differing professional opinion? Was it voiced, and was it heard? | Ch 3, "Professionalism" |

### Phase 2: Contributory-Factor Analysis

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is the analysis settling on one cause? | What other moral, mental and physical forces shaped this episode? | Ch 1, "Conclusion" |
| Did something small have a large effect? | Which minor incident or action had an outsized effect, and what let it spread? | Ch 1, "Uncertainty" |
| Which friction did we make ourselves? | Among the contributors, which were self-induced: an unclear goal, poor coordination, unclear or complicated plans, tangled task organisation or reporting lines, complicated technology? | Ch 1, "Friction" |
| Did the task outlive its purpose? | When the situation changed, did the task become obsolete while the intent still held? Did people follow the task or the intent? | Ch 4, "Commander's intent" |
| Was the intent gap two-sided? | If people did not know the purpose: was it stated clearly, and did anyone ask for it? | Ch 4, "Commander's intent" |

### Phase 3: Just-Culture Sorting

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Which kind of error is on the table? | Is this an error of overboldness (acting on initiative and getting it wrong), an error of inaction or timidity (waiting, or pointing to missing orders), or stupid or reckless action? The source treats the three differently. | Ch 3, "Professionalism" |
| Is inaction getting the same scrutiny as action? | Are the people who waited, or who held back for lack of orders, being examined as closely as the person who acted? | Ch 3, "Professionalism"; Ch 1, "Uncertainty" |
| Is the leniency being stretched? | Does the act fall outside what the source's leniency covers, that is, stupid or reckless action? If so, route the individual-action question to the NHS five-test tree and LFUO's reckless-and-willful threshold. | Ch 3, "Professionalism" |
| Is learning still happening where leniency applies? | Where the error was overbold, is there constructive criticism aimed at learning, without punishment? | Ch 3, "Professionalism" |

### Phase 4: Action Design

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Does the action add control that slows initiative? | Does a proposed action add sign-offs or monitoring that would leave people oversupervised in routine work, then expected to act boldly in the next incident? | Ch 4, "Philosophy of command"; Ch 3, "Equipping" |
| Is the action equipment-only? | Is a tool or technology change being offered as the whole fix? What change in method and practice goes with it? | Ch 3, "Equipping" |
| Does each revised step carry its purpose? | Does each revised plan or procedure step say why as well as what, so the next person can depart from it well when the unforeseen occurs? | Ch 4, "Commander's intent" |
| Is the rehearsal opposed or canned? | If the action includes an exercise, does it pit people against an independent, opposing will, or is it a scripted scenario? The source holds that "only in opposed, free-play exercises can we practice the art of war" [V]. | Ch 3, "Training" |

### Phase 5: Learning-Loop Closure

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Have the lessons been drawn out? | Can the group state the lessons, the stated purpose of a critique, before it disperses? | Ch 3, "Training" |
| Does a revised procedure leave room for judgement? | Is the revised SOP one means among several (the source also lists simple, flexible plans, contingency planning and fostering initiative), or is it being asked to cover every case on its own? | Ch 1, "Uncertainty" |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Juniors stay quiet while seniors are in the room; few mistakes are admitted | The climate the source ties to the senior's tolerance of mistakes has not been set (Ch 3, "Training") | Ask the senior person to say, before accounts begin, how admitted mistakes will be treated; invite all hands by name if needed; where the review is an LFUO FLA, confirm the no-punitive-action assurance in the delegation of authority |
| The person who acted is under the microscope; those who waited are not on the timeline | Inaction has been left out of the review, the reverse of the source's stance that errors of inaction or timidity are errors too (Ch 3, "Professionalism") | Put the waits and holds on the timeline; ask what the people who waited knew, what they were waiting for, and why waiting made sense |
| The finding is "they didn't follow the procedure" | The departure has been judged against the task only; the source holds that the task can become obsolete while the intent still guides (Ch 4, "Commander's intent") | Restate the intent; ask whether the departure served it and what the person knew when they departed |
| The finding is "they should have known the intent" | One-sided burden; the source places the burden of understanding "on senior and subordinate alike" [V] (Ch 4, "Commander's intent") | Ask both halves: was the purpose stated clearly and where people would see it; did anyone ask for it? |
| The analysis converges on one cause, or on "human error" | Single-cause framing against the source's "myriad moral, mental, and physical forces" [V] (Ch 1, "Conclusion") | List the other forces; run the self-induced-friction prompts (goal, coordination, plans, task organisation, technology) |
| The action list is mostly new approvals, dashboards or tools | The control-and-equipment reflex the source warns against (Ch 3, "Equipping"; Ch 4, "Philosophy of command") | Pair each tool with a change in method; test each approval step for whether it oversupervises the people whose initiative the event needed |
| The team wants to skip the review because the outcome was good | Success-skip; the source holds self-analysis essential "even after success" [V] (Ch 3, "Training") | Hold a short review now; ask what went well and why, not only what went wrong |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- The review turns on a departure from a plan, runbook or procedure, and the question is whether the departure served the purpose behind it.
- Scrutiny is falling on whoever acted, while waits, holds and deferrals go unexamined.
- The senior people in the room shape how candid the review can be, and the facilitator needs a grounded way to raise it.
- The team wants to skip reviewing a success, or to postpone a review until memory has faded.
- Action design is drifting toward more approvals, more monitoring or more tooling as the whole answer.
- The organisation runs on stated intent and devolved decisions, so the review needs a standard of judgement rather than of compliance alone.

Do not use this source as the AAR procedure (TC 25-20 and LFUO 2024 carry that) or as the individual-accountability decision aid (the NHS five-test tree carries that). MCDP 1's severity toward inaction is a leadership stance in a military organisation, not an accountability process for a blameless review.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored; nothing in MCDP 1 concerns software, and every outcome below belongs to the scenario. A payments company reviews a 70-minute checkout outage. Two actions dominate the timeline. At minute 12 the on-call engineer rolled back the previous day's deploy without waiting for the incident commander's go-ahead, which the runbook step "roll back only on incident-commander approval" requires. Card payments came back within minutes, but the rollback also reversed a schema migration, and a batch of refunds stuck for two days. Wallet payments stayed down. The on-call engineer messaged the platform team for a configuration fix; they had it ready at minute 20 and held it for 40 minutes waiting for a change-approval sign-off. None of them had seen the incident commander's opening message, "restore checkout in order to stop losing orders", which went to one channel only.

**Phase 0.** The facilitator holds the review the next morning rather than at the month-end slot, following the source's rule that critiques come "immediately after completing training, before memory of the events has faded" [V] (Source: U.S. Marine Corps, *MCDP 1: Warfighting*, Ch 3, "Training"). Before the first account she asks the engineering director to say how admitted mistakes will be treated, because "a subordinate's willingness to admit mistakes depends on the commander's willingness to tolerate them" [V] (Ch 3, "Training"). The director restates the company's no-blame commitment for reviews.

**Phase 1.** The facilitator restates the incident commander's intent and asks each party what purpose they were working to. The on-call engineer knew it; the platform team did not. She frames the two actions with the source's split between task and intent:

> Of the two, the intent is predominant. While a situation may change, making the task obsolete, the intent is more lasting and continues to guide our actions. [V] (Source: Ch 4, "Commander's intent")

The rollback departed from the runbook's task and served the stated intent. The hold followed the change-approval task while the intent went unserved for 40 minutes. The questions stay on why, in line with the source's focus on "why we took those actions and why they brought the results they did" [V] (Ch 3, "Training"): the rollback made sense at minute 12 because the engineer could see card payments failing and did not know the migration rode on the same deploy; the hold made sense to the platform team because they never ship without sign-off and did not know an incident with a stated purpose had been declared.

**Phase 2.** The group lists contributors as several forces rather than one cause (Ch 1, "Conclusion"). The self-induced friction prompts (Ch 1, "Friction") surface three: a runbook step that gave a task with no purpose (unclear plans), an intent posted to one channel (lack of coordination), and a migration coupled to an unrelated deploy (complicated technology). On the intent gap the facilitator holds both halves:

> A clear expression and understanding of intent is essential to unity of effort. The burden of understanding falls on senior and subordinate alike. The seniors must make their purposes perfectly clear but in a way that does not inhibit initiative. Subordinates must have a clear understanding of what their commander expects. [V] (Source: Ch 4, "Commander's intent")

So the review asks how the intent was posted, and also why nobody on the platform team asked what the incident was for when the request arrived; the source says a subordinate not given a clear purpose "should ask for one" [V] (Ch 4, "Commander's intent").

**Phase 3.** The group starts to treat the rollback as the error of the day. The facilitator names the source's two kinds of error and the limit it puts on leniency (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). The rollback reads as an error of overboldness, which the source says to deal with leniently, with counselling and constructive criticism rather than punishment. The 40-minute hold reads as an error of inaction, and there the source's line is that "we should deal severely with errors of inaction or timidity" [V]. This review runs blameless, so the team reads that severity as scrutiny, not sanction: the hold gets the same timeline detail as the rollback. Neither act comes near stupid or reckless, so no individual-action question goes to the NHS tree.

**Phase 4.** Someone proposes that every rollback need two approvers. The facilitator tests it against the source's warning that people cannot be expected to show "boldness and initiative in the field when they are accustomed to being oversupervised in garrison" [V] (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command"). The group drops the proposal. In this scenario the review ends with three owned actions and no disciplinary referral: runbook steps rewritten as task plus purpose; the incident commander's intent posted to every responder channel with the page; the migration decoupled from routine deploys.

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **The zero-defects review.** Signal: every admitted mistake draws a consequence, and admissions dry up. The source rejects the zero-defects mentality so as not to stifle "boldness or initiative through the threat of punishment" [V] (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). Follow-up: separate learning from sanction, and have the senior person say so before accounts begin.
- **Reviewing only whoever acted.** Signal: the timeline lists actions but not waits, holds or deferrals. The source treats inaction and timidity as errors in their own right (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). Follow-up: put the holds on the timeline and ask why waiting made sense.
- **Importing the source's severity into a blameless review.** Signal: someone cites MCDP 1 to justify sanctions for the people who waited. The source states its severity as the way leaders deal with errors in a military organisation; it is not an AAR procedure, and where the review runs under LFUO's no-punitive-action firewall, sanctions are out of scope. Follow-up: use the distinction to widen what the review examines.
- **Treating leniency as licence.** Signal: an "overbold" label is used to excuse a reckless act. The leniency does not give "free license to act stupidly or recklessly" [V] (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). Follow-up: route that question to the NHS five-test tree and LFUO's reckless-and-willful threshold.
- **The task-compliance verdict.** Signal: "they didn't follow the procedure" closes the discussion. Follow-up: restate the intent and review the departure against it (Ch 4, "Commander's intent").
- **The one-sided intent burden.** Signal: either "they should have known" or "nobody told us", said alone. Follow-up: ask how the purpose was stated and whether anyone sought it; the burden "falls on senior and subordinate alike" [V] (Ch 4, "Commander's intent").
- **The single-cause conclusion.** Signal: the review names one root cause. Follow-up: list the other forces, and check for a minor incident with a decisive effect (Ch 1, "Conclusion"; Ch 1, "Uncertainty").
- **The late or skipped review.** Signal: a success goes unreviewed, or the review waits for a monthly slot. Follow-up: hold it straight after the event, successes included (Ch 3, "Training").
- **Control-and-equipment actions.** Signal: the action list is mostly approvals, monitoring and tools. Follow-up: pair each with a change in method, and test each for oversupervision (Ch 3, "Equipping"; Ch 4, "Philosophy of command").
- **Citing MCDP 1 as AAR doctrine.** Signal: MCDP 1 is named as the doctrine behind AAR practice, or behind TC 25-20. The source never mentions the AAR, and TC 25-20 is an Army training circular that describes itself as supplementing the Army's FM 25-101. Follow-up: cite MCDP 1 for its critique paragraph and its command philosophy, and TC 25-20 for the procedure.

## Citation and Source-Integrity Notes

**Borrowed-through gaps.** MCDP 1 quotes and cites authors this corpus does not hold: Clausewitz's *On War* (the most-cited work, in 15 notes, and the source of the friction concept), Sun Tzu's *Art of War*, John Boyd (the OODA loop at Notes 18 of Ch 2; implicit communication at Notes 5 of Ch 4), William S. Lind (reconnaissance pull at Notes 11 of Ch 4), David Gelernter (topsight at Notes 9 of Ch 4), Patton, Napoleon and the Joint Pub 1-02 definitions. This distillation quotes none of them. The review practice it projects, the critique, is the source's own and names no lineage.

**Named limits of the source.** MCDP 1 is doctrine for warfighting, not an AAR text, and says it "contains no specific techniques or procedures" [V] (Foreword). Its review content is one paragraph, set in training rather than operations; carrying it to incidents is this projection's move. Its vocabulary is military (commander, subordinate, Marine), and the civilian translation here is operator-applied. The leniency-and-severity passage describes how leaders deal with errors by junior leaders; it is not a just-culture framework, and it offers no decision tree or threshold definitions. Ch 1's forces, friction and nonlinearity are a frame for contributory-factor work, not an analysis method. The source's material on tempo, the OODA loop, maneuver, main effort and centers of gravity is not projected: it concerns acting against an opponent, and the source claims a growing advantage for the side that completes the decision cycle faster, not an outcome for any given incident, so using it as an AAR verdict would claim more than the source says.

**Evidence-marker continuity.** The four blockquotes are `[V]` and are copied from the deep reference's Pass-D passages: the critique paragraph (Ch 3, "Training"; source lines 2554-2567), the zero-defects passage (Ch 3, "Professionalism"; source lines 2395-2401), the intent-predominant passage (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"; source lines 3713-3715) and the burden-of-understanding passage (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"; source lines 3764-3769). Inline `[V]` fragments come from the same deep-ref passages and match the converted source word for word, with curly quotation marks straightened and line-end hyphenation removed. The zero-defects blockquote keeps the source's double quotation marks around "zero defects" (the deep reference nests them as single quotes); it starts after a garbled footnote mark that follows "learning." at source line 2395, and the intent blockquote starts after the garbled "when and where'," at source line 3712, so no quotation needed an OCR restoration. No `[BT]` words (epigraphs, Clausewitz, Joint Pub definitions) are quoted. Concept 11's joint reading of three Ch 1 passages is marked `[AR]`. The distillation paraphrases, rather than quotes, the source's "yes-men" sentence, and quotes only the "to depart from the original plan" clause of the purpose-of-intent sentence, to keep the verbatim register to the passages the projection turns on.
