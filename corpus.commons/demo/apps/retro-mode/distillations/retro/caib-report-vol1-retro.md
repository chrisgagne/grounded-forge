## Retrospective Relevance

A retrospective is not an accident investigation, and an incident in progress belongs on the AAR axis. But the Columbia report is a detailed account of what a regular review cadence can miss, iteration after iteration. Over 22 years, Flight Readiness Reviews ratified repeated foam shedding as safe, with past success standing in for engineering analysis, until the accepted anomaly destroyed an Orbiter (Source: CAIB, *Report Vol. I*, Ch 6, pp. 121–130; Ch 7, p. 181; Ch 8, pp. 196–197). The report gives a retro facilitator a way to look at **the anomaly the team has stopped seeing**: the first acceptance that set the precedent, the label that drifted ("in family"; in a team, "known issue"), and the signals that were mixed because the next iteration went fine (Ch 8, p. 196). It also explains why a team **agrees too quickly**, why **quiet or junior voices don't speak**, and why a **manager in the room** changes what gets said: status decided whose information counted, and dissent needed a route that leaders had to build (Ch 6, p. 169; Ch 7, p. 192; Ch 8, pp. 201–203). And it warns that improvements fade: post-Challenger reforms were undone over time (Ch 8, p. 198; Ch 9, p. 208).

It fires mainly in Phase 3 (the recurring pattern, and why), Phase 2 (surfacing what people know but have not said), Phase 0.5 (experiments that quietly lapsed) and Phase 4 (experiments that need structural backing). It does not help with retro formats, energy or facilitation mechanics.

## Key Concepts for Retrospective

1.  **The accepted anomaly.** A deviation tolerated once with a rationale becomes the precedent for the next: "For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations." [V] (Ch 8, p. 196). The Board borrows the name, normalisation of deviance, from Diane Vaughan [BT]. In a retro, the question is which "known issue" has become furniture.

2.  **Mixed signals from iterations that go fine.** Incidents examined one at a time, each followed by flights where nothing went wrong, sent mixed signals, and the recurring problem became a routine signal rather than a warning (Ch 8, pp. 196–197) [AR]. "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct." [V] (Ch 8, p. 197).

3.  **Labels that drift.** Foam went from a safety-of-flight issue to "in family" to a turnaround and maintenance item; the Board calls "in-family" "a strange term indeed for a violation of system requirements" [V] (Ch 8, p. 196). Labels such as "known issue" or "tech debt" can do the same work in a team.

4.  **Seeking dissent when the room agrees.** "Organizations with strong safety cultures generally acknowledge that a leader's best response to unanimous consent is to play devil's advocate and encourage an exhaustive debate." [V] (Ch 7, p. 192).

5.  **Status decides whose information counts.** "Structure and hierarchy represent power and status." [V] Positions in the organisation determined the weight given to information, "by their own judgment and in the eyes of others" [V] (Ch 8, p. 201). People who are marginal may have useful information they don't express, and even when encouraged they find it intimidating to contradict a leader or a group consensus (Ch 8, p. 203) [AP].

6.  **Leaders must build routes for concerns.** Engineers had a duty to raise concerns, but leaders had a corresponding and perhaps greater obligation to create viable routes for them (Ch 6, p. 169) [AP]. Mechanisms such as time-outs and reporting channels only work if they reduce fear of retribution and management pays attention (Ch 7, p. 192) [AP].

7.  **Proving a concern before it counts.** Engineers were made to prove a situation unsafe rather than managers proving it safe (Ch 6, p. 169) [AP]. In a retro, a delta dismissed until someone can prove its impact is the same pattern at small scale.

8.  **Improvements fade.** Post-Challenger changes "were undone over time by management actions" [V] (Ch 8, p. 198); "This decline in vigilance is a characteristic of many large organizations, and it has been demonstrated in NASA's own history." [V] (Ch 9, p. 208).

9.  **Beyond the team's control, not beyond its view.** The Board traces the decisions on the floor to budget, schedule and structure set elsewhere (Ch 5; Ch 8, p. 195) [AR]. "Leaders create culture. It is their responsibility to change it." [V] (Ch 8, p. 203). A retro can name a structural cause and route it upward even when the team cannot fix it.

## Questions to Ask During Retrospective

### Phase 0: Setup

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Who is in the room, and does it change what gets said? | With a manager present, whose information will be weighed less, by others and by themselves? | Ch 8, p. 201 |
| Is there a route for a concern? | What will make it safe for a quiet or junior person to raise something the room disagrees with? | Ch 6, p. 169; Ch 7, p. 192 |

### Phase 0.5: Experiment Review

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Did last quarter's improvement survive? | Is the change we made after the last bad sprint still in place, or has it quietly lapsed? | Ch 8, p. 198; Ch 9, p. 208 |

### Phase 2: Data Gathering

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What has become normal? | Which problem has recurred so often that we no longer raise it? When did we first accept it, and why? | Ch 8, p. 196 |
| What did someone ask for and not get? | Did anyone request time, information or help this iteration that never came? | Ch 6, pp. 140–153 |
| What wasn't said? | Is there something people noticed but did not raise, and what stopped them? | Ch 8, p. 203 |

### Phase 3: Insight / Cause Analysis

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is this a drifting label? | What do we call this problem now, and what did we call it the first time? | Ch 6, p. 122; Ch 8, p. 196 |
| Did the good iterations hide it? | Have the iterations where nothing went wrong been read as evidence that the problem is safe? | Ch 8, pp. 196–197 |
| Who had to prove what? | Was a concern dismissed because no one could prove its impact? | Ch 6, p. 169 |
| Is the cause outside the team? | Which part of this comes from schedule, budget or structure set elsewhere, and who owns it? | Ch 8, pp. 195, 203 |

### Phase 4: Experiment Design

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Will the experiment surface the weak signal? | Does the experiment make the recurring problem visible as a trend, rather than closing it once more? | Ch 8, p. 203 |
| Does it need someone else's authority? | Can the team run this, or does it need a structural change that someone outside must back? | Ch 7, p. 193; Ch 8, p. 203 |

### Phase 5: Close

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Did everyone get to disagree? | Before closing, has someone argued the other side of what we agreed? | Ch 7, p. 192 |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| A "known issue" that appears every retro and never gets an experiment | Normalisation of deviance: the first acceptance set the precedent (Ch 8, p. 196) | Trace when it was first accepted and why |
| "It hasn't hurt us yet" | Mixed signals from iterations that went fine (Ch 8, pp. 196–197) | Ask what would happen the iteration it does |
| Instant agreement | No one arguing the other side (Ch 7, p. 192) | Ask someone to make the case against |
| The same few people speak; juniors are quiet | Status deciding whose information counts (Ch 8, pp. 201, 203) | Gather input in writing first; ask quieter people directly |
| A delta dismissed as "prove it" | Inverted burden of proof (Ch 6, p. 169) | Ask what evidence says it is fine |
| A fix from two quarters ago has quietly lapsed | Vigilance fading; changes undone (Ch 8, p. 198) | Review past improvements, not just last sprint's |

## When to Use This Reference

- **Reach for it** when the same pattern reappears across retros, when a team agrees too quickly, or when a manager's presence or a status gap is shaping who speaks.
- **Reach for it** to make the case that a "known issue" deserves attention even though nothing has broken yet.
- **Route to the AAR axis** when the team is mid-incident; the report is an incident investigation at heart, and its AAR distillation carries the method.
- **Reach for something else** for retro formats and facilitation mechanics (Field Guide, Liberating Structures, FLO).

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored; the report's own case is the loss of Columbia. A product team's retro board shows, for the fifth sprint running, a sticky reading "flaky integration tests". Each time it is grouped under "known issues" and no experiment is chosen. This sprint a release slipped when a real failure was dismissed as flakiness.

**Phase 2.** The facilitator asks when the flakiness was first accepted and why. The answer is that a deadline was near and someone said "re-run it" (Source: CAIB, *Report Vol. I*, Ch 8, p. 196).

> For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations. [V] (Source: Ch 8, p. 196)

**Phase 3.** The team sees that each green re-run had been read as evidence that the tests were fine, when it said nothing about the failures they were hiding (Ch 8, pp. 196–197). A junior tester says, once asked directly, that she had seen two real failures dismissed as flakiness and had not raised them. A senior engineer had said they were noise (Ch 8, pp. 201, 203).

> Organizations with strong safety cultures generally acknowledge that a leader's best response to unanimous consent is to play devil's advocate and encourage an exhaustive debate. [V] (Source: Ch 7, p. 192)

**Phase 4.** The experiment is to log every re-run with its failure, so the trend becomes visible, and to let anyone block a release on a failure that is not understood (Ch 8, p. 203). The engineering manager takes the test-infrastructure budget question upward.

**Phase 0.5, next retro.** The team checks that the log is still in use (Ch 9, p. 208).

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **The furniture problem.** Signal: a known issue on every board. Diagnosis: normalisation of deviance (Ch 8, p. 196). Follow-up: give it an experiment or a deliberate decision to live with it, with the risk named.
- **Quiet consensus.** Signal: fast agreement. Diagnosis: no devil's advocate (Ch 7, p. 192). Follow-up: ask for the case against.
- **Prove-it dismissals.** Signal: a delta set aside until someone can show its impact. Diagnosis: inverted burden of proof (Ch 6, p. 169). Follow-up: ask what shows it is safe to ignore.
- **Forgotten fixes.** Signal: past improvements never revisited. Diagnosis: improvements fade (Ch 8, p. 198). Follow-up: review older experiments periodically.
