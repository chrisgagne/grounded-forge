# Stakeholder-Engagement Distillation Index

> **Purpose:** Situation-to-resource router for stakeholder-engagement work. "In phase X of an engagement, which references should I reach for?"

This is the *when-to-use* layer for the stakeholder-engagement task. It partitions the task into phases and lists distillations appropriate to each phase. For the *what-is* layer (descriptions of each reference), read [`reference-index.json`](../../reference-index.json) at the corpus root.

**Workflow:** Distillation index (when) → distillation (how) → deep reference (citations, if needed).

---

## Format

Two table shapes appear in this index.

**Phase-by-phase tables** (the main routing layer) use four columns. The "Need" column describes the situation; "Reference" names the source; "Distillation" links to the projected distillation; "Reach for this when" gives a one-line routing cue.

```markdown
| Need | Reference | Distillation | Reach for this when |
|---|---|---|---|
| ... | ... | ... | ... |
```

**Quick-start tables** (the situation-first lookup) use three columns: a situation description, the primary references that apply, and the distillation file paths.

```markdown
| Engagement type | Primary references | Distillations |
|---|---|---|
| ... | ... | ... |
```

### Just-culture and patient-safety stakeholder engagement

### Doctrinal frame for multi-stakeholder engagement

- `mcdp1-warfighting-stakeholder-engagement.md`, U.S. Marine Corps (*MCDP 1: Warfighting*, 1997, supersedes FMFM 1, 1989). **Public domain (US Government work); scope=open.** A combat doctrine with no stakeholder vocabulary and no theory of standing, projected narrowly: it lands on engagement inside a line of authority (leaders, the people they delegate to, peers) and on engagement with a party whose aims oppose yours; it speaks to cooperative external stakeholders only by analogy. Intent over task in an *in order to* form, subordinates who should understand the intent at least two levels up and ask for a purpose when none is given (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"); the mission as a two-way senior-subordinate contract, method prescribed only as far as coordination needs (Ch 4, "Mission tactics"); unity through lateral coordination rather than imposed control (Ch 4); honest, professional dissent until a decision is stated, support as if it were one's own after it, no yes-men (Ch 3, "Professionalism"); trust earned both ways; implicit communication through long working relationships, key people talking directly, orally and in person (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command"); orienting on an opposing party on its own terms without assuming shared values or objectives (Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy"); the critique as open dialogue about why, held soon after (Ch 3, "Training"); leniency for overbold errors, severity for inaction (Ch 3). Main effort ranks your own workstreams, not stakeholders (Ch 4). Caution: the adversarial tools (tempo, surfaces and gaps, centers of gravity, surprise) are framed against an enemy and do not transfer to cooperative engagement. Pair with OpenStax *Business Ethics* for who has standing, and with TC 25-20 for the review protocol (the two documents use *critique* in opposite senses).

## Anti-patterns this index helps avoid

- Treating standing as a function of seniority rather than of who is affected by the decision.
- Convening one large meeting to do constraint-discovery, synthesis, and ratification in sequence.
- Letting late-stage stakeholders reopen a closed decision without asking whether they had standing originally.
- Running a synthesis workshop when the team already knows the answer and only needs ratification.
