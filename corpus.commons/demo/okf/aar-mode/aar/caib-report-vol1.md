## After-Action Review Relevance

The Columbia report is the most complete published model in the demo corpus of a review that refuses to stop at the broken part and the nearest person. Its Board found a physical cause (foam striking the left wing's leading edge) and organisational causes of equal weight, and it explains why investigations so often miss the second: "Many accident investigations make the same mistake in defining causes. They identify the widget that broke or malfunctioned, then locate the person most closely connected with the technical failure" [V] (Source: CAIB, *Report Vol. I*, Ch 7, p. 177). For an AAR facilitator it supplies four things the corpus otherwise holds only in pieces. First, a **mechanism for drift**: how a deviation, accepted once with an engineering rationale, becomes the precedent that makes the next, larger one look normal (Ch 6, pp. 121–130; Ch 8, p. 196). Second, a **catalogue of how dissent got lost**: the inverted burden of proof, a "mandatory need" test, routing through the wrong channel, status deciding whose data counted, briefings condensed until the uncertainty dropped out, and safety staff who were present but silent (Ch 6, pp. 140–172; Ch 7, pp. 190–192; Ch 8, pp. 200–202). Third, a way to **tie the mission-week decisions to years of budget, schedule and structure** without excusing the people involved (Ch 5; Ch 8, p. 195). Fourth, the **Board's own method**: a safety investigation rather than a blame investigation, privileged witness statements, independence from the organisation under review, and a separate track for personal accountability (App A, pp. 231–235).

It fires hardest in Phase 2 (contributory factors, especially organisational and historical ones), Phase 1 (reconstructing how the decisions looked at the time, and where signals were missed), Phase 4 (structural actions, not retraining), and Phase 5 (the report's warning that vigilance fades and reforms get undone). It is less useful for the mechanics of a short team AAR; its scale is a national investigation, and its lessons must be scaled down.

## Key Concepts for After-Action Review

1.  **Organisational causes as equal to the physical cause.** The Board held that NASA's culture and structure "had as much to do with this accident as the External Tank foam" [V] (Ch 7, p. 177), and its formal cause statement names history and culture (compromises, resource constraints, schedule pressure, treating a developmental vehicle as operational) and the practices they bred: reliance on past success instead of sound engineering, barriers that blocked safety information and stifled dissent, unintegrated management, and an informal chain of command outside the rules [AP] (Ch 7, p. 177). In an AAR, a findings list with only technical items and individual actions is, on the Board's account, incomplete.

2.  **Normalisation of deviance.** The Board's mechanism, using a term it borrows from Diane Vaughan [BT]: a deviation from design is first tolerated with an engineering rationale; "For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations." [V]; later incidents read as the design behaving as predicted, and the experience base "functioned as an elastic waistband, expanding to hold larger deviations from the original design" [V] (Ch 8, p. 196). Categories drift with it: foam went from a safety-of-flight issue to "in family" to a turnaround and maintenance item, and an In-Flight Anomaly was downgraded to an "action" (Ch 6, pp. 122–126; Ch 8, pp. 196–197) [AE]. The AAR question is when the event's precursor was first accepted, and what rationale accepted it.

3.  **Weak, mixed and missing signals.** Anomalies examined one at a time, followed by flights where nothing went wrong, sent mixed signals; strong signals (wiring faults, cracked parts) stopped launches while foam was defined as a nagging issue (Ch 8, pp. 196–197) [AR]. After analysis, STS-112's large foam strike was itself "redefined as a weak signal" [V] (Ch 8, p. 197). The Board names the missing signals: no trend analysis, imagery not obtained, concerns not voiced, information dropped from briefings (Ch 8, p. 203) [AP]. An AAR timeline should carry the signals that existed but did not register, and say why each was discounted.

4.  **The inverted burden of proof.** "The engineers found themselves in the unusual position of having to prove that the situation was unsafe — a reversal of the usual requirement to prove that a situation is safe." [V] (Ch 6, p. 169). They were asked to show a "mandatory need" for imagery without being told how, though only the imagery could have settled the question (Ch 6, p. 157; Ch 8, p. 201) [AE]. In reconstruction, ask who carried the burden of proof at each decision point, and whether it was the one the safety case required.

5.  **Informal chains of command and status.** Decisions ran through an informal chain in which status decided whose information counted: "employees' positions in the organization determined the weight given to their information, by their own judgment and in the eyes of others" [V] (Ch 8, p. 201). A trusted tile expert's early opinion was taken as gospel though he lacked RCC expertise, and "Mission management welcomed this opinion and sought no others." [V] (Ch 6, p. 169; Ch 8, p. 202). Low-status sources (Kennedy, Langley, a team routed through engineering) were heard less (Ch 6, p. 152; Ch 8, pp. 201–202) [AE].

6.  **Information loss as it rises.** "Generally, the higher information is transmitted in a hierarchy, the more it gets "rolled-up," abbreviated, and simplified." [V] (Ch 7, p. 190). A 40-minute briefing became a three-minute item; the analysis's assumptions and uncertainty "were never fully presented" to the decision-making teams; the key fact sat at the bottom of a slide (Ch 6, p. 145; Ch 7, pp. 191–192) [AE]. In an AAR, compare what the analysts knew with what the decision-makers heard.

7.  **Schedule pressure as a contributory factor.** The Board found that a fixed Node 2 launch date pressed on decisions, that "The weight of evidence supports the workforce view." [V] over management's denial of undue pressure (Ch 6, p. 131), and that much of the concern about Columbia's foam strike was about its threat to the schedule (Ch 6, p. 139) [AP]. Its mechanism: small additions of risk that accumulate until no one sees the total (Ch 6, p. 139) [AP]. An AAR should ask what the people involved believed the schedule required, not only what the schedule said.

8.  **A silent safety function.** Safety representatives attended every meeting and "simply listened and concurred" [V] (Ch 8, p. 202); the Board concluded "That silence was not merely a failure of safety, but a failure of the entire organization." [V] (Ch 7, p. 192). Their silence followed from structure: safety staff funded by and dependent on the programme they oversaw (Ch 7, pp. 186–187) [AR]. The AAR question is not only "did safety speak?" but "could it have?".

9.  **Benchmarks for the action plan.** The Board measures NASA against organisations that manage high risk well: the Navy's submarine and reactor programmes and the Aerospace Corporation. They insist on airing minority opinions, use written staff work rather than slides, train on their own past accidents, and separate technical authority from cost and schedule [AE] (Ch 7, pp. 182–184). "Leaders continually emphasize that when no minority opinions are present, the responsibility for a thorough and critical examination falls to management." [V] (Ch 7, p. 183).

10.  **A safety investigation, not a blame investigation.** The Board chose to "identify and correct threats to the safe operation of the Space Shuttle" [V] rather than assign blame or legal liability, took privileged witness statements so people would disclose mistakes and speculate about their organisation's flaws, and left personal accountability to a separate process (App A, pp. 232–233). It also secured its independence from the organisation under review: its own staff, budget, records and databases, and members who could file minority reports (App A, pp. 232–236) [AE].

11.  **Responsibility without scapegoating.** Context "does not mean that individuals are not responsible and accountable" but "NASA's problems cannot be solved simply by retirements, resignations, or transferring personnel" [V] (Ch 8, p. 195); people's actions are shaped by the organisations in which they work (Ch 6, p. 170) [AP]. This is the report's just-culture stance, stated at organisational scale.

12.  **Vigilance decays.** Post-Challenger reforms were undone over time by management actions (Ch 8, p. 198), and the Board expects the energy after Columbia to fade too: "This decline in vigilance is a characteristic of many large organizations, and it has been demonstrated in NASA's own history." [V] (Ch 9, p. 208). The STS-27R inspection precedent had been forgotten, which the Board calls a lack of institutional memory (Ch 6, p. 127) [AE].

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| The findings list a broken part and a person who should have caught it | The investigation has stopped at the widget and the nearest person; the Board calls the resulting belief that the problem is solved "misguided and potentially disastrous" (Ch 7, p. 177) | Ask what in the organisation's history, structure and resources made the failure likely |
| The anomaly had happened before and was "known" | Possible normalisation of deviance: an accepted precedent widened to take in the new event (Ch 8, p. 196) | Trace the anomaly's classification over time and the rationale at each step |
| People say "we couldn't have done anything anyway" | An untested assumption that ended inquiry; the Board found the Columbia crew could conceivably have been rescued (Ch 6, pp. 173–174; Ch 7, p. 181) | Ask whether the option was ever analysed, and by whom |
| A request was cancelled because no one had a "requirement" | Process over merits; the requester may never have been asked (Ch 6, pp. 153, 172) | Find each request and what happened to it |
| The decision rested on one trusted expert | Confirmation by a familiar voice; the expert's scope may not match the question (Ch 6, pp. 149, 169) | Ask what the expert was expert in, and who else was asked |
| The briefing was a slide or a verbal summary | Rolled-up information; uncertainty dropped out (Ch 7, pp. 190–192) | Recover the analysts' full assumptions and compare |
| Safety was "in the room" | Presence is not voice; check its independence and resources (Ch 7, pp. 186–187; Ch 8, p. 202) | Ask what the safety role said, and what it could have done |
| Management says there was no pressure; staff say there was | The Board sided with the workforce on the weight of evidence (Ch 6, p. 131) | Gather accounts from those who did the work |

## When to Use This Reference

- **Reach for it** when an event involved organisational decisions under uncertainty, a long history of an accepted anomaly, a concern raised and not acted on, or schedule and budget pressure; and when a sponsor wants the review to stop at the technical cause or an individual.
- **Reach for it** to design a high-stakes or cross-functional review's terms of reference: independence, privileged statements, separate accountability, scope that reaches history.
- **Pair it** with LFUO or the NHS Just Culture Guide for the individual-level sorting and the interview technique; the report states the stance at organisational scale but gives no interview protocol.
- **Reach for something else** for a 15-minute team AAR (TC 25-20), or when the event is a single slip on familiar work (DOE HPI Vols. 1 and 2).

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored; the report's own case is the loss of Columbia. A software company's billing service double-charged customers for six hours. The proximate cause was a retry setting that let a payment be resubmitted after a timeout. The draft review blames the engineer who changed the timeout and recommends retraining.

**Phase 0.** The facilitator asks the sponsor for written terms: a review to find and correct threats, with what people say kept out of any performance process, and any accountability question handled separately; the engineering director whose team owns billing will not chair it (Source: CAIB, *Report Vol. I*, App A, pp. 232–233).

**Phase 1.** The timeline is extended backward. Duplicate charges had appeared in the ledger three times in the past year, each "within tolerance" and closed as a reconciliation task. The facilitator records how each was classified and on what rationale, because on the Board's account it is the first acceptance that sets the precedent (Ch 8, p. 196).

> For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations. [V] (Source: Ch 8, p. 196)

During the incident, a support lead asked twice for the payment provider to be paused. Both requests went through a ticket queue rather than the incident channel, and the incident commander asked who "required" a pause. The facilitator records both, and the fact that the support lead was never asked directly (Ch 6, pp. 152–153).

**Phase 2.** The analysis names organisational factors alongside the setting. Engineers who doubted the retry logic were asked to show it was unsafe before anyone would roll it back, when a payment change should have been shown safe (Ch 6, p. 169). A quarterly launch date had been briefed to the board, and engineers describe every rollback being weighed against it, though management says there was no pressure (Ch 6, pp. 131, 139). A senior engineer's early "retries are idempotent" settled the question in the incident channel, and no one asked whether he knew the provider's behaviour (Ch 6, p. 169).

> The engineers found themselves in the unusual position of having to prove that the situation was unsafe — a reversal of the usual requirement to prove that a situation is safe. [V] (Source: Ch 6, p. 169)

**Phase 3.** Asked whether the engineer should be disciplined, the facilitator asks whether replacing him would stop the next duplicate charge. The room agrees it would not (Ch 8, p. 195).

**Phase 4.** "Retrain engineer" is replaced with structural actions. Every duplicate charge is logged as an anomaly with a trend owner. The payments platform owner, not the launch team, is given authority to block changes to payment-path settings. Any engineer may pause the provider without proving harm first. Incident commanders ask for dissent before closing a decision (Ch 7, pp. 183, 193; Ch 8, p. 203).

**Phase 5.** A date is set, six months out, for someone outside billing to check that the anomaly log is still being used. The report's warning is that urgency fades (Ch 9, p. 208).

> NASA's challenge is to design systems that maximize the clarity of signals, amplify weak signals so they can be tracked, and account for missing signals. [V] (Source: Ch 8, p. 203)

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Widget-and-person findings.** Signal: a technical fix and a retraining action. Diagnosis: the error the Board says many investigations make (Ch 7, p. 177). Follow-up: add the organisational causes.
- **Hindsight's Russian roulette.** Signal: "they knew it was dangerous and gambled". Diagnosis: the Board rejects that framing as possible only in hindsight; each decision seemed correct at the time (Ch 8, p. 197). Follow-up: reconstruct how the risk was defined then.
- **Proving danger.** Signal: concerns dismissed for lack of proof. Diagnosis: inverted burden of proof (Ch 6, p. 169; Ch 7, p. 190). Follow-up: ask what evidence of safety existed.
- **Channel policing.** Signal: the review asks whether a concern went through the proper route, not whether it was right. Diagnosis: process over merits (Ch 6, p. 172; Ch 7, p. 181). Follow-up: judge the concern on its content.
- **Silent safety.** Signal: "safety was present and agreed". Diagnosis: presence without voice (Ch 8, p. 202). Follow-up: examine the safety role's independence.
- **Personnel change as closure.** Signal: the action is a reassignment. Diagnosis: the Board's warning that this does not solve the problem (Ch 8, p. 195). Follow-up: name the conditions the next person will inherit.
- **One-time urgency.** Signal: actions with no follow-up beyond the review. Diagnosis: vigilance decays and reforms get undone (Ch 8, p. 198; Ch 9, p. 208). Follow-up: external verification at intervals.

## Citation and Source-Integrity Notes

**Borrowed-through gaps.** The report credits the term "normalization of deviance" and much of Chapter 8's analysis to Diane Vaughan's *The Challenger Launch Decision* (1996); "failures of foresight" to Barry Turner; the slide analysis to Edward Tufte; the Russian-roulette comparison to Richard Feynman's appendix to the Rogers report; and its theoretical yardsticks to High Reliability and Normal Accident Theory (Weick, Roberts, La Porte, Sagan, Perrow). None of these is held in the corpus; their ideas appear here only as the Board reports them, paraphrased. Vaughan also served on the Board's staff (App C, p. 244).

Its organisational findings concern NASA's Human Space Flight Program in 2003. The rescue scenario rests on a NASA study done after the accident. The software scenario in the worked example is the projection's.

| Operator describes safety or risk staff who were present but said nothing | Phase 2 | Silent safety; structural dependence (Ch 7, pp. 186–187, 192; Ch 8, p. 202) | "Could the safety person have stopped this? What would it have cost them?" |
| Operator describes an anomaly "everyone knew about" | Phase 0, Phase 1 | Precursor history; drift of classifications (Ch 6, pp. 122–128) | "If everyone knew, when did it stop being a problem, and who decided that?" |
