# US Forest Service LFUO 2024, After-Action Review Distillation

**Source:** US Forest Service, Risk Management Council & Office of Safety & Occupational Health (2024). *Learning From Unintended Outcomes and Learning Review Implementation Guide*. Revised April 2024. **Licence:** Public domain (17 USC §105 — US federal-employee work). **Scope:** open.

## After-Action Review Relevance

The LFUO 2024 guide is a learning-review framework that runs from choosing the review tool to the Learning Review Board. Its load-bearing contributions to AAR work are: a four-tool ladder that routes events to the right learning tool by learning opportunity rather than outcome severity; a coherent stance against causal statements and counterfactual reasoning; the Five Hows / Lessons Learned Analysis as the structured local-rationality reconstruction tool; the Ten Principles and Agreements as the pre-conditions for honest accounts; and the administrative-firewall commitment, which the guide calls critical to protecting information, "the lifeblood of safety" [V] (Source: Part 3, "Administrative assurance of no punitive actions"). The guide carries borrowed-through citations to Dekker, Hollnagel, Weick and Sutcliffe, Klein, and Reason, but the demo does not hold those authors directly; citations to those lineages from this distillation are borrowed-through, not first-hand. The guide replaces "root cause" with *networked causality*: "the combined total of those conditions that collectively influenced the event or outcome" [V] (Source: Glossary, "Networked causality"). Frame to bring: the FLA/Learning Review are not the same thing as an AAR — the four-tool ladder exists to prevent the wrong tool from being applied to the right event.

## Key Concepts for After-Action Review

1.  **Four-tool ladder: AAR / RLS / FLA / Learning Review.** Each tool serves a distinct scope. The AAR produces immediate local learning, confidential to the work unit. The RLS produces a simple shareable lesson. The FLA produces a scalable no-punitive-action learning product without recommendations. The Learning Review adds vetted recommendations via focus groups, optional academic SMEs, and a Learning Review Board (Source: US Forest Service, *LFUO Guide*, Part 2, "The tools"). Scale by learning opportunity, not outcome severity (Part 3, "Starting the process").

2.  **Learning opportunity over outcome severity.** The guide warns that "the natural tendency is to base the size and complexity of the review team upon the severity of the outcome. Often, the more 'serious the accident,' the more resources are put toward the review team. This tendency may pretermit stellar learning opportunities" [V] (Source: Part 3, "Starting the process"). The guide's illustration is a tree-cutting accident in which a firefighter was knocked unconscious and released the same day; had the tree fallen an inch or two to the left, he would have been killed or seriously injured. The learning opportunity does not depend on that chance.

3.  **Administrative firewall as precondition.** "It is critical to maintain a solid firewall between the FLA and any potential administrative actions that may be taken against the employee. Information is the lifeblood of safety" [V] (Source: Part 3, "Administrative assurance of no punitive actions"). The guide requires this commitment in the signed delegation of authority; its Esperanza example shows employees refusing to talk when they did not expect fair treatment (Appendix A).

4.  **Three foundational principles: Just Culture, Systems Thinking, Learning.** Just Culture is defined as the intersection of trust, fairness, and dignity — forward-looking accountability, not criminal-justice culpability. Systems Thinking examines policies, procedures, processes, and culture shaping the event. Learning treats unintended outcomes as "valued learning opportunity and as a means to reducing uncertainty" [V] (Source: Part 1, all three sections). The guide presents the three as intertwined.

5.  **Networked causality replaces root cause.** "Cause isn't something investigators 'find' or 'discover'; cause is always something we create by recreating the event showing how certain anomalies led to the accident" [V] (Source: Appendix B, "A Deeper Dive into Causal Factors") The replacement vocabulary is *networked causality*. "FLAs must avoid causal statements" [V] (Source: Part 5, "Recommendations vs Lessons Learned").

6.  **Counterfactual reasoning is a forbidden move.** "FLA teams must guard against making counterfactual arguments such as: 'If this person had done X, then the outcome would have been Y and the accident would not have occurred.' The FLA is only useful when it learns why people did what they actually did (why it made sense to them at the time)" [V] (Source: Part 9, "Avoid Counterfactual Arguments").

7.  **Five Hows as local-rationality reconstruction tool.** The Lessons Learned Analysis applies the Five Hows to each key decision in the event: explore how and why decisions that seemed wrong in hindsight "were actually valid or at least legitimate in context at the time" [V] (Source: Part 7, "The Five Hows – A Sensemaking Tool") Consensus is not the goal; tension between team-member perspectives is valuable.

8.  **Beliefs, perceptions, expectations, paradigms decomposition.** The Lessons Learned Analysis procedure surfaces the beliefs, perceptions, expectations and paradigms held before the experience that in hindsight turned out wrong, inaccurate, misleading or deceptive (Source: Part 7, "A Process for conducting a Lessons Learned Analysis", step 4). This four-category decomposition makes invisible pre-event mental models visible and testable.

9.  **Ten Principles and Agreements.** The group-dialogue pre-conditions, including: no administrative actions from anything learned through the FLA; respectful discussion; learning-over-blame-assessment; willingness to be vulnerable; team is not there to "fix" a problem; "almost all human actions and decisions are intuitive responses to circumstances largely based on past experiences" [V] (Source: Part 6, "Principles and Agreements for All Involved in the Facilitation"). Explain the FLA process and discuss all ten before the dialogue opens (Part 6, "Agenda for the Dialogue Session").

10.  **Deflection-question protocol for emotional recall.** The guide tells interviewers not to deliberately take the interview into deeper recall of a traumatic event and to "leave that to the mental health professionals" [V]. When a participant's account reaches a vivid traumatic memory anyway, "Be prepared with a few deflection questions to mentally 'walk the person away' from these vivid memories they are sharing with you" [V] (Source: Part 6, "Interviews"). Redirect to something that went right or something they know well and are comfortable talking about. Do not steer the interview back into the vivid recall, and make sure the person knows how to reach mental health professionals.

11.  **Multiple improbable events as the pattern.** "Most often, the Lessons Learned Analysis will reveal that multiple improbable events were necessary for the accident to occur" [V] (Source: Part 7, "The Lessons Learned Analysis"). Single-cause anchoring misses what the guide says the analysis most often finds: several improbable events had to combine.

12.  **Work-as-imagined vs work-as-done as the diagnostic frame.** "How were we actually completing the work vs how managers were expecting the work to be completed?" [V] (Source: Part 1, "Systems Thinking") Illuminating the gap between the two "will illuminate substantial and critical organizational vulnerabilities" [V] (Source: Part 9, "Display Misalignments").

13.  **Reckless-and-willful threshold as the FLA termination criterion.** "A reckless and willful disregard for human safety is conduct that is intentional, unjustifiable, and occurred with the foreknowledge that the conduct was likely to result in serious harm, death, or injury to a human" [V] (Source: Appendix C) If this threshold is met, the FLA terminates; interview notes go to the lead facilitator for confidential and secure storage. Below the threshold, FLA continues.

14.  **Recommendations only through Learning Review.** "In many circumstances, recommendations actually interfere with learning. Often recommendations, when implemented, add complexity to the workplace, which has the paradoxical effect of increasing risk" [V] (Source: Part 5, "Recommendations vs Lessons Learned") Recommendations require Learning Review with the Learning Review Board and Safety Action Plan.

## Questions to Ask During After-Action Review

### Phase 0: Scoping

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Which tool fits this event? | Apply the four-tool ladder: is this an immediate-local AAR, an RLS, an FLA, or a Learning Review? |
| Is the learning opportunity here even if the outcome was minor? | Would a small difference have changed the outcome classification? If yes, the learning opportunity is real regardless of actual outcome severity. |
| Is the administrative firewall in place? | Has the Delegating Official issued a signed delegation of authority committing to no punitive administrative actions? |
| Is there enough trust in this unit for honest accounts? | Is there enough trust here to support such an analysis? Are there conflicting stories surrounding the event? |

### Phase 1: Timeline and Local-Rationality Reconstruction

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| What did participants believe, perceive, expect, and assume before the event? | Work through the four-category decomposition for each key decision: what was the belief, perception, expectation, and paradigm the participant held at the time? |
| Are we interviewing before hindsight contaminates accounts? | Interview as soon as possible (Handout C, "FLA 'Watch outs'"); "memories change; perspectives shift as time progresses" [V] (Part 5, "Should the affected people take Notes?"). |
| Are accounts diverging? | "Don't see this as somebody being right and somebody being wrong" [V] (Part 8, "Different Perspectives"). The guide quotes Dekker, Cilliers and Hofmeyr: diversity of narrative is a source of resilience in complex systems [BT]. |
| Has the interview reached a traumatic memory? | Do not take the interview deeper into that recall; the guide leaves it to mental health professionals. Apply the deflection-question protocol: ask about something that went right or something the person knows well. Make sure they know how to reach mental health professionals (Part 6, "Interviews"). |

### Phase 2: Contributory-Factor Analysis

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Has anyone used the phrase "root cause"? | Redirect: cause is constructed, not discovered. What are the conditions — networked, not linear — that collectively influenced the outcome? |
| Is counterfactual reasoning creeping in? | Redirect to what people actually did and why it made sense to them at the time. |
| Are multiple improbable events visible? | The Lessons Learned Analysis most often reveals that multiple improbable events were necessary. Is the team anchoring on one? |
| Are we applying the Five Hows to each key decision? | For each key action identified: how did the participant develop their understanding of the situation, and why did the action make sense to them? |
| Is the work-as-imagined / work-as-done gap visible? | How were employees actually completing the work vs how administrators expected it to be completed? |

### Phase 3: Just-Culture Sorting

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Has the reckless-and-willful threshold been assessed? | Was the conduct intentional, unjustifiable, and did the person have foreknowledge that it was likely to result in serious harm? The guide expects this to be highly unlikely (Part 9). |
| Is the team using accountabilism language? | "Accountabilism" is the guide's term for claiming to learn from accounts but then punishing via judgmental language or forced retraining. Is that pattern appearing? |
| Is the just-culture framing forward-looking? | "Preventing the next accident, not focused on correcting history" [V] (Part 1, "Just Culture") — is the conversation pointed forward? |

### Phase 4: Action Design

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Are recommendations being produced from an FLA? | Recommendations require a Learning Review with focus groups, a Learning Review Board, and a Safety Action Plan (academic SMEs as an option). |
| Is the proposed action adding complexity to a complex system? | "Often recommendations, when implemented, add complexity to the workplace, which has the paradoxical effect of increasing risk" [V] (Part 5, "Recommendations vs Lessons Learned"). Is the action a barrier suited to a simple or complicated failure, or will it add complexity? |
| Has the Safety Action Plan named responsibility and timing? | The SAP designates who is responsible for each recommendation, and those people are consulted before any resource is committed (Part 11, "Recommendations"). |

### Phase 5: Learning-Loop Closure

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Has the story been validated through the Readback process? | Read the finalised learning product aloud to participants; they correct important details. "Remember this is their story, not yours" [V] (Part 8, "Validating Stories through the Readback process"). |
| Is the learning product in the right format for its audience? | Organisational learning product (for policy-makers: gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done) vs field learning product (for frontline: emotive, experiential, personally reflective). |
| Has the Recommended Reading gap been named? | The guide cites Dekker, Hollnagel, Reason and Conklin in its Recommended Reading, Weick and Sutcliffe in its Glossary and Handout D, and Klein in its Glossary; the demo holds none of them directly. |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| A participant says "the root cause was X" | Root-cause framing has entered the room — the networked-causality position has not been established | Redirect: "Cause is constructed, not discovered. What are the conditions — networked, not linear — that collectively influenced the outcome?" |
| The group moves quickly from timeline to action design | Multiple improbable events have not been surfaced; the analysis has anchored on the most obvious factor | Return to the LLA: what other conditions had to align for this outcome to occur? |
| An FLA is producing a list of recommendations | Recommendations belong to the Learning Review pathway, not the FLA | Name this explicitly; if the sponsoring leader wants recommendations, initiate the Learning Review process |
| An interviewee's account slows or stops at a traumatic moment | The interview has reached vivid traumatic recall, which the guide leaves to mental health professionals | Apply the deflection-question protocol: redirect to a comfortable domain and do not steer back into the vivid recall; point them to mental health support |
| The conversation uses "should have" or "could have" language | Counterfactual reasoning has entered — the forbidden move | Redirect to: "What did they actually do, and why did that make sense at the time?" |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- The scoping decision needs a principled way to choose between AAR, RLS, FLA, and Learning Review — the guide names the four tools and gives the Delegating Official's questions for choosing.
- The group is using root-cause language and needs a named epistemological replacement (networked causality).
- The administrative firewall question has not been answered and the review is about to proceed without it.
- A participant's account is approaching a traumatic memory and the deflection-question protocol is needed.
- Phase 4 is producing recommendations when the sponsoring leader has not initiated a Learning Review.

Reach for LFUO for the learning-review stance (networked causality, local rationality, administrative firewall) and for events that outgrow a local AAR; pair it with TC 25-20 for the AAR protocol itself.

## Worked Example

A maintenance team reviews a near-miss: a gas line was struck during a dig because the locate marks were placed two metres from the actual line. The incident caused no injury but a two-foot difference in dig position would have been a fatality. The facilitator applies the four-tool ladder (Source: US Forest Service, *LFUO Guide*, Part 2, "The tools"; Part 3, "Starting the process"): the learning opportunity is high (near-fatality pattern) despite the minor outcome; the FLA is the right tool. The Delegating Official signs the administrative-firewall commitment before any interviews occur (Part 3, "Administrative assurance of no punitive actions"). The facilitator explains the FLA process and discusses the Ten Principles and Agreements before the dialogue opens (Part 6, "Agenda for the Dialogue Session"). During Phase 1, the facilitator applies the four-category decomposition (Part 7, step 4) to the locate crew's key decision: what did they believe about the line's depth and position? what did they perceive from the as-built drawings? what did they expect the locate flags to represent? The Five Hows reveal that the as-built drawings were last updated twelve years ago and the locate crew had no reason to question them — the decision to trust the drawings was locally rational. In Phase 5, the facilitator runs the Readback: the team corrects two details in the learning narrative before it is finalised. The learning product distinguishes the organisational product (gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done in the drawing-maintenance process) from the field product (emotive narrative for frontline crews about what the crew saw and decided in the moment).

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- Scaling the review team to outcome severity rather than learning opportunity, a tendency that "may pretermit stellar learning opportunities" [V] (Part 3, "Starting the process").
- Producing causal statements in an FLA; cause is constructed, not found.
- Applying counterfactual reasoning: learning why people did not do what hindsight suggests is worthless; learn why they did what they did.
- Anchoring on a single root cause when multiple improbable events were necessary.
- Adding recommendations to an FLA; without a Learning Review and Safety Action Plan, recommendations are unvetted and unowned.
- Delaying interviews until hindsight has already contaminated accounts.
- Letting the review punish through judgmental language or required training while claiming to learn from accounts ("accountabilism", Glossary).
- Proceeding without a signed administrative-firewall commitment; the guide calls the firewall critical, and its Esperanza example shows employees refusing to talk.
- Treating the work-as-imagined gap as an individual failing rather than an organisational vulnerability.
- Citing Dekker, Hollnagel, Weick, Klein, or Reason as if the demo holds them directly; the citations are borrowed-through this guide's Recommended Reading, Glossary, handouts and footnotes.

**Named limits of the source.** The LFUO guide is designed for the US Forest Service context and uses wildland-fire and forest-management worked examples throughout. Applicability to non-field, non-safety-critical, or non-US-federal contexts is by adaptation. The guide explicitly limits FLAs: they cannot produce recommendations (recommendations require a full Learning Review). The reckless-and-willful threshold definition (Appendix C) is the guide's own working definition, not a legal standard; facilitators operating in environments with specific legal accountability frameworks should check local applicability.
