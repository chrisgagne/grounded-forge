# US DOE, Human Performance Improvement Handbook Vol. 2, Decision-Making Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Department of Energy (2009). *Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 2: Human Performance Tools for Individuals, Work Teams, and Management*. DOE-HDBK-1028-2009. Distribution Statement A, approved for public release. Licence: **Public domain (US Government work, 17 USC §105)**; DOE asks for acknowledgement in reuse. **Scope:** open. Third-party material inside the handbook is paraphrased here, never quoted: the individual and work-team tools (Sections 1 and 2, including the decision-making, problem-solving, questioning-attitude and validate-assumptions tools, whose primary references the Foreword credits to two INPO guides), the survey instruments, and the just-culture and investigation material that Volume 1 credits to named authors.

## Decision-Making Relevance

Volume 2 treats decision making as an error-prone activity with its own tool. Its **Decision Making** tool sets out a five-step method (goal, options, analysis, plan, review) and a list of practices that make a decision conservative, meaning it puts the facility's safety needs ahead of near-term production; it says a methodical process guards against rule-based and knowledge-based errors (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 2*, Sec 2, pp. 63–65). Around it sit tools for the parts of a decision where people most often go wrong: **validating assumptions** before they harden into fact, a **questioning attitude** with named "danger words", **pausing when unsure** in knowledge-based situations, **PACTS** for working backward from a problem before deciding what to do, the **devil's advocate**, and the **signature** discipline for the moment someone puts their name to a decision (Sec 1, pp. 10–25; Sec 2, pp. 61–66).

The management section adds decisions about the organisation itself: how to choose and read performance indicators, how to use benchmarking and independent oversight to see past one's own assumptions, and why even small changes need structured handling to keep managers from erring (Sec 3, pp. 71–82, 93–94). The Foreword frames the whole volume as a menu to choose from, not a requirement (Foreword, p. ii). In decision work it fires hardest in Phase 2 (bounding: assumptions and facts), Phase 4 (deciding) and Phase 6 (monitoring), with uses in every phase. Its companion `doe-hpi-handbook-vol1-decision-making.md` carries the performance-mode framing and conservative decision-making as a principle; this volume carries the procedures.

## Key Concepts for Decision-Making

1.  **The five-step decision tool.** Goal (the desired future state and the critical attributes for success), Options (several alternatives), Analysis (critical assumptions, effects on stakeholders and users, pros and cons, short- and long-term risks, benefits and costs, relevant operating experience), Plan (who, what, by when; conservative on critical attributes; contingencies; abort or hold criteria; stakeholder involvement) and Review (periodic effectiveness reviews and an independent review of the proposed decision) [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 63–64). Problem solving looks backward to causes; decision making looks forward to effects [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 61, 63).

2.  **Conservative decisions.** Whether immediate or formal, safety requires conservatism, which the handbook promotes by: staying within the safe operating envelope; using all available information and resisting the urge to discount contradictory or disconfirming data; using all available expertise, including front-line workers; minimising uncertainty by relying on facts and challenging assumptions; maintaining safety despite production pressure; considering cumulative risk; and preparing contingencies [AP] (Sec 2, p. 63).

3.  **Validate assumptions.** Assumptions bound a problem while knowledge is gathered, but they must not harden into fact or be forgotten; they are most tempting under stress and time pressure and where knowledge is lacking [AR] (Sec 1, p. 22). Write each down (what it affects, the reasoning, the worst case, the level of certainty), find objective evidence, check it in the field, and close out every unverified assumption before the product is delivered [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 22–23). At-risk practices include not verifying an assumption because the source seems competent and leaning too heavily on past successes [AP] (Sec 1, p. 23).

4.  **Questioning attitude and danger words.** Questions such as *what if* and *why is this okay* help catch improper assumptions; phrases such as *I think*, *we've always done it this way* or *we didn't have a problem last time* signal an assumption [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 13, 22). The planning-stage version (F-A-C-T-S) asks the decision-maker to foresee activities with critical attributes, confirm knowns and unknowns, and test the current situation against documentation, a peer, or alternate analysis, stopping if still unsure [AP] (Sec 1, p. 14). It warns that "cookbooking" and over-reliance on rules of thumb lead to rule-based errors [AR] (Sec 1, p. 13).

5.  **Pause when unsure in knowledge-based situations.** With no rule or pattern to apply, the handbook puts the chance of error at 10 to 50 percent (and 1:2 to 1:10 in problem solving), so the best action when unsure is to stop and involve at least one other knowledgeable person [AP] (Sec 1, p. 16; Sec 2, p. 61). Everyone has the responsibility and authority to stop when uncertainty persists [AP] (Sec 1, p. 16).

6.  **PACTS before deciding a fix.** State the problem as a gap agreed by those closest to it; analyse with structured methods; identify causes consistent with the facts and sensitive to refuting evidence; test them; then propose solutions weighed for risk, benefit and cost [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 61–62). At-risk practices include deciding without understanding the risks, acting when unsure of the cause, using intuition or brainstorming alone for risk-important issues, and avoiding controversy [AP] (Sec 2, p. 62).

7.  **The devil's advocate and differing opinions.** Leaders can assign a devil's advocate to watch the team's decision process for flaws, because most people focus on getting the task done rather than on what to avoid; review meetings value diverse and critical opinions, use known processes for examining differing professional opinions, and can designate a devil's advocate to challenge assumptions and statements of belief [AR] (Sec 2, pp. 63, 66).

8.  **Signing for a decision.** Before putting their name to a technical product, the signer affirms knowledge, involvement, independence, quality, that it is right and proper, and that they have no doubt; otherwise they stop and ask for help [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 24–25). At-risk practices include deferring to what management wants without critical thought, relaxing standards for expediency and being in a hurry [AP] (Sec 1, p. 25).

9.  **Choosing what to measure.** "Frequently, things are counted because they can be, but provide little insight as to how the organization is functioning now and no clue to future performance" [V] (Sec 3, p. 77). The handbook gives seven criteria for judging an indicator (direct, objective, adequate, quantitative where possible, disaggregated where appropriate, practical, reliable), recommends two or three per objective, and says "Leading indicators are more valuable than lagging indicators, but normally more difficult to select and use" [V] (Sec 3, pp. 78–79). With sparse data "a single event could appear to be a trend" [V], so statistical analysis is needed to avoid knee-jerk responses (Sec 3, p. 77).

10.  **Seeing past one's own assumptions.** "Benchmarking is a powerful management tool because it overcomes “Paradigm Blindness.”" [V], the belief that the current way is best because it has always been done that way (Sec 3, p. 71). Independent oversight is "essentially a reverse benchmarking exercise" [V], and outsiders "may spot troubled areas and process weaknesses far more readily than individuals living within the system" [V] (Sec 3, p. 80).

11.  **Decisions to change how work is done.** "Without a structured approach to planning and implementing change, the error potential for managers and the support staff is unacceptably greater" [V] (Sec 3, p. 93). The handbook offers an eleven-step process, from creating a vision and consulting the people affected through to short-term successes and patience [AP] (Sec 3, pp. 93–94).

12.  **Deciding which tools to adopt.** "This handbook is not a requirements document" [V]; organisations should treat it as a menu and "Organizations should avoid changing their error prevention tools if the current tools and behavior standards presently in place are effective" [V] (Foreword, p. ii).

## Questions to Ask During Decision-Making

### Phase 1: Framing (recognising what kind of decision is being made)

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a rule for this? | Is this a situation we have a rule or procedure for, or one with no rule or pattern, where the chance of error is high and a second knowledgeable person is needed? | Sec 1, p. 16; Sec 2, p. 61 |
| Are we deciding, or diagnosing? | Do we need to work backward to why the problem exists before deciding what to do? | Sec 2, pp. 61, 63 |
| Is the problem stated as a gap? | Can we state what is versus what should be without naming a cause? | Sec 2, pp. 61–62 |
| What does success require? | What is the goal, stated as a future state, and which critical attributes must the decision protect? | Sec 2, p. 64 |

### Phase 2: Bounding (defining the decision space)

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What are we assuming? | Which statements in the case are assumptions (*I think*, *we've always*, *last time it was fine*)? Have they been written down? | Sec 1, pp. 22–23 |
| What is the evidence for each? | For each assumption, what objective evidence supports it, and has anyone checked it in the field? | Sec 1, pp. 22–23 |
| Are we trusting a source because it seems competent? | Is a vendor's, expert's or colleague's claim being accepted without verification? | Sec 1, p. 23; Sec 2, pp. 68–69 |
| Where is the safe envelope? | What limits must the decision stay within? | Sec 2, p. 63 |
| What do the knowns and unknowns look like? | Which critical facts have been verified against current conditions, and which remain unknown? | Sec 1, p. 14 |

### Phase 3: Exploring (generating alternatives)

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Do we have more than one option? | What other alternatives could reach the goal? | Sec 2, p. 64 |
| Have we used the people who know? | Have front-line workers and others with relevant expertise been asked? | Sec 2, p. 63 |
| Is someone looking for what could go wrong? | Has a devil's advocate been named? | Sec 2, pp. 63, 66 |
| What have others done? | Has anyone looked at how better-performing organisations handle this, or at relevant operating experience? | Sec 2, p. 64; Sec 3, pp. 71, 90 |

### Phase 4: Deciding (analysing and selecting)

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is disconfirming data being discounted? | What information points against the favoured option, and is it being explained away? | Sec 2, p. 63 |
| Is production pressure deciding? | Would we choose the same option without the schedule? | Sec 2, p. 63 |
| Have we counted the cumulative risk? | What does this decision add to risks already being carried? | Sec 2, p. 63 |
| Do we understand the risks? | Can we state the short- and long-term risks, benefits and costs of each option? | Sec 2, pp. 64–65 |
| Would the signer sign without doubt? | Does the person putting their name to this have the knowledge, involvement, independence and no remaining doubt, or are they deferring to what management wants? | Sec 1, pp. 24–25 |
| Has it been reviewed independently? | Has someone not involved in the analysis reviewed the proposed decision? | Sec 2, p. 64 |

### Phase 5: Ratifying (committing to and communicating the decision)

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is the plan specific? | Who does what by when? | Sec 2, p. 64 |
| When would we stop? | What are the abort or hold criteria, and what contingencies cover unintended consequences? | Sec 2, p. 64 |
| Are stakeholders involved? | Have the people affected been consulted and their commitment sought? | Sec 2, pp. 60, 64; Sec 3, p. 93 |
| Are open assumptions closed? | Has every unverified assumption been closed out as valid or otherwise before the decision is handed on? | Sec 1, p. 22 |

### Phase 6: Monitoring (post-decision review)

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| How will we know it worked? | Which two or three indicators, ideally leading, will track the decision's effect, and who owns them? | Sec 3, pp. 78–79 |
| Are we reacting to a single event? | Is a change in course being driven by one data point? | Sec 3, p. 77 |
| Is the effectiveness review scheduled? | When will the periodic effectiveness review happen? | Sec 2, p. 64 |
| Did it work without side effects? | After a significant change, has it been re-assessed for unintended consequences? | Sec 3, p. 76 |
| Would outsiders see what we miss? | Would a benchmarking visit or an independent review challenge the assumptions we are now living with? | Sec 3, pp. 71, 80 |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| *I think*, *we've always*, *it was fine last time* carrying the argument | Unrecorded assumptions; over-reliance on past success (Sec 1, pp. 22–23) | Write each assumption down and ask for its evidence |
| The vendor or expert says it is equivalent, so it is | Not verifying an assumption because of the source's perceived competence (Sec 1, p. 23); validate vendor data with objective evidence (Sec 2, p. 69) | Ask for the evidence and check it in the field |
| The schedule is the main reason for the choice | Production pressure overriding conservatism (Sec 2, p. 63) | Ask whether the same option would be chosen without the schedule |
| Data that cuts against the favoured option is labelled noise | Discounting disconfirming data (Sec 2, p. 63) | Give it to a devil's advocate to argue |
| A decision is signed by someone who did not do or check the work | Signature at-risk practice (Sec 1, p. 25) | Ask what the signature means and who can sign without doubt |
| The decision has no stopping condition | Missing abort or hold criteria (Sec 2, p. 64) | Set them before committing |
| A metric is adopted because the tool already reports it | "things are counted because they can be" [V] (Sec 3, p. 77) | Choose against the seven criteria; add a leading measure |
| "We do it this way because we always have" | Paradigm blindness (Sec 3, p. 71) | Benchmark or invite an independent view |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- A decision rests on assumptions or on someone else's assurance, and they need to be surfaced and checked.
- Schedule or production pressure is pushing a choice where safety or quality is at stake.
- The team is in unfamiliar territory and needs permission and a procedure to pause and bring in another person.
- A decision needs a plan with owners, contingencies and abort criteria, and a review afterwards.
- Someone must choose how to measure whether a decision worked.
- An organisation is deciding which error-prevention practices to adopt or change.

For the reasoning behind conservative decisions and the performance modes, read `doe-hpi-handbook-vol1-decision-making.md`. For decision biases and group processes in general, OpenStax Organizational Behavior carries more. The handbook's setting is DOE facility and engineering work.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored, set in the kind of work the handbook was written for. An engineering lead at a utility must decide whether to install a replacement protective relay from a new vendor during a maintenance outage that closes on Friday. The original model is out of stock. The vendor's sales engineer says the new relay is "a drop-in equivalent". One technician mentions that the new relay's default settings differ from the old one's; the project manager says there is no time to look into it.

**Framing and bounding.** The lead recognises an unlike-replacement decision, one of the cues the handbook lists for its decision tool (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 2*, Sec 2, p. 64). The goal is stated as a future state: protection that trips at the same conditions as before, returned to service by the end of the outage or not at all (Sec 2, p. 64). The lead writes down the assumptions: "drop-in equivalent", "default settings are acceptable", "the wiring matches". The first rests only on the vendor's word, which the handbook says to validate with objective evidence rather than trust (Sec 1, pp. 22–23; Sec 2, p. 69).

**Exploring.** Options are listed: install the new relay with verified settings; extend the outage; temporarily reinstate the old relay pending stock; or source an identical unit from another site. A senior technician is named devil's advocate (Sec 2, p. 63).

**Deciding.** The devil's advocate presses on the technician's comment about default settings, which the project manager had dismissed. The lead treats it as disconfirming data that must not be discounted (Sec 2, p. 63). Checking the settings against the old relay's takes half a day and finds one protection setting that differs. The schedule argument is set against the handbook's practice of maintaining safety despite production pressure (Sec 2, p. 63). The chosen option is to install the new relay with corrected settings, verified by an independent check and a functional test, with an abort criterion: if the test does not match the old relay's response, reinstate the old relay and extend the outage (Sec 2, p. 64). The engineer who will sign the modification confirms they can do so without doubt (Sec 1, pp. 24–25).

**Monitoring.** The lead sets a review after the first month in service and records the vendor's settings difference as operating experience for other sites (Sec 2, p. 64; Sec 3, pp. 90–91). The lead also notes, for the next management review, that the out-of-stock part is a latent condition worth fixing upstream:

> Once they are created they do not fade away but rather accumulate in the system [V] (Source: Sec 3, p. 70)

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Assumption as fact.** Signal: *it's equivalent*, *it was fine last time*. Diagnosis: an unrecorded, unverified assumption (Sec 1, pp. 22–23). Follow-up: document it, ask for evidence, close it out before deciding.
- **Trusting the expert.** Signal: no one checks the vendor's or senior's claim. Diagnosis: not verifying because of perceived competence (Sec 1, p. 23; Sec 2, p. 69). Follow-up: validate with objective evidence.
- **Schedule as the decider.** Signal: the deadline settles the debate. Diagnosis: production pressure overriding conservative practice (Sec 2, p. 63). Follow-up: ask what the decision would be without the deadline.
- **Discounting the dissenter.** Signal: a contrary observation is dismissed. Diagnosis: discounting disconfirming data (Sec 2, p. 63). Follow-up: assign a devil's advocate to pursue it.
- **No exit.** Signal: the plan has no abort or hold criteria. Diagnosis: an incomplete plan step (Sec 2, p. 64). Follow-up: set the stopping condition before committing.
- **Deciding before the goal.** Signal: options are debated before anyone states what success is. Diagnosis: the first at-risk practice in the decision tool (Sec 2, p. 65). Follow-up: write the goal first.
- **Easy metrics.** Signal: the decision's success is tracked by whatever is already counted. Diagnosis: counting because it can be counted (Sec 3, p. 77). Follow-up: choose indicators against the seven criteria.

## Citation and Source-Integrity Notes

**Borrowed-through gaps.** Volume 2 names no outside author. Its Foreword credits two INPO guides as the primary references for the individual and work-team tools, which include the decision-making, problem-solving, questioning-attitude, validate-assumptions and signature tools used here; INPO is not held in the corpus, and all of that material is paraphrased.

**Named limits of the source.** The handbook's decision tool is written for engineering and facility decisions where safety is at stake; its conservatism is a safety stance, not a general rule for all decisions. It presents its change process as one of many. It does not discuss decision biases at length or give a theory of choice; Volume 1 and OpenStax carry more of that. The relay scenario is the projection's.
