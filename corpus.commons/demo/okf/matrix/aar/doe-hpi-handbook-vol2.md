# US DOE, Human Performance Improvement Handbook Vol. 2, After-Action Review Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Department of Energy (2009). *Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 2: Human Performance Tools for Individuals, Work Teams, and Management*. DOE-HDBK-1028-2009. Distribution Statement A, approved for public release. Licence: **Public domain (US Government work, 17 USC §105)**; DOE asks for acknowledgement in reuse. **Scope:** open. Third-party material inside the handbook is paraphrased here, never quoted: the individual and work-team tools (Sections 1 and 2, whose primary references the Foreword credits to two INPO guides), the three survey instruments (INPO and Naval Postgraduate School), and the investigation, just-culture, culpability and reporting material of Section 3 (pp. 85–89 and 96–99), which this distillation paraphrases as a conservative choice.

## After-Action Review Relevance

Volume 2 is DOE's catalogue of error-prevention tools, and for an AAR it does two things. First, it names the **specific tool that should have caught each kind of error**, so a review can ask which defence was missing or degraded rather than "who slipped": self-checking for skill-based work; place-keeping, flagging and the communication tools for the specific errors each names (an omitted or repeated step, a similar but wrong component, a misheard message); procedure adherence, validating assumptions and a planning-stage questioning attitude for rule-based work; pausing when unsure, structured problem solving and methodical decision making for knowledge-based situations; peer-checking for wrong actions; concurrent and independent verification for wrong conditions; peer review for defects in documents (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 2*, Sec 1, pp. 5, 13, 16, 18, 26–29; Sec 2, pp. 41–46, 51, 61, 63). Each tool comes with a list of "at-risk practices", the behaviours, beliefs, assumptions or conditions that undermine it, which gives the facilitator a ready list of contributory factors to probe (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

Second, its management section treats the review itself as a tool. "Investigating Events Triggered by Human Error" sets a traditional, blame-seeking view against one in which error is a symptom of deeper system trouble and the starting point of an investigation, tells the investigator to debrief people before learning the event's details, and, using the Anatomy of an Event model as a guide, works backward to failed defences, precursors and organisational contributors (Sec 3, pp. 85–89). Around it sit the post-job review (pluses, deltas and face-to-face follow-up), the error-reporting prerequisites with a just culture as the required first step, the culpability decision tree from individual to organisational culpability, and operating experience built on precursor events (Sec 2, pp. 54–57; Sec 3, pp. 90–99). In AAR work it fires hardest in Phase 1 (reconstruction), Phase 2 (which defences failed) and Phase 4 (fitting actions to error types), with real use in Phases 0, 3 and 5. It pairs with its companion, `doe-hpi-handbook-vol1-aar.md`, which carries the error taxonomy and event model these tools are built on.

## Key Concepts for After-Action Review

1.  **Tools fitted to error types.** The handbook does not tabulate tools against error types, but its tool texts make the fit explicit: situational-awareness tools and self-checking for skill-based work, with attention peaking when a component's status changes; procedure use and adherence and validate assumptions for rule-based work; pause when unsure, PACTS problem solving and methodical decision making for knowledge-based situations, where it puts error chances at 10–50 percent and 1:2 to 1:10 [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 5, 16, 18; Sec 2, pp. 61, 63). For an AAR, the question "which tool would have caught this, and was it in use?" follows from naming the error type.

2.  **Active errors, latent errors, defects.** An active error is "An error that changes equipment, system, or plant state, triggering immediate, undesired consequences" [V] (Definitions, p. 128). A defect is "An undesired result of an error committed earlier in the engineering process, which becomes embedded in either the physical plant or design bases documentation" [V] (Definitions, p. 129). Latent conditions "remain hidden until revealed by periodic testing, self-assessment processes, operating experience, or an event" [V] (Definitions, p. 130), and "Once they are created they do not fade away but rather accumulate in the system" [V] (Sec 3, p. 70). An AAR timeline needs both kinds on it.

3.  **Investigating events triggered by human error.** The handbook contrasts a traditional view that blames competence, attention or attitude with one where error is a symptom of deeper trouble, latent conditions set it up and decide its severity, and error is where an investigation starts, aimed at preventing recurrence rather than punishment [AR] (Sec 3, p. 85). Hindsight disposes investigators to look for individual shortcomings, and explaining what people should have done says nothing about why they did what they did [AR] (Sec 3, p. 85). Paraphrased; see the source line.

4.  **Debrief before you know too much; reconstruct the inside view.** Interview participants before acquiring detailed knowledge of the event; let each tell the story and tell it back; mark the critical junctures; at each, probe the cues noticed, the knowledge and past experience drawn on, the expectations and options seen, the goals and conflicts between them, time pressure, and whether the outcome fitted expectations; divide the sequence into episodes and find what data people had in each; then explain why their actions made sense, on the principle that people act reasonably given their knowledge, goals, viewpoint and limited resources [AP] (Sec 3, pp. 85–88). At-risk practices include learning too much before debriefing and concluding that people acted unreasonably without saying why [AP] (Sec 3, pp. 88–89).

5.  **The backward pass through the Anatomy of an Event.** After the reconstruction, identify the tasks tied to the initiating action, the defences that failed to prevent, catch or mitigate the individual's error, those that failed to protect the facility, the job-site conditions and error precursors that provoked errors or encouraged violations, and, for each, the organisational process or cultural contributors [AP] (Sec 3, p. 88). Assuming the system is sound, or ignoring the front-end reconstruction in this back-end analysis, are named at-risk practices [AP] (Sec 3, p. 89).

6.  **At-risk practices as contributory factors.** Each tool lists the behaviours, beliefs, assumptions or conditions that undermine it; the Definitions call an at-risk practice "A behavior, belief, assumption, or condition that tends to diminish the effectiveness of a human performance tool or that increases the chance of error during an action" [V] (Definitions, p. 128). Examples an AAR will meet: a peer reluctant to correct a more senior performer, both parties relaxing because the other will check, a verifier relying only on process indications, peer-checking over-used until it breeds complacency, and treating repetitive work as routine and risk-free [AP] (Sec 1, p. 9; Sec 2, pp. 43, 45, 47–48).

7.  **Checking versus verification.** Checking confirms a correct action and prevents the performer's error; verification confirms that equipment condition or document accuracy matches the governing documents [AP] (Sec 2, p. 41). Peer-checking, the least rigorous, prevents a wrong act; concurrent verification confirms a condition side by side, without true independence; independent verification, separated in time and distance, tends to catch more errors; peer review detects defects in another's work product [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 41–50). An AAR can ask which of these the job had, and whether it was the right one for the failure.

8.  **Pause when unsure and the questioning attitude.** Everyone has the responsibility and authority to stop when uncertainty persists, making the job safe, notifying the supervisor and getting help; "danger words" such as *I assume*, *probably* or *we've always* are cues to stop and check facts [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 10, 16–17). At-risk practices include discounting less experienced people's concerns, not asking for help out of embarrassment, arguing over who is right rather than what is right, and rationalising anomalies [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 11, 17). An AAR can ask where in the sequence someone was unsure and what happened to that doubt.

9.  **The graded pre-job briefing and the "routine" trap.** The handbook grades briefings by hazard and complexity and states that most events occur during so-called routine activities, which people wrongly equate with no hazard [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 34–35). The agenda covers the tools for each critical step, operating experience, stop-work criteria and who makes critical decisions; at-risk practices include generalities, monologues, a checklist run through regardless of relevance, and not assigning abort responsibilities [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 35–37). An AAR can check what the briefing said against what happened.

10.  **Post-job review: pluses, deltas, face-to-face follow-up.** A short self-assessment after the work that captures what went well and what to improve, especially at critical steps, comparing what happened with what was planned, routes issues, through the corrective action programme as appropriate, and reports the resolution of high-interest issues back to the performers face to face [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 54–56). The technical version states that errors causing significant events are organisational failures and emphasises flawed defences and error traps met [AR] (Sec 2, p. 56).

11.  **Just culture first, then the culpability spectrum.** A just culture is the required prerequisite for error reporting: zero tolerance for reckless conduct and widespread confidence among managers that honest errors will go unpunished, with people judged by intent rather than by consequence [AP] (Sec 3, p. 96). The handbook names a Foresight Test, a Culpability Test, a Substitution Test and a Culpability Decision Tree whose outcomes run from intentional harm and possible reckless violation, through possible negligent error, to organisation-induced violation and organisation-induced error, with a prompt to evaluate organisational processes and management practices [AP] (Sec 3, pp. 96–99). Paraphrased; the handbook refers readers to Volume 1, Chapter 4, for more detail on these methods (Sec 3, p. 96).

12.  **Precursor events and operating experience.** The OE case rests on the claim that "serious accidents are almost always preceded by less serious “precursor events.”" [V] (Sec 3, p. 90); uncorrected minor failures recur and combine into serious events, and OE is meant to combat the tendency to think "It can't happen here" [AP] (Sec 3, pp. 90–91). For an AAR, it asks whether this event had precursors and whether this review's lessons will reach others.

## Questions to Ask During After-Action Review

### Phase 0: Scoping

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a just culture to report into? | Before asking people for accounts of their errors: do they know that honest errors will not be punished and that only reckless conduct will be? The handbook makes a just culture the required prerequisite for error reporting. | Sec 3, pp. 96–97 |
| Will people speak? | Which of the usual barriers apply here: embarrassment, fear that the account will count against the person, doubt that management will act, or the effort involved? What de-identification, protection, separation from discipline and feedback will this review offer? | Sec 3, p. 97 (paraphrased) |

### Phase 1: Timeline and Local-Rationality Reconstruction

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Whose story first? | Has each person told the story from their own point of view, and has it been told back to them to check it was understood as they meant it? | Sec 3, p. 86 |

| What did it look like from inside? | At each juncture: what were you seeing and focusing on, what did you expect, what did it remind you of, which rules seemed to apply, what goals were you working to and did they conflict, was there time pressure, did the outcome fit your expectation? | Sec 3, pp. 86–87 (paraphrased) |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| The investigator has read every log before talking to anyone | Hindsight risk: the investigation will look for evidence of individual shortcomings (Sec 3, pp. 85, 88) | Debrief the people involved first, from their point of view, before more file work |
| The analysis says what people should have done | Counterfactual explanation: the handbook holds that saying what people should have done does not explain why they acted as they did (paraphrased; Sec 3, p. 85) | Return to the critical junctures and ask what made the action reasonable at the time |
| Two people were on the job, so "it was checked" | The check may have been the wrong kind or disabled by at-risk practices: side-by-side when independence was needed, a junior reluctant to challenge, each relying on the other (Sec 2, pp. 43–48) | Ask what kind of check it was (action or condition) and how it was carried out |
| The verification confirmed the condition from a flow or pressure reading | Process indications can mislead through alternate paths; hands-on checks are preferred (Sec 2, pp. 45, 47) | Ask how the condition was confirmed, and whether a local check was possible |
| "It was a routine job" | The handbook says most events occur during so-called routine activities (Sec 2, p. 35) | Ask what the briefing covered and whether the critical step was named |
| Someone had a doubt and kept going | Pause-when-unsure not used, or not safe to use (Sec 1, pp. 16–17) | Ask what stopping would have cost the person and who would have helped |
| Actions are all "retrain" or "revise the procedure" | An at-risk practice in problem solving (Sec 2, p. 62) | Fit each action to the error type and the failed defence |
| Findings stop at the worker's behaviour | "Focusing totally on the individual’s behaviors, and ignoring job-site conditions and organizational processes and values that support individual performance" [V] is an at-risk practice in observation (Sec 3, p. 74) | Trace each failed defence and precursor to an organisational contributor |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- The review needs to name which defence or tool should have caught the error, and why it did not.
- A check or verification was in place and still failed, and the group needs the difference between checking an action and verifying a condition, and the at-risk practices that disable each.
- The facilitator needs a step-by-step debrief protocol that reconstructs the inside view before the backward pass to organisational contributors.
- Proposed actions are generic ("retrain", "be more careful") and need fitting to the error type.
- The organisation is setting up error and near-miss reporting, or a post-job review habit, and needs the prerequisites.

Do not use it as the AAR procedure (TC 25-20 and LFUO 2024 carry that) or in place of the NHS five-test tree for an individual-action decision. Its setting is DOE facilities, nuclear and laboratory work, and engineering offices; carrying it to other work is this projection's move. For the error taxonomy and the reasons behind the tools, read `doe-hpi-handbook-vol1-aar.md` alongside it.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored; the handbook's own examples are DOE facility work. A water treatment plant reviews an event in which a chemical feed pump was returned to service after maintenance with the wrong one of two identical, adjacent isolation valves opened. The chemical overdosed a basin for forty minutes until an alarm. The technician had been called to the phone midway through the restoration. A peer-checker had been assigned but left for another job before the valve step. The independent verification at the end of the job confirmed "flow established" from the control-room flow indication, which read normally because the other line was also flowing.

**Phase 0.** Before the debriefs, the facilitator checks that the plant has said clearly that honest errors will not be punished, since the handbook makes a just culture the prerequisite for people reporting errors (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 2*, Sec 3, p. 96), and arranges to debrief the technician, the peer-checker and the verifier before reading the full log, to keep hindsight out of the first accounts (Sec 3, p. 85).

**Phase 1.** Each person tells the story; the facilitator tells it back and marks the critical junctures: the interruption, the return to the valve gallery, the valve step, the verification (Sec 3, p. 86). At the valve step the technician was looking for "the valve I'd been working on" and found an identical one in the place they expected; nothing marked the right valve (Sec 3, pp. 86–88).

**Phase 2.** The error came after an interruption on familiar, skill-based work. Self-checking is the handbook's tool for skill-based work, and flagging is aimed at exactly the case of returning after a distraction to a similar but wrong component; neither was in use (Sec 1, pp. 18–19; Sec 2, p. 51). The peer-check was disabled by an at-risk practice, the peer not being in a position to observe; and the verification confirmed a condition only from process indications, which alternate flow paths can make unreliable (Sec 2, pp. 43, 47). The briefing had treated the restoration as routine and had not named the valve step as critical (Sec 2, pp. 35–36). Behind each factor sit organisational contributors: staffing that pulled the peer away, no approved flagging devices on site, and a verification method set by habit rather than designated by management (Sec 2, p. 46; Sec 3, p. 88). The handbook's definition gives the group words for the last of these:

> Undetected circumstances or situations such as equipment flaws, a willingness to sacrifice safety margin for immediate production goals, and various process, program, and procedure deficiencies that remain hidden until revealed by periodic testing, self-assessment processes, operating experience, or an event. [V] (Source: Definitions, p. 130)

**Phase 3.** A supervisor asks whether the technician should be disciplined. The facilitator asks whether a similarly trained technician, interrupted at the same point with two identical valves, could have done the same; the room says yes, which by the handbook's substitution test means blame is likely inappropriate and turns the review toward organisational processes and supervisory practices (Sec 3, pp. 96–99, paraphrased).

**Phase 4.** The draft action "retrain technician on valve line-ups" is replaced. The handbook lists concluding that training or a procedure change will always solve a problem as an at-risk practice (Sec 2, p. 62). The actions become: approved flagging devices and a procedure step to flag the component before work; the valve step marked as critical in the procedure and the briefing; independent verification by a hands-on check of valve position rather than flow indication; and a peer-check that cannot be signed unless the peer observed the act (Sec 1, p. 29; Sec 2, pp. 43, 47, 51). The facilitator reminds the group what tools can and cannot do:

> Safety is not obtained by mindlessly applying human performance tools but rather by people conscientiously applying their knowledge, skills, experience and insights, as well as the tools to accomplish their work goals. [V] (Source: Introduction, p. 1)

**Phase 5.** The supervisor reports the resolutions to the three people face to face, the event goes into the plant's operating experience as a precursor, and a re-assessment is booked to confirm the new verification method is in use (Sec 2, p. 55; Sec 3, pp. 76, 90–91).

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Investigating from the file.** Signal: the investigator knows the event in detail before anyone is interviewed. Diagnosis: hindsight disposes the analysis toward individual shortcomings (Sec 3, p. 85). Follow-up: debrief first, then read.
- **"It was checked."** Signal: a second person's presence is taken as proof the defence worked. Diagnosis: the wrong kind of check, or one disabled by at-risk practices (Sec 2, pp. 41–48). Follow-up: ask whether it checked an action or verified a condition, and how.
- **Retrain-or-rewrite reflex.** Signal: every action is training or a procedure revision. Diagnosis: an at-risk practice the handbook names (Sec 2, p. 62). Follow-up: fit each action to the error type and the failed defence.
- **Stopping at the worker.** Signal: the findings describe only behaviour. Diagnosis: the handbook's at-risk practice of ignoring job-site conditions and organisational processes (Sec 3, p. 74). Follow-up: trace each factor to its organisational contributor (Sec 3, p. 88).
- **Reporting without a just culture.** Signal: a new reporting channel announced to people who fear discipline. Diagnosis: launching a reporting system before establishing a just culture is the first at-risk practice the handbook lists for reporting [AP] (Sec 3, p. 98). Follow-up: settle the just-culture commitments first.
- **Silence after the review.** Signal: participants never hear what happened to their input. Diagnosis: the post-job review's at-risk practices of no follow-up and follow-up not done face to face (Sec 2, p. 55). Follow-up: report resolutions in person.
- **Dismissing the near miss.** Signal: no harm, so no review. Diagnosis: serious accidents are almost always preceded by precursor events (Sec 3, p. 90). Follow-up: review it and share it as operating experience.

## Citation and Source-Integrity Notes

**Borrowed-through gaps.** Its Foreword credits two INPO guides as the primary references for the individual and work-team tools, and its survey instruments to INPO and the Naval Postgraduate School; none of these is held in the corpus. It also says outside books and articles on accident reduction were used for the management tools, without saying which passages draw on them. All of that material is paraphrased here, and so, as a conservative choice, is the investigation, just-culture, culpability and reporting material.

**Named limits of the source.** The handbook is a menu, not a requirements document, and expects organisations to choose tools to suit their needs (Foreword, p. ii). Its setting is DOE facilities, its examples nuclear, industrial and engineering; the water-plant scenario above is the projection's. It does not tabulate tools against error types; the mapping in Concept 1 gathers statements from individual tool texts. The culpability decision tree survives in the converted text without its arrows, so this distillation names its questions and outcomes but not every branch.

**Evidence-marker continuity.** The two blockquotes and every inline `[V]` fragment are copied from the deep reference's verified quotations (Introduction, Section 3 prose and Definitions only). Everything from Sections 1 and 2, the survey instruments, the just-culture and culpability material and the investigation steps is paraphrased with `[AP]` or `[AR]`, never quoted, because the handbook adapts it from INPO or other outside sources, or, for the investigation, just-culture and culpability material, because the deep reference paraphrases it as a conservative choice.
