# US DOE, Human Performance Improvement Handbook Vol. 1, Decision-Making Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Department of Energy (2009). *Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 1: Concepts and Principles*. DOE-HDBK-1028-2009. Distribution Statement A, approved for public release. **Licence:** Public domain (US Government work, 17 USC §105); DOE asks for acknowledgement in reuse. Open scope; no copyleft propagation. Third-party material inside the handbook (Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model, Mager and Pipe's gap analysis, INPO-derived lists, the Janis groupthink symptoms, and material adapted from Reason, Rasmussen, Senge and Hollnagel) is paraphrased here, never quoted.

## Decision-Making Relevance

The handbook is about human error in hazardous work, and its decision-making content sits in three places. First, it classifies decisions by how much the decider already knows. In its terms, a decision made where no rule or practised skill applies is "knowledge-based", a mode it says "might better be called “lack of knowledge” mode" [V] (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 1*, Ch 2, p. 2-25), where the error chance it cites is roughly one in two to one in ten and the prevalent failure is an inaccurate mental model (Ch 2, pp. 2-26 to 2-27). For that kind of work it calls for "collaboration with a small team of thoughtful, committed, and experienced individuals" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-27), and for problem-solving and diagnosis it adds agreeing the mental model as a team, taking time-outs, and challenging assumptions with a devil's advocate (Ch 2, pp. 2-28 to 2-29).

Second, it sets a rule for decisions under uncertainty where safety is at stake: conservative decision-making, defined as being "cautious and protective of what is truly important—safety, reliability, quality, security, and so on" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-30), with a burden of proof that runs toward stopping: "The bias is set on proving work activities are safe before proceeding, rather than proving them unsafe before halting" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-6). Third, it names the group dynamics that corrupt joint decisions (groupthink, the "risky shift" of diffusion of responsibility, deference to a senior person, the halo effect) and the production pressure that, unless safety is preserved as a core value, makes production the default value, a passage the handbook adapts from Hollnagel [BT] (Ch 4, p. 4-4, endnote 8). It fires across all six phases, most strongly in Exploring, Deciding and Monitoring.

## Key Concepts for Decision-Making

1.  **Know which mode the decision is in.** Skill-based actions are practised and automatic; rule-based decisions match a situation to a stored IF-THEN rule; knowledge-based decisions respond to a situation where no rule is recognisable (Ch 2, pp. 2-21 to 2-26). The handbook warns against assuming expert work is knowledge-based because experts do it: the mode "might better be called “lack of knowledge” mode" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-25). Its examples of knowledge-based activity include budget allocation, changing policies, planning business strategy and root cause analysis (Ch 2, p. 2-26).

2.  **Every mental model is partly wrong.** A mental model is "the structured understanding of knowledge (facts or assumptions) a person has in his or her mind about how something works or operates" [V], and "all mental models are inaccurate to some extent" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-28). In team problem-solving, members should agree the model they will use, and frequent time-outs keep it current (Ch 2, p. 2-28).

3.  **The knowledge-based error mode.** Problem-solvers tend to use only readily available information, "often become over-confident in the correctness of their knowledge; an “I know I’m right” effect" [V], and fix on one aspect of the problem (Ch 2, p. 2-26); "Consequently, the prevalent error mode is an inaccurate mental model of the system, process, or facility status" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-26).

4.  **Assumptions and danger words.** Assumptions reduce mental strain and help constrain a problem, but are too often treated as fact; phrases such as "I think", "We've always" and "I believe" signal them (Ch 2, p. 2-29). A devil's advocate can test them by naming the conclusion, asking for the data and the reasoning, inferring the underlying belief, and testing it with the person, a process the handbook credits to Senge [BT] (Ch 2, p. 2-29).

5.  **Mental shortcuts.** Confirmation, similarity, frequency, availability, representative and framing biases create order amid uncertainty and need watching during diagnosis and problem-solving (Ch 2, pp. 2-2, 2-29). It lists confirmation, similarity, frequency and availability bias among the mental shortcuts that avoiding mental strain produces [BT] (Ch 2, p. 2-2).

6.  **Conservative decisions.** Being conservative means being "cautious and protective of what is truly important—safety, reliability, quality, security, and so on" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-30). Factors the handbook draws from INPO documents include placing systems in a known safe condition when uncertain, seeking expertise, avoiding hasty decisions, rigorously evaluating alternatives, a deliberate decision with abort criteria, and not proceeding in the face of uncertainty [BT] (Ch 2, p. 2-30). DOE's safety-culture attributes add that "Workers are expected and authorized to take conservative actions when faced with unexpected or uncertain conditions" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-6).

7.  **Burden of proof toward stopping.** "The bias is set on proving work activities are safe before proceeding, rather than proving them unsafe before halting. Personnel do not proceed and do not allow others to proceed when safety is uncertain" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-6).

8.  **Group decision errors.** Groupthink (eight symptoms adapted from Janis) [BT]; diffusion of responsibility, which "often causes a “risky shift” in decision-making" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-15), so a group gambles more than its members would alone; pilot/co-pilot deference to a senior person; and the halo effect (Ch 2, pp. 2-13 to 2-15). Remedies include freedom of thought, challenging others' assumptions, a devil's advocate, timeouts, and an independent task preview before the briefing (Ch 2, p. 2-15).

9.  **Risk perception.** "People in general are poor judges of risk and commonly underestimate it" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-4). People weigh likelihood and neglect severity, and fear risks less when they feel in control, gain a benefit, chose the risk, are used to it, or trust its source [BT] (Ch 2, pp. 2-4 to 2-5).

10.  **Production against prevention.** "Production and prevention (error and event) practices always compete in the minds of workers" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-12). In a passage adapted from Hollnagel, the handbook holds that unless safety is preserved as a core value, managers end up unconsciously rewarding task completion and production becomes the default value [BT] (Ch 4, p. 4-4, endnote 8). "Production behaviors naturally take precedence over prevention behaviors unless there is a strong safety culture—nurtured by strong leadership" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-12).

11.  **Normalisation in management decisions.** The bald-tire attitude, "A belief that past performance is justification for not changing (improving) existing practices or conditions" [V], is illustrated by Columbia: after 22 years of foam strikes, programme managers were not unduly alarmed by a strike unlike any before, and "Learned attitudes about foam strikes diminished management’s wariness of their danger" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-6). The handbook notes that "Managers can be tempted to ignore recommendations for improvement if results have been good" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-6).

12.  **Decisions to change things are error-prone too.** The handbook notes that "schedule changes are a common contributor to facility events" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-24). Change fails most often without a plan that defines the problem, current and desired states, the values needed, responsibility, process, schedule, reinforcement and verification [BT] (Ch 3, p. 3-24).

## Questions to Ask During Decision-Making

### Phase 1: Framing (recognising what kind of decision is being made)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Which mode is this decision in? | Is there a practised routine or a known rule for this, or are we reasoning from first principles with no rule? If the latter, treat it as knowledge-based, whatever our expertise. |
| Is production pressure shaping the frame? | Is the decision being framed as "how do we keep to schedule" when it is also "is this safe to do"? Which value is the default here? |
| Is this a decision to change something? | Does the decision change crew, schedule, procedure or equipment? If so, is it being managed as a change with a plan? |

### Phase 2: Bounding (defining the decision space)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Where is the irreversible step? | Which step, if done wrong, will cause irreversible harm? Is it marked as the critical step? |
| Are we weighing severity as well as likelihood? | Have we asked how bad the outcome would be, not only how likely? |
| What would make us stop? | Have we set abort criteria before starting? |

### Phase 3: Exploring (generating alternatives)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Whose mental model are we using? | Has the group agreed on how the system works before diagnosing it? When did we last check that model against the facts? |
| Which statements are assumptions? | Where did someone say "I think", "We've always" or "I believe"? What data supports each, and what reasoning connects data to conclusion? |
| Who is arguing the other side? | Has a devil's advocate been named for this decision? |
| Which biases might be at work? | Are we sticking with the first solution because we invested in it (confirmation), reaching for what worked in a similar-looking case (similarity), or for what comes to mind first (availability)? |
| Who else knows this system? | Have we sought prompt help from people with relevant expertise? |

### Phase 4: Deciding (analysing and selecting)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Is safety proven, or only unchallenged? | Are we requiring proof that proceeding is safe, or waiting for proof that it is unsafe before we stop? |
| Is there uncertainty left? | If safety is uncertain, can the system be put in a known safe condition while we resolve it? |
| Is the group taking a risk none of us would take alone? | Would each person here make this call alone? If not, is diffusion of responsibility at work? |
| Is anyone deferring? | Has the most junior person said what they think? Is anyone holding back because of rank or reputation? |
| Is the decision hurried? | Are we rushing because of the clock, and does the clock outrank the risk? |

### Phase 5: Ratifying (committing to and communicating the decision)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Is the direction clear? | Does the decision state roles and responsibilities, contingencies and abort criteria? |
| Do the people carrying it out know they may stop? | Has it been said that anyone may stop the work if conditions turn unexpected or uncertain? |
| What does our reaction signal? | What will the way we decide, and how we react if it goes wrong, teach people about what we value? |

### Phase 6: Monitoring (post-decision review)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Has the deviation become normal? | Is something happening every time without harm, and have we stopped treating it as a warning? |
| Are good results being used to avoid change? | Are recommendations being set aside because the numbers look good? |
| Are we hearing weak signals? | What small anomalies have we seen since the decision, and did they get a strong enough response? |
| Did the change hold? | Was the change verified as successful, with new behaviours reinforced? |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| "We're experts, we know this system" on a first-of-a-kind problem | Mistaking expertise for a rule; knowledge-based mode (Ch 2, p. 2-25) | Name it as a no-rule decision; agree the mental model; slow down |
| The team keeps reinterpreting new data to fit the first diagnosis | Confirmation bias (Ch 2, p. 2-2) | Ask what evidence would change the diagnosis; assign a devil's advocate |
| The plan goes ahead because no one has shown it is unsafe | Burden of proof running the wrong way (Ch 4, p. 4-6) | Ask for the case that it is safe; if uncertain, move to a known safe state |
| The group is bolder than any individual in it | Risky shift from diffusion of responsibility (Ch 2, p. 2-15) | Ask each person privately whether they would make the call alone |
| Junior people agree with the senior person and say no more | Pilot/co-pilot deference or groupthink (Ch 2, pp. 2-13 to 2-14) | Hear juniors first; ask for doubts explicitly |
| "It's happened every time and nothing went wrong" | Bald-tire normalisation (Ch 2, p. 2-6) | Treat the repetition as the warning; review how close past occurrences came |
| A schedule change is approved without discussion | Change unmanaged (Ch 3, p. 3-24) | Run the change through a short plan: problem, desired state, owner, verification |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- A decision with safety, reliability or quality at stake must be made under uncertainty, and the group needs a rule for when to stop.
- A team of experts faces a problem none of their rules cover.
- A group decision feels bolder, faster or more unanimous than it should.
- Good recent results are being used to argue against changing a practice.
- A schedule, crew or procedure change is being decided casually.

Pair it with OpenStax Organizational Behavior for general decision-bias theory and with MCDP 1 for decisions where delay itself is the danger; the handbook's conservative bias is written for hazardous operations and does not address cases where waiting carries the greater risk.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored. A laboratory group is midway through a two-day experiment when a sensor reading drifts outside the expected band. The principal investigator, keen to finish before a funding review, proposes carrying on: "the sensor has drifted before and it was always noise." Two postdocs agree at once; a technician says nothing.

**Framing.** The facilitator asks whether any of them has a rule for this reading. No one does, so the group is in what the handbook calls knowledge-based mode, which it says "might better be called “lack of knowledge” mode" [V] (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 1*, Ch 2, p. 2-25), where the prevalent error is an inaccurate mental model (Ch 2, p. 2-26).

**Exploring.** "It was always noise" is a danger-word assumption and a bald-tire belief, of the kind the handbook defines as:

> A belief that past performance is justification for not changing (improving) existing practices or conditions [V] (Source: Ch 2, p. 2-6)

The facilitator asks for the data behind it. Earlier drifts were in a different direction. The technician, asked directly, says the cooling line was serviced that morning.

**Deciding.** The group notices it is more willing to continue together than any member says they would be alone, the "risky shift" the handbook ties to diffusion of responsibility (Ch 2, p. 2-15). The facilitator reads the handbook's burden-of-proof rule:

> The bias is set on proving work activities are safe before proceeding, rather than proving them unsafe before halting. Personnel do not proceed and do not allow others to proceed when safety is uncertain. [V] (Source: Ch 4, p. 4-6)

They pause the run in a known safe state, check the cooling line, and set an abort criterion for the restart. In this scenario the check finds a loose fitting and the experiment resumes the next morning; the funding review gets an honest one-day delay.

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Expertise as a rule.** Signal: an expert team treats a novel problem as routine. Diagnosis: knowledge-based mode unrecognised (Ch 2, p. 2-25). Follow-up: name the mode, agree the model, slow down.
- **Proving it unsafe before stopping.** Signal: work continues because no one has shown harm. Diagnosis: reversed burden of proof (Ch 4, p. 4-6). Follow-up: require the case for safety.
- **The risky shift.** Signal: a group accepts a risk no member would accept alone. Diagnosis: diffusion of responsibility (Ch 2, p. 2-15). Follow-up: individual check before group commit.
- **Silent juniors.** Signal: the most junior person has not spoken. Diagnosis: deference and groupthink (Ch 2, pp. 2-13 to 2-14). Follow-up: hear them first.
- **Production by default.** Signal: schedule framing dominates and safety is assumed. Diagnosis: production as the default core value (Ch 4, p. 4-4). Follow-up: state the safety question explicitly in the decision.
- **Success as a reason not to change.** Signal: recommendations dismissed because results are good. Diagnosis: bald-tire attitude, the Columbia pattern (Ch 2, p. 2-6). Follow-up: review the near misses behind the good results.
- **Unmanaged change.** Signal: a schedule or crew change is waved through. Diagnosis: schedule changes commonly contribute to events (Ch 3, p. 3-24). Follow-up: give the change a plan and a verification step.
