# OpenStax Introduction to Business, Decision-Making Distillation

**Source:** OpenStax (2018). *Introduction to Business*. Rice University. CC BY 4.0. https://openstax.org/details/books/introduction-business. Lead author Lawrence J. Gitman; six senior contributing authors.

## Decision-Making Relevance

OpenStax's Introduction to Business treats decision-making as a core managerial activity threaded across functional areas. Ch 6 ("Management and Leadership in Today's Organizations") names decision-making as one of the manager's key roles and distinguishes programmed (routine, rule-amenable) from non-programmed (novel, ambiguous) decisions, anchoring the five-step decision process (recognise, gather information, select, implement, gather feedback), which the text says managers follow whether a decision is programmed or non-programmed. Ch 6.8 frames crisis-management decision-making with worked guidance from named CEOs responding to Hurricane Katrina.

Beyond Ch 6, decision-making recurs across the survey: Ch 2 frames ethical decision-making with the feelings test and newspaper/social-media test; Ch 7 discusses centralisation vs decentralisation as a decision about who decides; Ch 9 covers motivation theories that shape how employees decide whether to exert effort (expectancy, equity, goal-setting); Ch 10 develops production-control decisions through Gantt charts, CPM, and PERT; Ch 14 frames financial decisions through ratio analysis; Ch 16 develops the risk-return trade-off as the core financial-decision principle; and Ch 17.3 sets out a six-step planning process (set a goal, acquire knowledge, compare alternatives, choose a strategy, make a commitment, stay flexible) for career planning.

This distillation consolidates these threads: how to recognise decision type and choose the right process, how to avoid getting stuck at a stage (analysis paralysis), how to evaluate alternatives systematically when stakes are high, and how to handle crisis-mode decisions when contingency planning is incomplete.

## Key Concepts for Decision-Making

1.  **Programmed vs non-programmed decisions.** Programmed decisions respond to routine situations that occur frequently across the organisation and are handled by standard procedures (the text's example is recruitment and selection when hiring). Non-programmed decisions answer infrequent, unforeseen, or unusual problems and opportunities, where the manager lacks a precedent. The text says managers follow the same five steps for both: recognise/define the problem; gather information; select alternatives; act; gather feedback. (Source: OpenStax, *Introduction to Business*, Ch 6.6, "Managerial Decision Making")

2.  **Risk-return trade-off.** The source states the principle directly: "The higher the risk, the greater the return that is required" [V] (Source: OpenStax, *Introduction to Business*, Ch 16.1, "The Role of Finance and the Financial Manager"). Financial managers weigh risk and return factors when making investment and financing decisions. Applied beyond finance (this distillation's extension; the source's broader point, in Ch 1.1, is that greater business risk carries greater potential profit or loss), the cost of new product development, market entry, or technology investment must be weighed against the magnitude and likelihood of benefit.

3.  **Five-step decision-making process.** Presented in Ch 6.6 as the steps managers typically follow for both programmed and non-programmed decisions: (1) recognise or define the problem or opportunity; (2) gather information to identify alternative solutions or actions; (3) select one or more alternatives after evaluating strengths and weaknesses; (4) put the chosen alternative into action; (5) gather information to obtain feedback on effectiveness. The text warns: "It can be easy (and dangerous) for managers to get stuck at any stage of the decision-making process," [V] citing analysis paralysis. (Source: Ch 6.6, Exhibit 6.7)

4.  **Four planning types ordered by horizon.** Strategic (1-5 years; top management; broad/general; high uncertainty); tactical (less than 1 year; middle management; more specific; moderate certainty); operational (current; supervisory; specific/concrete; reasonable certainty); contingency (alternative paths for crises). (Source: Ch 6.2, Table 6.2)

5.  **Environmental forces require continuous scanning.** The source's business environment has seven sub-environments (economic, political and legal, demographic, social, competitive, global, technological), and it argues managerial control over them is limited: "managers are primarily adapters to, rather than agents of, change" [V] (Source: Ch 1.2, "Understanding the Business Environment"). Ch 11.2 names six forces marketers scan (cultural/social, demographic, economic, technological, political/legal, competitive); *environmental scanning* is the team-based process of continually collecting and evaluating that information, with the goal of identifying current and future market opportunities and threats. (Ch 11.2)

6.  **Two ethical-decision tests.** The text runs them after three checks: does the action break a law, does it violate the company's code of ethics, does it meet one's own ethical philosophy? The *feelings test* asks how the decision makes you feel; discomfort that lingers after deciding (a loss of sleep or appetite) is a feeling of conscience that can guide future ethical dilemmas. The *newspaper or social-media test*: how would an objective reporter describe the decision on the front page? The text says "This test is helpful in spotting and resolving potential conflicts of interest" [V]. (Source: Ch 2.2, "Making the Right Decision")

7.  **The pyramid of corporate social responsibility for stakeholder-affecting decisions.** Four responsibilities stack from the foundation upward: economic responsibility (without profit, the others don't matter), legal responsibility (obey the law), ethical responsibility (do what is right, just, and fair), philanthropic responsibility (be a good corporate citizen). All four must be considered for any decision affecting stakeholders. (Source: Ch 2.3, Exhibit 2.4)

8.  **Make-or-buy decision criteria.** Quantity needed (a part used in only one of many products may be more cost-effective to buy); secrecy (design features that must be kept secret to protect a competitive advantage favour making); whether outside suppliers can deliver high quality reliably. (Source: Ch 10.4, "Make or Buy?") Applied beyond manufacturing parts, the same frame covers outsourcing services and in-house vs vendor IT.

9.  **Factors shaping the choice between mechanistic and organic structure.** Strategy (innovation favours organic; cost-control favours mechanistic), size (larger firms tend mechanistic), and the external environment (complex/unstable favours organic), among others. The structure decision shapes how quickly and where decisions get made. (Source: Ch 7.6)

10.  **Expectancy theory for effort decisions.** Per the source, the probability of an individual acting in a particular way "depends on the strength of that individual's belief that the act will have a particular outcome and on whether the individual values that outcome" [V] (Source: Ch 9.6, "Expectancy Theory"). Decisions depend on three relationships: effort-to-performance (will my effort produce performance?); performance-to-outcome (will performance produce the outcome?); outcomes-to-individual-needs (do I value the outcome?). A practical implication, developed in OpenStax Principles of Management (Ch 14.3) rather than in this source, is to make outcomes contingent on performance.

11.  **Equity theory in distribution decisions.** When designing reward systems or compensation structures, equity theory predicts that workers compare their outcome-input ratio to a referent's. Perceived inequity triggers restorative behaviours: changed work habits (less effort), asking for a raise, distorted perceptions of self or others, reframing, or leaving. The text's implication for managers: understand employees' perceptions of fairness and take steps to reduce concerns about inequity. (Source: Ch 9.6, "Equity Theory")

12.  **Critical Path Method and PERT for project decisions.** For complex projects with many interdependent tasks, CPM identifies the longest path through linked activities; PERT extends with three time estimates (optimistic, most probable, pessimistic) to anticipate delays. The decision: where to invest schedule-protection effort. Tasks on the critical path get priority. (Source: Ch 10.5)

## Questions to Ask During Decision-Making

### Phase 1: Framing (Recognising the decision and gathering context)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Identify the decision type | Is this a programmed (routine, rule-amenable) decision or a non-programmed (novel, ambiguous) one? Programmed decisions can lean on standard procedures; non-programmed ones have little precedent. Either way, the text's five steps apply. |
| Choose the planning horizon | Is this strategic (1-5 years; broad), tactical (less than 1 year; specific), operational (current), or contingency (crisis-alternative)? |
| Surface ethical implications | Does this decision affect stakeholders without voice? Whose welfare is at stake? Does it pass the feelings test and the newspaper/social-media test? |
| Identify environmental forces | Which of the six environmental forces (cultural/social, demographic, economic, technological, political/legal, competitive) most shape this decision? |
| Detect crisis vs business-as-usual | Is this happening within a contingency plan, or beyond what was planned? If beyond, who has the authority to make non-programmed decisions in real time? |

### Phase 2: Bounding (Defining the decision and its constraints)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Clarify what is being decided | What is the decision actually about? What is in scope and what is out? |
| Identify stakeholders affected | Who has standing — employees, customers, society (including environmental and community), investors, regulators? Whose interests should be considered even if they have not yet asserted themselves? |
| Identify time and resource constraints | How long can I take? What resources can I draw on? |
| Identify regulatory and legal constraints | Are there laws, antitrust rules, consumer protection laws, contract terms, or other legal constraints that bound the decision space? |
| Surface ethical constraints | What four-tier responsibility frame (economic, legal, ethical, philanthropic) applies? Where are the deontological lines? |

### Phase 3: Exploring (Generating alternatives)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Generate enough options | Have I generated three to five real alternatives, or stopped at two? |
| Engage diverse perspectives | Have I sought out colleagues with different functional backgrounds (operations, marketing, finance, HR, legal)? Survey-textbook structure suggests every functional area has a stake. |
| Test the make-or-buy frame | If the decision involves capability — internal vs external? Quantity, secrecy (to protect a competitive advantage), and supplier reliability and quality. |
| Consider evidence-based methods | What internal data, industry benchmarks, or external research bear on the decision? |
| Account for cross-cultural variation | If the decision affects employees across cultures, has cultural variation been considered (English-speaking individualism vs Northern European personal-life priorities, etc.)? |

### Phase 4: Deciding (Analysing and selecting)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Apply the risk-return trade-off | What is the magnitude of return? What is the magnitude of risk? Does the return justify the risk given the firm's risk appetite? |
| Use ratio analysis where applicable | For financial decisions, what do liquidity, profitability, activity, and debt ratios suggest about the firm's capacity to absorb risk? |
| Test for groupthink | Is the decision being endorsed by consensus that may suppress dissent? Have I asked the room "what evidence would change our view?" (See OpenStax Principles of Management, Ch 2.6, for groupthink.) |
| Test for analysis paralysis | Have I gathered enough information? Am I delaying past the point of useful additional information? |
| Apply the two ethical tests | Feelings test: how does the decision feel? Newspaper/social-media test: how would it look on the front page? |

### Phase 5: Ratifying (Implementing the decision)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Determine whose ratification is required | Board-level? Senior management? Stakeholder consultation? |
| Communicate clearly | Have I communicated the reasoning, not just the outcome, to those affected? Especially for stakeholder decisions, the *process* matters as much as the result. |
| Address conflict from the decision | Some decisions create unhappy stakeholders. Have I prepared for conflict productively rather than avoiding it? |
| Preserve unity of command | If implementing through line organisation, are reporting relationships clear? Avoid matrix-style ambiguity. |

### Phase 6: Monitoring (Evaluating effectiveness)

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| Define monitoring metrics | What indicators will tell me whether the decision is working? Are these specific, measurable, and time-bound? |
| Distinguish bad luck from bad decision | A bad outcome may follow a sound process if uncertainty was high. Am I evaluating *process* as well as outcome? |
| Be willing to reverse | If new information shows the decision was wrong, am I able to reverse course? (Step 5 of the text's process is gathering feedback on the chosen plan's effectiveness; OpenStax Principles of Management, Ch 2.4, names the trap of escalation of commitment.) |
| Capture learnings | What did the process teach? Build the lesson into the next decision of this type. |

## What to Look For

- **Pattern: Decision-makers cannot articulate why they need to decide now.** Signal: pressure to decide without clear bounding. Diagnosis (in source vocabulary): the decision lacks framing — the "right" planning horizon hasn't been chosen. Follow-up: identify whether the decision is strategic, tactical, operational, or contingency-driven; bound the scope before proceeding.
- **Pattern: Crisis response is improvised because contingency plans are inadequate.** Signal: confusion about chain of command; non-programmed decisions made under high pressure. Diagnosis: contingency planning gap. Follow-up: empower local decision-makers (Norfolk Southern model) to make non-programmed decisions; conduct a postmortem after the crisis to inform the next round.
- **Pattern: Sales/effort decisions are misaligned with reward structure.** Signal: employees not exerting effort despite stated incentives. Diagnosis: failure of effort-performance, performance-outcome, or outcome-needs link in expectancy theory. Follow-up: check each link explicitly; redesign reward to make outcomes contingent on performance.
- **Pattern: Reward decisions provoke complaints despite seemingly fair distribution.** Signal: high performers complain; turnover rises. Diagnosis: equity theory at work — workers comparing outcome-input ratio to referents. Follow-up: find out how employees perceive fairness and which referent they compare against; take steps to reduce concerns about inequity (Ch 9.6).
- **Pattern: Project schedules consistently slip.** Signal: late deliverables; quality compromises to meet deadlines. Diagnosis: critical-path tasks unprotected; PERT/CPM not in use. Follow-up: identify critical-path tasks; protect them with buffer; use PERT three-estimate technique for complex projects.
- **Pattern: Manager defends a decision that early evidence suggests was wrong.** Signal: more resources committed despite signals to the contrary. Diagnosis: step 5 of the text's process (gathering feedback on the chosen plan's effectiveness) is not being acted on; OpenStax Principles of Management (Ch 2.4) names this pattern escalation of commitment. Follow-up: precommit to monitoring metrics; commit in advance to act on what the evidence shows.
- **Pattern: Ethical concerns are framed as compliance questions only.** Signal: discussion focuses on "is this legal" rather than "is this right." Diagnosis: the CSR pyramid being collapsed — legal responsibility being treated as ethical responsibility. Follow-up: separate the four CSR levels explicitly; apply the feelings test and newspaper test.
- **Pattern: Cross-cultural team's commitment to a decision differs by region.** Signal: same decision generates enthusiastic uptake in one region, foot-dragging in another. Diagnosis: cultural variation in motivation drivers (e.g., English-speaking countries' emphasis on individual achievement vs Northern European emphasis on job accomplishment and protecting personal life). Follow-up: tailor the rationale and communication to cultural context; use Adler/Gunderson framework as orientation.

## When to Use This Reference

- A non-programmed decision is approaching and the manager wants to use the systematic five-step process.
- A crisis is unfolding without a contingency plan; non-programmed decisions must be made in real time.
- A capital expenditure or major investment requires risk-return analysis.
- A reward structure or compensation system is being designed and expectancy/equity considerations matter.
- A project schedule is at risk; CPM/PERT logic should guide where protection is invested.
- An ethical dimension to a decision is unclear and needs structured consideration.
- An organisation is choosing between mechanistic and organic structural design.
- A career or life-planning decision is being made and the six-step planning process applies.

## Worked Example

A regional pizza chain owner must decide whether to invest in an automated pizza-making robot for one of its 30 stores. The robot costs $600,000, requires 18 months of integration, and will reduce per-pizza labor cost by ~30%. Local labor markets are tight, but the robot risks customer pushback (the chain's brand emphasises "hand-crafted").

Framing: This is a non-programmed strategic decision (1-5 year horizon). The owner uses the five-step process rather than improvising. Six environmental forces in play: technological (robot is new and improving), economic (tight labor market raises costs), competitive (national chains automating), social (consumer attitudes toward automation in food), demographic (younger customers more accepting of automation), legal (food safety regulation).

Bounding: The decision is whether to invest in *one store as a pilot* — not to automate the chain. Ethical considerations: employees affected by job displacement; customers' expectation of quality. The CSR pyramid: economic responsibility favors the investment if ROI is sufficient; ethical responsibility raises the displacement question.

Exploring: The owner generates four alternatives: (1) install the robot in one store as a pilot; (2) wait two years for the technology to mature; (3) negotiate higher wages and retain hand-crafted positioning; (4) outsource pizza production to a centralised commissary serving all 30 stores. The owner consults operations head, marketing head, finance head, and HR head — engaging diverse functional perspectives.

Deciding: Risk-return analysis: $600,000 investment with 18-month payback period if labor savings materialize and customer experience holds; risk if customers reject the automated pizza (~25% probability of meaningful brand impact, per the chain's market research). Make-or-buy decision applied: the chain considers whether any competitive advantage in the technology needs protecting in-house (the text's secrecy criterion) and whether an outside supplier can support it reliably. The two ethical tests are applied: the feelings test surfaces the founder's discomfort about employee displacement; the newspaper test asks how a story about robot pizza vs hand-crafted positioning would land.

Selection: The owner chooses option (1) — pilot in one store — with explicit success criteria: maintained customer satisfaction at 4.0/5.0 or above; payback within 24 months; no more than 3 jobs eliminated per store (workers offered transfer to other stores). Communication plan ensures employees in the pilot store know the criteria and timeline.

Implementation: Pilot launched. Weekly reviews track customer satisfaction, throughput, and employee transition.

Monitoring: At 18 months, customer satisfaction has dropped to 3.7/5.0 — below the 4.0 threshold. Despite favorable financials, the owner reverses the decision and removes the robot, citing the precommitted monitoring criteria.

This worked example illustrates the five-step process (including step 5, gathering feedback), the risk-return trade-off, the make-or-buy frame, both ethical tests, and precommitting to monitoring criteria so the decision can be reversed on evidence.

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Treating a novel situation as routine.** Applying standard procedures where there is no precedent (the text's non-programmed case) skips the information-gathering and evaluation steps the situation needs.
- **Treating ethical questions as compliance questions only.** Reduced moral attention produces decisions that are technically legal but harmful to stakeholders.
- **Centralisation overdone.** Centralisation can prevent quick local decisions in dynamic environments.
- **Decentralisation without skills or training.** Pushing authority down without ensuring competence can produce costly mistakes.
- **Cost-cutting that erodes service differentiation.** The text warns that cost advantages "are subject to continual erosion" [V] as competitors adopt the same technology or suppliers, while differentiation, including service, lasts longer; with services over 80% of U.S. GDP, cutting service quality trades a durable advantage for a fragile one.
- **Ignoring the equity-theory dimension of pay decisions.** Employees who perceive inequity may exert less effort, ask for a raise, distort their perceptions, or leave (Ch 9.6).
- **Failing to monitor.** Without precommitted monitoring metrics, decisions cannot be evaluated and learning is lost.
