# U.S. Marine Corps, MCDP 1: Warfighting — Stakeholder-Engagement Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Marine Corps (Krulak, Charles C., Commandant), *MCDP 1: Warfighting*. Headquarters United States Marine Corps, Department of the Navy, 1997. PCN 142 000006 00. Supersedes FMFM 1 *Warfighting* (1989). US Government public-domain doctrinal publication; freely distributable. **Scope:** open.

## Stakeholder-Engagement Relevance

MCDP 1 is the Marine Corps' statement of how it fights and prepares to fight. Its parties are seniors, subordinates, peers inside one command, and an enemy. It never uses the word *stakeholder* and offers no theory of who has standing in a decision. It defines the essence of war as a violent struggle between two hostile, independent and irreconcilable wills, each trying to impose itself on the other (Ch 1, "War defined") [AP], and it calls war "fundamentally an interactive social process" [V] (Ch 1, "War defined"). A projection onto stakeholder engagement therefore has to be narrower than the axis, and this one is.

The doctrine lands in two engagement settings. The first is engagement *inside a line of authority*: a leader asking others to act, the people who take on that work, and peers who must coordinate without a central controller. Here MCDP 1 is specific about how the parties should talk. The senior states intent as well as task (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"); the mission is a two-way contract (Ch 4, "Mission tactics"); juniors owe honest, professional opinions, disagreement included, until a decision is stated, and support once it is (Ch 3, "Professionalism"); trust is earned and runs both ways (Ch 3, "Professionalism"); key people talk directly, in person, inside long working relationships (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command"); and the review after training, which the doctrine calls a *critique*, is held as open dialogue in which all hands contribute (Ch 3, "Training"). Holding such a review after an engagement is this distillation's application. The second setting is engagement with *a party whose aims run against yours*. The doctrine's discipline of orienting on the enemy (understanding the other side on its own terms and refusing to assume it shares your values or objectives) transfers to an opposing counterparty (Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy").

For cooperative external stakeholders (customers, communities, partners, regulators acting in good faith) the doctrine speaks only by analogy. Where this distillation moves a doctrinal idea from commanders and enemies to leads, teams and stakeholders, it says so, and the move is the distillation's, not the doctrine's. The doctrine's adversarial machinery (tempo against an enemy, surfaces and gaps, centers of gravity, critical vulnerabilities, surprise) presupposes an opposing will; this distillation does not carry it into cooperative engagement. What it carries is the command-climate material a stakeholder-engagement practitioner can use on their own side of the table, and the orienting discipline for the other side when that side is genuinely opposed.

## Key Concepts for Stakeholder-Engagement

1.  **Intent over task.** Every mission has two parts, the task to be accomplished and the intent behind it; the task says what is to be done, and the intent explains why [AP]. The doctrine ranks them: "Of the two, the intent is predominant." [V] The intent outlasts a task that events have made obsolete (the full passage appears in the Worked Example). Its compact form is the *in order to* statement ("Control the bridge in order to prevent the enemy from escaping across the river." [V]), and a statement of intent should be brief: "the more concise, the better" [V]. For engagement, the intent is what a party can still act on after the plan has gone stale. (Source: U.S. Marine Corps, *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Commander's intent")

2.  **The burden of understanding runs both ways.** "The burden of understanding falls on senior and subordinate alike." [V] Seniors must make their purposes clear without inhibiting initiative; subordinates must understand what their commander expects, and "should understand the intent of the commander at least two levels up" [V]. The duty to ask is explicit: "A subordinate commander who is not given a clear purpose for the assigned mission should ask for one." [V] (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Commander's intent")

3.  **The mission as a contract.** Mission tactics assigns a mission without specifying how it must be accomplished, and "relies on a subordinate's exercise of initiative framed by proper guidance and understanding" [V]. The senior still sets limits: "The senior prescribes the method of execution only to the degree that is essential for coordination. The senior intervenes in a subordinate's execution only by exception." [V] "Mission tactics serves as a contract between senior and subordinate." [V] The senior agrees to provide support and the guidance that allows proper judgment without unnecessarily prescribing actions; the subordinate agrees to act in conformity with the senior's intent, responsibly and loyally, within the proper limits of authority [AP]. Subordinates adapt as the situation changes: "They inform the commander of what they have done, but they do not wait for permission." [V] The doctrine's own reason for decentralising is to generate tempo and to cope with the uncertainty, disorder and fluidity of combat (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command") [AP]; this distillation uses the senior–subordinate relationship the doctrine describes, not that combat rationale. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Mission tactics")

4.  **Unity through lateral coordination, not imposed control.** Decentralised initiative needs a source of unity, and the doctrine finds it sideways: "We seek unity not principally through imposed control, but through harmonious initiative and lateral coordination within the context provided by guidance from above." [V] Elsewhere it holds that "Unity of effort is as important during the preparation for war as it is during the conduct of war." [V] (Ch 3, "Force planning"). For multi-party engagement inside one organisation, peers coordinating with each other inside a shared intent is the doctrine's alternative to routing every question through the centre. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Mission tactics")

5.  **Honest dissent before the decision, support after it.** Until a commander has reached and stated a decision, subordinates owe honest, professional opinions even where they disagree with the senior [AP]; once the decision is reached, juniors "must support it as if it were their own" [V]. The doctrine closes the loophole on the other side: "Ready compliance for the purpose of personal advancement — the behavior of 'yes-men' — will not be tolerated." [V] This is the doctrine's clearest rule for surfacing and then settling disagreement. The full passage appears in the Worked Example. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 3, "Professionalism")

6.  **Trust is earned and runs both ways.** Trust is foundational in the doctrine, held by seniors in the abilities of their subordinates and by juniors in the competence and support of their seniors [AP]. "Trust must be earned, and actions which undermine trust must meet with strict censure. Trust is a product of confidence and familiarity." [V] (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). Ch 4 returns to it: "Perhaps most important, our philosophy demands confidence among seniors and subordinates." [V] (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command")

7.  **Implicit communication through long working relationships.** The doctrine prefers "implicit communication — to communicate through mutual understanding, using a minimum of key, well-understood phrases or even anticipating each other's thoughts" [V] to detailed, explicit instructions, as faster and more effective [AP]. It spells out four practical implications: long-term working relationships; key people (the *actuals*) talking directly to one another; communicating orally; and communicating in person [AP]. Ch 3 ties the same ability to stable personnel, which fosters "cohesion, teamwork, and implicit understanding" [V] (Ch 3, "Personnel management"). The Notes cite John Boyd for the idea of implicit communication (Notes 5 of Ch 4). (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Philosophy of command")

8.  **Orienting on the other party.** Maneuver warfare means "focusing outward on the particular characteristics of the enemy rather than inward on the mechanical execution of predetermined procedures" [V]. The discipline is to understand the enemy on its own terms and to refuse to assume it shares one's thinking, values or objectives; the doctrine's stated purpose is to "see the enemy as he sees himself so that we can set him up for defeat" [V]. The underlying premise is from Ch 1: the enemy is "an independent and animate force with its own objectives and plans" [V] (Ch 1, "War defined"). The distillation uses the orienting discipline for understanding an opposing party, not the purpose the doctrine attaches to it. The full passage appears in the Worked Example. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy")

9.  **Main effort ranks your own actions, not the parties.** "Of all the actions going on within our command, we recognize one as the most critical to success at that moment." [V] The unit assigned that mission is the main effort and receives priority for support of any kind; the harmonising question for every subordinate facing a decision is "How can I best support the main effort?" [V]. The commitment is real but reversible: "The main effort involves a physical and moral commitment, although not an irretrievable one." [V] When it shifts, "we seek to exploit success rather than reinforce failure" [V]. By the distillation's analogy, the concept applies to an engagement lead's own workstreams; stakeholders are not units under command and are not ranked by it. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 4, "Main effort")

10.  **The critique: open dialogue about why.** MCDP 1's word for its review practice is *critique*; it does not use the term after-action review. The load-bearing passage:

    > "Critiques should be held in an atmosphere of open and frank dialogue in which all hands are encouraged to contribute. We learn as much from mistakes as from things done well, so we must be willing to admit mistakes and discuss them. Of course, a subordinate's willingness to admit mistakes depends on the commander's willingness to tolerate them. Because we recognize that no two situations in war are the same, our critiques should focus not so much on the actions we took as on why we took those actions and why they brought the results they did." [V] (Ch 3, "Training")

    Critiques follow success as well as failure, and timing matters: "we should conduct critiques immediately after completing training, before memory of the events has faded" [V]. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 3, "Training")

11.  **Leniency for overbold errors, severity for inaction.** "errors by junior leaders stemming from overboldness are a necessary part of learning" [V], and "there must be no 'zero defects' mentality" [V]; on the other hand "we should deal severely with errors of inaction or timidity" [V], and "We will not accept lack of orders as justification for inaction" [V]. The leniency is bounded: commanders still counsel on mistakes, since "constructive criticism is an important element in learning" [V], and it does not "give subordinates free license to act stupidly or recklessly" [V]. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Ch 3, "Professionalism")

12.  **Guidance that requires judgment.** The Foreword: "This book contains no specific techniques or procedures for conduct. Rather, it provides broad guidance in the form of concepts and values. It requires judgment in application." [V] Ch 3 says the same of doctrine generally: "Our doctrine does not consist of procedures to be applied in specific situations so much as it sets forth general guidance that requires judgment in application. Therefore, while authoritative, doctrine is not prescriptive." [V] (Ch 3, "Doctrine"). This is the stance in which the rest of this distillation should be read: none of it is an engagement procedure. (Source: *MCDP 1*, Foreword; Ch 3, "Doctrine")

## What to Look For

**Signal:** Parties carry out the agreed tasks faithfully after circumstances have changed, even though the tasks no longer serve the purpose.
**Diagnosis:** The engagement handed over tasks without intent. The doctrine's reason for ranking intent above task is exactly this case: a changed situation can make the task obsolete, while "the intent is more lasting and continues to guide our actions" [V] (Ch 4, "Commander's intent").
**Follow-up:** Restate the purpose in an *in order to* form and give it to everyone acting, at least two levels down. Tell them that if the purpose is unclear they should ask for it, which is the doctrine's own expectation of a subordinate (Ch 4, "Commander's intent").

**Signal:** Meetings end in apparent agreement, and the decision is then quietly worked around or reopened.
**Diagnosis:** Disagreement was not drawn out before the decision, or the point at which argument ends was never stated. MCDP 1 pairs a duty of honest, professional opinion before the decision with a duty of support after it, and refuses the agreement-for-advancement pattern of *yes-men* outright (Ch 3, "Professionalism") [AP].
**Follow-up:** Make the two halves explicit: open the floor for disagreement, state the decision in so many words, then ask for support as if it were each person's own. Watch for the person who never disagrees.

**Signal:** Messages between two parties pass through several intermediaries and written summaries, and each side keeps misreading the other.
**Diagnosis:** The parties lack the mutual understanding the doctrine calls implicit communication, which it builds through long-term working relationships and through key people talking directly, orally and in person (Ch 4, "Philosophy of command") [AP].
**Follow-up:** Put the people who hold the decisions in direct contact, and keep them working together across engagements rather than assembling a new cast each time.

**Signal:** The plan for engaging an opposing party assumes it will react as we would, and its moves keep surprising us.
**Diagnosis:** Nobody has oriented on the party. The doctrine's rule is not to assume the other side thinks as we do or has the same values or objectives (Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy") [AP].
**Follow-up:** Build the picture from the other party's side: what it wants, how it decides, what it expects from us. Use this for understanding. The doctrine's purpose for orienting is to set an enemy up for defeat; carrying that purpose into stakeholder engagement is the anti-pattern below, not this follow-up.

**Signal:** The review after an engagement turns into a list of what went wrong, and junior people stay silent.
**Diagnosis:** The review climate does not tolerate admitted mistakes. The doctrine says plainly that "a subordinate's willingness to admit mistakes depends on the commander's willingness to tolerate them" [V] (Ch 3, "Training").
**Follow-up:** Have the most senior person open with a mistake of their own, invite everyone to contribute, and ask why actions were taken and why they led where they did.

**Signal:** The engagement lead pushes for speed at every step and treats any pause as ground lost.
**Diagnosis:** Tempo has been carried over without the premise that makes it a weapon. MCDP 1 weights tempo heavily ("Tempo is itself a weapon — often the most important." [V], Ch 2, "Styles of warfare"), but the speed it values is speed relative to an enemy, and it also counts a "nervy, calculating patience" [V] as a form of boldness (Ch 2, "Surprise and boldness") and warns against rushing decisions when time is not a limiting factor (Ch 4, "Decisionmaking") [AP].
**Follow-up:** Ask whether time is actually a limiting factor in this engagement. Many stakeholder situations call for the patience clause more than for speed.

## When to Use This Reference

- **Handing engagement work to others.** A lead is asking teams, workstream owners or partners to act on their behalf and needs to give purpose rather than a script: intent over task, the mission as a two-way contract, method specified only as far as coordination needs.
- **A leadership group whose decisions keep being reopened.** Agreement in the room does not hold outside it, or nobody disagrees with the most senior person. MCDP 1's dissent-before, support-after rule and its refusal of yes-men say when disagreement is due and when it ends; the Open Practice Library's Disagree and Commit is the team-practice counterpart.
- **Peers who must coordinate without a central controller.** Several teams or departments share an outcome and each is waiting on the centre; the doctrine's unity through lateral coordination inside shared intent is the frame.
- **Engaging a party whose aims oppose yours.** A counterparty in a dispute, an organised opposition to a change: the orienting discipline (understand the party on its own terms; do not assume shared values or objectives) applies, with the doctrine's purpose of defeating the other side left behind.
- **Designing the review after an engagement.** When the aim is candour about mistakes, the critique passage (open dialogue, all hands contribute, focus on why, the senior's tolerance sets the ceiling) sets the climate.

Reach elsewhere when the question is who has standing or how to weigh competing stakeholder claims (OpenStax *Business Ethics*), how to run the conversation in the room (Liberating Structures; TC 25-20 for the review protocol), or how to engage cooperative external parties such as customers and communities, where this source speaks only by analogy.

## Worked Example

*The scenario is operator-authored. What happens in it is the scenario's; nothing here reports an effect MCDP 1 demonstrated.*

A regional water utility is replacing lead service pipes across three neighbourhoods over eighteen months. The programme director, Dana, has three workstream leads (field crews, resident communications, permits). A residents' association has organised against the dig schedule, which closes streets during school drop-off and pick-up.

**Framing the question (Phase 2).** Dana's first brief to the leads was a list of tasks: which streets, which weeks. When the permits lead found that a closure clashed with a school's pick-up, she kept to the schedule, because the schedule was the task. Dana rewrites the brief around intent, following MCDP 1's split between task and purpose:

> "The task describes the action to be taken while the intent describes the purpose of the action. . . . Of the two, the intent is predominant. While a situation may change, making the task obsolete, the intent is more lasting and continues to guide our actions." [V] (Ch 4, "Commander's intent")

Her new statement is one sentence in the doctrine's *in order to* form: replace every lead line in the three neighbourhoods by next autumn, in order to end lead exposure without making daily life harder than the work requires. She tells the leads they may move a dig week themselves when a closure would defeat that purpose, provided they tell her afterward. That is the mission-tactics pattern of subordinates who "inform the commander of what they have done, but they do not wait for permission" [V] (Ch 4, "Mission tactics"). The utility is not a military command; the move from commander and subordinate to director and workstream lead is the distillation's analogy.

**Mapping the opposing party (Phase 1).** The communications lead's plan assumed residents mainly lacked information, and proposed a leaflet campaign. Dana asks for the association's view from its own side first, using the doctrine's orienting discipline:

> "We should try to 'get inside' the enemy's thought processes and see the enemy as he sees himself so that we can set him up for defeat. It is essential that we understand the enemy on his own terms. We should not assume that every enemy thinks as we do, fights as we do, or has the same values or objectives." [V] (Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy")

The association is not an enemy, and Dana has no interest in setting it up for defeat; she uses only the discipline of seeing the other party on its own terms, not the purpose the doctrine gives it. In the scenario, two conversations with the association's organisers show that the leaflets would miss the point: parents want the schedule to respect school hours, and older residents fear the utility will not come back to restore their pavements.

**Surfacing conflict inside the programme (Phase 4).** Re-sequencing around school hours costs the field crews idle time, and the field-crew lead argues hard against it. Dana holds the decision open and asks each lead for their view, on the doctrine's rule for the window before a decision:

> "Until a commander has reached and stated a decision, subordinates should consider it their duty to provide honest, professional opinions even though these may be in disagreement with the senior's opinions. However, once the decision has been reached, juniors then must support it as if it were their own." [V] (Ch 3, "Professionalism")

**Ratifying (Phase 6).** Dana decides to re-sequence and says so in plain words. The field-crew lead, who argued against it, briefs his crews on the new sequence as his own plan; the scenario's point is that the dissent window closed when the decision was stated, not that the lead was persuaded.

**Post-engagement (Phase 7).** After the first neighbourhood, Dana holds a review within the week, while the events are fresh, and opens it by naming her own mistake: the first brief gave tasks without purpose. She asks why each choice was made and why it led where it did, not only what happened, which is the focus MCDP 1 sets for its critiques (Ch 3, "Training"). In the scenario, the second neighbourhood's schedule is drafted with the association's organisers before any street is closed.

What the example takes from MCDP 1: the ranking of intent above task and the *in order to* form (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"); subordinates who adapt and report after rather than waiting for permission (Ch 4, "Mission tactics"); the discipline of understanding the other party on its own terms (Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy"); the dissent-before, support-after rule (Ch 3, "Professionalism"); and the critique held soon after, open to all hands and focused on why (Ch 3, "Training"). The water utility, the association, the school-hours conflict and every outcome are the scenario's. The doctrine does not mention utilities, residents or stakeholders.

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Task-only briefing.** *Signal:* people ask permission for every deviation, or follow a plan past the point where it makes sense. *Why it fails, on the doctrine's terms:* the task goes stale when the situation changes and the intent is what keeps guiding action (Ch 4, "Commander's intent"). *Instead:* give the purpose with every task, briefly, and expect people to ask for it when it is missing.
- **Consensus theatre.** *Signal:* nobody disagrees with the most senior person in the room. *Why it fails:* the doctrine makes honest, professional disagreement a duty until the decision is stated, and will not tolerate compliance for personal advancement (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). *Instead:* ask for disagreement by name before deciding.
- **Relitigating a stated decision.** *Signal:* a decision is reopened by those who argued against it, or carried out half-heartedly. *Why it fails:* the same passage closes the window: once the decision is reached, juniors "must support it as if it were their own" [V] (Ch 3, "Professionalism"). *Instead:* state the decision clearly so everyone knows the argument is over.
- **Over-control of delegated engagement.** *Signal:* the lead scripts every stakeholder conversation, or uses dashboards and status tools to steer each move. *Why it fails:* the senior prescribes method "only to the degree that is essential for coordination" [V] and intervenes "only by exception" [V] (Ch 4, "Mission tactics"), and the doctrine warns that advanced information technology can tempt a senior toward precise, positive control of subordinates, which it calls incompatible with its philosophy of command (Ch 3, "Equipping") [AP]. *Instead:* set intent and coordination limits, then let the people in the conversation adapt and report.
- **A zero-defects climate for engagement roles.** *Signal:* people choose the safe non-action over a bold move because only mistakes get punished. *Why it fails:* the doctrine treats errors of overboldness leniently and errors of inaction or timidity severely (Ch 3, "Professionalism") [AP]; the reverse teaches timidity. *Instead:* weigh inaction as a failure too, while still counselling on mistakes and refusing recklessness.
- **Mirror-imaging the other party.** *Signal:* the engagement plan for an opposing party is written as if that party shared our goals and reasoning. *Why it fails:* the orienting discipline rules out assuming another party thinks as we do or has the same values or objectives (Ch 4, "Orienting on the enemy") [AP]. *Instead:* build the other party's view from its side before planning moves.
- **Treating stakeholders as the enemy.** *Signal:* cooperative parties are discussed in the language of targets, weak points and defeat. *Why it fails (the distillation's judgment, not a claim the doctrine makes about stakeholders):* MCDP 1's adversarial tools are each framed against an enemy: tempo as a pace the enemy cannot maintain (Ch 4, "Maneuver warfare"), centers of gravity and critical vulnerabilities as what the enemy cannot do without and where it can be hurt most (Ch 2, "Centers of gravity and critical vulnerabilities"), surprise as disorientation that degrades the enemy's ability to resist (Ch 2, "Surprise and boldness"), surfaces and gaps as enemy strengths and weaknesses (Ch 4, "Surfaces and gaps"); applied to parties whose interests partly align with ours, they turn partners into opponents. *Instead:* use the command-climate material on your own side of the table, and orient on opposing parties only to understand them.
- **Ranking stakeholders as main and supporting efforts.** *Signal:* some stakeholders are declared *supporting* and expected to yield. *Why it fails:* the doctrine's main effort ranks the actions within one's own command (Ch 4, "Main effort"); stakeholders are not units under command. *Instead:* name the main effort among your own workstreams, and treat stakeholders' interests as what that effort must serve or negotiate.
- **Speed as a reflex.** *Signal:* every pause is treated as ground lost. *Why it fails:* the doctrine's tempo is relative to an enemy, and it also values calculated patience and warns against rushing when time is not a limiting factor (Ch 2, "Surprise and boldness"; Ch 4, "Decisionmaking") [AP]. *Instead:* ask what the timing actually costs or gains with this party.

| US Army TC 25-20, *A Leader's Guide to After-Action Reviews* | The two documents use the word *critique* in opposite senses. TC 25-20 defines the AAR *against* the critique: in its account a critique gives only one viewpoint, frequently offers participants little opportunity to discuss events, and, in a climate focused only on what is wrong, stifles learning and team building (TC 25-20, Ch 1, "Definition and Purpose"). MCDP 1's critique is held as open and frank dialogue in which all hands are encouraged to contribute, and asks why actions were taken (MCDP 1, Ch 3, "Training"). A reader moving between the two should not carry TC 25-20's negative sense of the word into MCDP 1. Use TC 25-20 for the review protocol itself (attendance, questioning, the no-grading rule); use MCDP 1 for the leader's tolerance of admitted mistakes that the review depends on. |
| Hurtado, *Open Kanban* | Open Kanban's *Courage* value names the upward move: when a person in authority makes a mistake and someone of lower rank notices it, telling them takes courage; courage with respect for people enables delegation and demand-pull (Open Kanban, "Open Kanban Values"). MCDP 1 gives upward candour a time window (before the decision is stated) and a duty that follows it (support as if it were one's own). Use Open Kanban for the value; use MCDP 1 for when disagreement is due and what happens after. |
| Schwaber & Sutherland, *The Scrum Guide* | The same dissent-then-support shape around a single decision-holder: those wanting to change the Product Backlog do so by trying to convince the Product Owner, and the entire organization must respect the Product Owner's decisions (Scrum Guide, Scrum Team, "Product Owner"). The Sprint Goal likewise commits the Developers to an objective while leaving flexibility in the exact work (Scrum Artifacts, "Sprint Backlog", "Commitment: Sprint Goal"), close kin to MCDP 1's intent over task. Use the Scrum Guide inside a Scrum team; MCDP 1 supplies the general reasoning for purpose over prescription. |
| SSDL, *Systems Thinking Foundations* | Brief 1.03 holds that each person has a valid picture of the parts while their understanding of the whole is limited by their own vantage point, and that understanding is shaped by lived experiences, values, assumptions, power and biases. MCDP 1's orienting discipline asks the same of one opponent: understand it on its own terms and do not assume shared values or objectives. The aims differ: SSDL uses the partial views to build a shared model with the parties (Group Model Building), while MCDP 1 orients in order to defeat. For cooperative stakeholders, use SSDL's frame; MCDP 1 adds the discipline for a party that is genuinely opposed. |
| OpenStax *Business Ethics* | Supplies what MCDP 1 lacks entirely: who counts as a stakeholder (customers, clients, employees, shareholders, communities, the environment, the government, the media) and the claim that all stakeholders matter but not all have equal priority, with internal and external stakeholders distinguished (Business Ethics, Ch 3.1, "Stakeholder Relationships"). MCDP 1's main effort ranks your own actions, not the parties. Use Business Ethics to identify and weigh stakeholder claims; use MCDP 1 to organise your own side's effort once those claims are weighed. |

Pair them: OB keeps the disagreement on the idea; MCDP 1 sets when it is due and when it ends. |

## Citation and Source-Integrity Notes

**Borrowed-through gaps.** MCDP 1 draws on authors who are not held as primary references in this corpus:
- Carl von Clausewitz, *On War* (Howard/Paret translation), the most-cited work in the Notes (15 notes). The *Zweikampf* image in Ch 1 ("War defined") is Clausewitz's [BT].
- John Boyd. The Notes cite his "An Organic Design for Command and Control" for the idea of implicit communication (Notes 5 of Ch 4), and his lecture "The Patterns of Conflict" for the OODA loop (Notes 18 of Ch 2) [BT]. This distillation uses the doctrine's own statement of implicit communication, not Boyd's.
- Joint Pub 1-02, quoted in the Notes for formal definitions such as mission-type order [BT].

**Named limits of the source.**
- MCDP 1 does not use the word *stakeholder* and has no theory of who has standing. Its parties are seniors, subordinates, peers within one command, and an enemy. Every move in this distillation from those parties to stakeholders is an analogy and is labelled as one.
- It describes itself as broad guidance requiring judgment, with "no specific techniques or procedures for conduct" [V] (Foreword). Nothing here is an engagement procedure.
- Its review practice is the *critique*, described as part of training (Ch 3, "Training"). It does not use the term after-action review; applying the critique after a stakeholder engagement is this distillation's move.
- The main effort ranks actions and units within one's own command (Ch 4, "Main effort"). It says nothing about ranking outside parties.
- Its adversarial concepts (tempo against an enemy, surfaces and gaps, centers of gravity and critical vulnerabilities, surprise, the clash of irreconcilable wills) presuppose an opposing will. This distillation does not transfer them to cooperative engagement.
- It is a U.S. Government public-domain publication (Scope: open), so its words may travel verbatim into compiled applications.

**Evidence-marker continuity.** The deep reference at `references/mcdp1-warfighting-deep.md` marks the doctrine's own words `[V]` and words it borrows from others (epigraphs, Clausewitz, Boyd, Joint Pub 1-02) `[BT]`. Every `[V]` quotation in this distillation is a span of a `[V]` passage in the deep reference, checked word for word against the converted source `corpus.commons/demo/sources/converted/mcdp1-warfighting.md`. Curly quotation marks are straightened, the ellipsis in the first Worked Example blockquote marks one omitted sentence, and quotation marks inside a quotation are set as single quotes ('yes-men', 'zero defects', 'get inside'), where the source has double. One quoted word carries an OCR restoration taken from the deep reference: *objectives* in the orienting passage, which the scan renders "ohjectives". Several passages this distillation needed are paraphrased with `[AP]` rather than quoted because the scan garbles them (the essence-of-war sentence in Ch 1, the trust passage in Ch 3, the full implicit-communication sentence in Ch 4). No `[BT]` material is quoted. Unmarked prose, including every question, pattern, anti-pattern and the Worked Example scenario, is the distillation's own.
