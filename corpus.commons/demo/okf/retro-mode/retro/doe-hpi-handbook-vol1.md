# US DOE, Human Performance Improvement Handbook Vol. 1, Retrospective Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Department of Energy (2009). *Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 1: Concepts and Principles*. DOE-HDBK-1028-2009. Distribution Statement A, approved for public release. Licence: **Public domain (US Government work, 17 USC §105)**; DOE asks for acknowledgement in reuse. **Scope:** open. Third-party material inside the handbook (Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model, Mager and Pipe's gap analysis, INPO-derived lists, the Janis groupthink symptoms, and the tests it adapts from Reason and Johnston) is paraphrased here, never quoted.

## Retrospective Relevance

The handbook has no retrospective format; its closest practice is the post-job review, a feedback step that covers surprises, the usability of work documents, knowledge and skill shortcomings, minor errors, unanticipated conditions, tools, planning and supervision (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 1*, Ch 1, p. 1-9). What it brings to a retro is a way to read the deltas the team writes down. When a delta is a mistake someone made, the handbook asks what kind of error it was and fits the improvement to it: retraining does not fix a slip on a familiar task, a misapplied rule may be corrected through retraining, and a wrong mental model needs analysis first (Ch 2, pp. 2-27 to 2-28). When a delta keeps coming back, it reads the recurrence as a latent condition that has accumulated rather than faded (Ch 3, p. 3-16), or as a deviation that has been normalised (Ch 2, p. 2-6). When blame language appears, it supplies the reframing question "how did the organization fail the individual?" (Ch 4, p. 4-26).

It also names the team dynamics a lead will see in the room: deference to a senior person, free riding, groupthink and a group taking a risk no individual would (Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15). In retro work it fires mainly in Phase 3 (insight) and Phase 4 (experiment design), with uses in Phase 0.5 (experiment review) and Phase 2 (data gathering). Reach for it when the experiment on the board is "be more careful", "add a checklist reminder", or "retrain".

## Key Concepts for Retrospective

1.  **Slip, lapse, mistake, violation.** "Error is behavior without malice or forethought; it is not a result" [V]. Slips are physical actions that fail to achieve the immediate objective and lapses are failures of memory or recall; mistakes come from "an inadequate plan to achieve the intended outcome" [V] (Source: Ch 2, p. 2-8). Violations are deliberate departures, and "Most violations are well intentioned, arising from a genuine desire to get a job done according to management’s wishes" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-10). In a retro, a delta that names a mistake is a prompt to ask which kind.

2.  **Fit the experiment to the error.** For skill-based slips, retraining "is a waste of time and is an insult to the worker" [V]; the conditions that provoked the slip (interruptions, distractions, tools, fatigue) are what "can be controlled" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-27). Rule-based errors "may be corrected through retraining" [V]; knowledge-based mistakes need analysis first (Ch 2, pp. 2-27 to 2-28).

3.  **"Pay attention" is not an experiment.** "Avoiding error is not as simple as telling someone to “pay attention.”" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-19), because attention is limited and captured by unrelated matters; the handbook's response is to minimise interruptions and distractions (Ch 2, p. 2-19).

4.  **Frequency versus severity.** "Reducing the error rate minimizes the frequency, but not the severity of events. Only controls can be effective at reducing the severity of the outcome of error" [V] (Ch 1, p. 1-16). An experiment can make a problem rarer, or make it less harmful when it happens; a retro that only produces the first kind is missing the second.

5.  **Recurring deltas as latent conditions.** Latent conditions "do not fade away, but rather they accumulate in the system" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-16), and the handbook's safety-culture attributes treat recurrent errors as indicators of latent organisational weaknesses (Ch 4, p. 4-10).

6.  **Normalised practices.** The bald-tire attitude is "A belief that past performance is justification for not changing (improving) existing practices or conditions" [V] (Ch 2, p. 2-6); at-risk shortcuts are reinforced by convenience and rarely penalised (Ch 2, pp. 2-6 to 2-7).

7.  **Team errors in the room.** The handbook names the halo effect, pilot/co-pilot deference to a senior person, free riding, groupthink (eight symptoms adapted from Janis) and diffusion of responsibility [BT] (Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15); its remedies include freedom of thought, challenging others' assumptions, a devil's advocate and timeouts (Ch 2, p. 2-15).

8.  **Blame stops learning.** "If an individual believes his or her errors will be punished, then information related to those errors will likely remain obscure" [V] (Ch 4, p. 4-13). The blame cycle runs from error through discipline to reduced trust and less communication, while the latent weaknesses persist (Ch 4, p. 4-23).

9.  **Feedback needs visible change.** Post-job reviews should be quick to do, and "the worker must see appropriate changes in response to his or her feedback" [V] (Ch 3, p. 3-19). On surveys the handbook warns against asking for input and then not acting on it (Ch 3, p. 3-23).

## Questions to Ask During Retrospective

### Phase 0: Setup

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Will people name their own mistakes? | Does anyone expect a mistake raised here to count against them? If so, expect those mistakes to stay unspoken. | Ch 4, p. 4-13 |
| Is a senior person likely to steer? | Who in the room might others defer to, and how will their view be held back until others have written theirs? | Ch 2, p. 2-13 |

### Phase 0.5: Experiment Review

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Did the last experiments change anything people can see? | For each experiment from last time: what changed that the people who raised the issue can see? | Ch 3, p. 3-19 |
| Is an old delta back? | Is this the same problem as before? If so, which condition has been present each time? | Ch 3, p. 3-16; Ch 4, p. 4-10 |

### Phase 2: Data Gathering

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What conditions surrounded the mistake? | For each delta that names a mistake: what was going on at the time (time pressure, interruptions, unfamiliar task, unclear standard, confusing tools)? | Ch 2, pp. 2-31 to 2-32 |
| Is agreement real? | Has everyone written their own view before discussion, or are we converging on the first voice? | Ch 2, pp. 2-14 to 2-15 |

### Phase 3: Insight / Cause Analysis

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What kind of error is this delta? | Was it a slip or lapse on something we do all the time, a rule applied in the wrong place, or a situation none of us had a rule for? | Ch 2, pp. 2-19 to 2-27 |
| Has "carelessness" become the explanation? | If the insight reads "not paying attention" or "didn't follow the process", what made attention hard or the process unused? | Ch 2, p. 2-19; Ch 3, p. 3-22 |
| Is this practice normal because nothing has gone wrong yet? | Are we keeping a shortcut because it has always been fine? How close has it come? | Ch 2, pp. 2-6 to 2-7 |
| How did our setup let this happen? | In place of who slipped: what in our tools, process or workload made the slip likely? | Ch 4, p. 4-26 |
| Did a team dynamic play a part? | Did someone notice and not say, or did a check get skipped because the person was trusted? | Ch 2, pp. 2-12 to 2-15 |

### Phase 4: Experiment Design

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Does the experiment fit the error? | If it was a slip, does the experiment change the conditions rather than ask for more care or more training? | Ch 2, p. 2-27 |
| Is it only a reminder? | Does at least one experiment make the problem less harmful when it happens, not only rarer? | Ch 1, p. 1-16 |
| Is the risky step marked? | Is the one step where a mistake cannot be undone identified, so extra care goes there? | Glossary, p. ii |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Experiment reads "be more careful", "double-check", "pay attention" | Asking for attention the handbook says is limited (Ch 2, p. 2-19) | Ask what interruption, tool or workload made the slip easy; experiment on that |
| Experiment reads "train X on Y" for a task X does daily | Retraining a skill-based slip (Ch 2, p. 2-27) | Ask whether X knew how; if so, change the conditions instead |
| The same delta for the third retro | Latent condition accumulating (Ch 3, p. 3-16) | List what was present each time; escalate if the condition sits outside the team |
| "It's always worked" defends a shortcut | Bald-tire normalisation (Ch 2, p. 2-6) | Ask how many near misses the shortcut has had |
| Stickies converge instantly on the lead's view | Groupthink or deference (Ch 2, pp. 2-13 to 2-15) | Write individually first; ask for the strongest case against the leading view |
| A name appears on a delta | Blame drift; information will dry up (Ch 4, p. 4-13) | Reword the delta as the condition, not the person; ask how the setup let it happen |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- A delta names a mistake and the proposed experiment is training, reminders or "be more careful".
- The same issue keeps returning across retros and the team needs language for why.
- A shortcut is being defended because it has never caused trouble.
- Blame is creeping into the deltas and the lead needs a reframing question.
- Agreement comes too quickly or one senior voice sets the direction.

Do not use it for the retro format itself (the Field Guide and Scrum Guide carry that) or for the individual just-culture call (the NHS guide carries that). Its setting is DOE facilities; carrying it into a software team's retro is this projection's move.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored. A product team's retro has a delta on the board for the second sprint running: "Released with the wrong feature flag again." Last sprint's experiment was "everyone double-check the flags before release." The person who flipped the flag both times stays silent.

**Phase 0.5.** The lead asks what last sprint's experiment changed. Nothing visible: the double-check was a reminder. The handbook's line on attention fits: avoiding error "is not as simple as telling someone to “pay attention.”" [V] (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 1*, Ch 2, p. 2-19).

**Phase 3.** The lead asks what kind of error it was. The flag is flipped every release from a long list of similar names, usually late in the day, often while answering release questions in chat. The team recognises a slip on a practised task, with precursors the handbook lists: repetitive action, interruptions, confusing displays (Ch 2, pp. 2-31 to 2-32). Someone says "we should train people on the flag tool"; the lead reads the handbook's position:

> It is very hard to train a worker not to repeat something he or she did not intend to do in the first place. Training is not the solution in these instances. [V] (Source: Ch 2, p. 2-27)

The lead asks the reframing question, not who flipped the flag: how did the setup make the slip easy? The silent team member then adds that two flags differ by one word.

**Phase 4.** The team drops "double-check" and commits one experiment aimed at the conditions: rename the two near-identical flags and show the target environment in the release dialog. They add a second aimed at severity, because "Only controls can be effective at reducing the severity of the outcome of error" [V] (Ch 1, p. 1-16): a one-click flag rollback. Both have owners and land next sprint.

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **The care experiment.** Signal: "be more careful" on the board. Diagnosis: attention is limited (Ch 2, p. 2-19). Follow-up: change what makes the slip easy.
- **Training for a slip.** Signal: training proposed for a task people do daily. Diagnosis: remedy mismatched to error type (Ch 2, p. 2-27). Follow-up: ask whether they already knew how.
- **Frequency-only experiments.** Signal: every experiment aims to make the problem rarer. Diagnosis: severity untouched (Ch 1, p. 1-16). Follow-up: add one that limits harm.
- **Defending the shortcut.** Signal: "it's always been fine". Diagnosis: bald-tire normalisation (Ch 2, p. 2-6). Follow-up: count the close calls.
- **Naming the person.** Signal: a delta with a name in it. Diagnosis: the blame cycle; reporting dries up (Ch 4, pp. 4-13, 4-23). Follow-up: rewrite the delta as a condition.
- **Feedback with no visible change.** Signal: the team stops raising issues that went nowhere. Diagnosis: people must see change from their feedback (Ch 3, p. 3-19). Follow-up: open each retro with what changed since last time.
