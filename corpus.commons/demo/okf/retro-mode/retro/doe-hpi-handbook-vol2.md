# US DOE, Human Performance Improvement Handbook Vol. 2, Retrospective Distillation

**Source:** U.S. Department of Energy (2009). *Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 2: Human Performance Tools for Individuals, Work Teams, and Management*. DOE-HDBK-1028-2009. Distribution Statement A, approved for public release. Licence: **Public domain (US Government work, 17 USC §105)**; DOE asks for acknowledgement in reuse. **Scope:** open. Third-party material inside the handbook is paraphrased here, never quoted: the individual and work-team tools (Sections 1 and 2, whose primary references the Foreword credits to two INPO guides), the survey instruments, and the just-culture and investigation material that Volume 1 credits to Reason, Johnston and Dekker.

## Retrospective Relevance

Volume 2 carries the closest thing to a retrospective format in the DOE handbooks: the **post-job review**, a brief, face-to-face self-assessment after the work that collects what went well ("pluses") and what to improve ("deltas"), especially around critical steps, compares what happened with what was planned, routes the issues somewhere they will be worked, and reports the resolution back to the people who raised them (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 2*, Sec 2, pp. 54–57). Its list of at-risk practices for post-job reviews (no time allotted, principal participants absent, no follow-up method, follow-up not done face to face) reads as a checklist of why retros stop working (Sec 2, pp. 55–57).

Around that format the handbook supplies three things a retro lead can use. For the insight phase, the PACTS problem-solving tool and its at-risk practices: state the problem as a gap before naming causes, corroborate causes before acting, and do not let one person dominate or the group avoid controversy (Sec 2, pp. 61–62). For experiment design, the decision-making steps (goal, options, analysis, plan with owners and abort criteria, review) and the performance-indicator guidance on telling whether an experiment worked (Sec 2, pp. 63–65; Sec 3, pp. 77–79). For the room, the devil's advocate and the just-culture stance against judging people by consequence (Sec 2, pp. 63, 66; Sec 3, p. 96). It fires mainly in Phase 0.5 (experiment review), Phase 3 (insight) and Phase 4 (experiment design), with uses in Phases 2 and 5. Its companion `doe-hpi-handbook-vol1-retro.md` reads mistake-shaped deltas by error type; this volume supplies the meeting discipline and the follow-through.

## Key Concepts for Retrospective

1.  **Post-job review: forum, feedback, forms, follow-up.** Provide time for all active participants to talk or document feedback; identify pluses and deltas, especially at critical steps, comparing what happened with what was planned; record and route them, using the corrective action programme where appropriate; and follow up with the people on issues of high interest to them [AP] (Sec 2, p. 55). The technical version adds a face-to-face meeting led by the task lead and a final face-to-face report on resolutions [AP] (Sec 2, p. 56).

2.  **Why small deltas matter.** The handbook's reason for post-job reviews is that procedure, process, equipment and tool problems and minor errors tend to be latent and to accumulate if uncorrected [AR] (Sec 2, p. 54). Its management section puts it plainly: "Once they are created they do not fade away but rather accumulate in the system" [V] (Sec 3, p. 70). A retro that skips the small deltas lets them pile up.

3.  **PACTS for the insight phase.** Problem statement (a gap between what is and what should be, agreed by those closest to it), Analysis (structured, proportionate methods), Causes (those whose correction would prevent recurrence, consistent with the facts and sensitive to refuting evidence), Testing (corroborate causes, especially with those closest to the problem) and Solution (actions weighed for risk, benefit and cost) [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 61–62). The handbook places problem solving in the knowledge-based mode, with high error chances, and advises involving at least one more person [AP] (Sec 2, p. 61).

4.  **How cause analysis goes wrong in a group.** PACTS lists: defining the problem in terms of possible causes; trying solutions before defining the problem; concluding a procedure change or training will always fix it; naming a cause when none can be found; acting when unsure of the cause; ruling out causes without facts; letting one person dominate; leaving stakeholders out; and avoiding controversy [AP] (Sec 2, p. 62).

5.  **The devil's advocate.** Team leaders can assign a devil's advocate to watch for flaws and oversights, because most people focus on finishing the task rather than on what to avoid; in review meetings, a chair may designate one to challenge assumptions and statements of belief [AR] (Sec 2, pp. 63, 66). The handbook also warns that team errors happen in meetings and that interpersonal obstacles to open communication must be cleared first [AP] (Sec 2, p. 66).

6.  **Experiment design as a small decision.** Goal (the desired future state and what success requires), Options (several), Analysis (assumptions, effects on others, risks, benefits, costs), Plan (who, what, by when; contingencies; abort or hold criteria) and Review (periodic effectiveness review) [AP] (Sec 2, pp. 63–64). Deciding before defining the goal is the first at-risk practice listed [AP] (Sec 2, p. 65).

7.  **Knowing whether an experiment worked.** Lagging measures show results; leading measures show system conditions and forecast performance; "Leading indicators are more valuable than lagging indicators, but normally more difficult to select and use" [V] (Sec 3, p. 79). "Frequently, things are counted because they can be, but provide little insight as to how the organization is functioning now and no clue to future performance" [V], and with sparse data "a single event could appear to be a trend" [V] (Sec 3, p. 77).

8.  **Blame and the just culture.** The handbook criticises judging people by the consequence of an act rather than its intent, so that the same act is disciplined only when it causes harm, and calls a just culture, with honest errors unpunished and only recklessness sanctioned, the required prerequisite for people reporting errors [AR] (Sec 3, p. 96). "However, most organizations do not do a good job of acquiring feedback on human errors" [V] (Sec 3, p. 95).

9.  **Small changes are changes.** "most daily management activity involves some degree of change, such as changes in crew composition, maintenance schedule, policies, procedures, and equipment" [V], and structured change reduces the error potential of the people making it (Sec 3, p. 93). The eleven-step process includes consulting the people affected, generating and rewarding short-term successes, and patience [AP] (Sec 3, pp. 93–94).

10.  **Doubt as data.** Questioning-attitude cues include a gut feeling that something is not right and *danger words* such as *I assume* or *we've always*; at-risk practices include rationalising doubts and anomalies and discounting less experienced people's concerns [AP] (Sec 1, pp. 10–11, 17). In a retro, a delta phrased as a hunch is worth a question, not a dismissal.

## Questions to Ask During Retrospective

### Phase 0: Setup

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Can people name their own mistakes here? | Does the team believe honest errors will not be held against them? If not, expect the mistake-shaped deltas to stay off the board. | Sec 3, pp. 95–96 |
| Is the time protected? | Is the retro scheduled with enough time, with the people who did the work present, or is it squeezed in after the fact? | Sec 2, pp. 55–57 |

### Phase 0.5: Experiment Review

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Did the people who raised it hear back? | For each delta from last time, has someone told the person who raised it what happened, face to face? | Sec 2, pp. 55–56 |
| Was there a follow-up method at all? | Did each experiment have an owner and a way to follow it up, or did it end at the board? | Sec 2, pp. 55–56; Sec 2, p. 64 |
| Did it work, or did it just happen? | What measure would show whether the experiment changed anything, and is it a result we were already counting or a condition that predicts results? | Sec 3, pp. 77–79 |
| Are we reading one data point as a trend? | Is the "it got worse" or "it got better" based on one event? | Sec 3, p. 77 |
| Did it have side effects? | After a significant change, has anyone checked that it worked without unintended consequences? | Sec 3, p. 76 |

### Phase 2: Data Gathering

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Are we comparing with the plan? | Compared with what we planned, what actually happened, especially at the steps where a mistake would have hurt most? | Sec 2, p. 55 |
| Have the usual topics come up? | Any surprises, documents or tools that got in the way, knowledge gaps, minor errors, workarounds, supervisory support, planning and scheduling, or a lingering gut feeling? | Sec 2, p. 54 |
| Are the small things on the board? | Are minor errors and nuisances being left off because they did no harm? | Sec 2, p. 54; Sec 3, p. 70 |
| Is a hunch being waved away? | Did anyone have a feeling something was off, or catch themselves saying *I assume*? | Sec 1, pp. 10–11 |

### Phase 3: Insight / Cause Analysis

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Is the problem stated as a gap? | Can we say what is and what should be, in words the people closest to it agree with, before naming any cause? | Sec 2, p. 61 |
| Is a cause being assumed? | Is the problem statement already a cause in disguise? | Sec 2, p. 62 |
| Has the cause been tested? | What evidence supports the cause, what would refute it, and have the people closest to the problem checked it? | Sec 2, pp. 61–62 |
| Do we actually know? | If no cause can be found, are we willing to say so rather than pick one? | Sec 2, p. 62 |
| Is one voice carrying the analysis? | Is one person's idea dominating, or is the group avoiding a disagreement? | Sec 2, p. 62 |
| Is blame following the outcome? | Would we be discussing this person's act if it had caused no trouble? | Sec 3, p. 96 |

### Phase 4: Experiment Design

| Need | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| What is the goal? | What future state would tell us this worked, before we pick the experiment? | Sec 2, pp. 64–65 |
| Did we consider options? | What other experiments could reach the same goal, and what does each assume? | Sec 2, p. 64 |
| Is it "train them" or "change the process doc" again? | Would this change still help if the training or document change alone does not fix it? | Sec 2, p. 62 |
| Who, what, by when, and when do we stop? | Does each experiment have an owner, a date and a condition under which we would abandon it? | Sec 2, p. 64 |

|---|---|---|
| Where do the deltas go? | Which deltas go to our own experiments, and which need to be routed to someone outside the team? | Sec 2, p. 55 |
| Who reports back? | Who will tell each person who raised a high-interest issue what happened to it, and when? | Sec 2, pp. 55–56 |

## What to Look For

| Signal | Diagnosis | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Last retro's deltas were never mentioned again | The post-job review's at-risk practices: no follow-up method, no follow-up with the people involved (Sec 2, p. 55) | Name an owner and a report-back for each delta before closing |
| The retro is skipped when things went fine | The handbook treats minor problems as latent conditions that accumulate (Sec 2, p. 54; Sec 3, p. 70) | Keep a short version even after good work |
| "The problem is that we don't have X" | A problem defined in terms of a cause (Sec 2, p. 62) | Restate it as the gap between what is and what should be |
| The group settles on a cause within minutes | Causes not corroborated; possibly one voice dominating or controversy avoided (Sec 2, pp. 61–62) | Ask what would refute it; give someone the devil's advocate role |
| The experiment is a new rule or a training session | Concluding training or a procedure change will always fix it (Sec 2, p. 62) | Ask what else in the work would have to change |
| "It got worse this sprint" from one incident | "a single event could appear to be a trend" [V] (Sec 3, p. 77) | Look at more data before changing course |
| A metric is chosen because the tool already shows it | Counting what can be counted rather than what predicts (Sec 3, p. 77) | Ask what condition, if it changed, would show the experiment working |

## When to Use This Reference

Reach for this source when:
- The team needs a short, repeatable after-the-work review format, and a list of what makes such reviews decay.
- Deltas keep being raised and nothing comes back to the people who raised them.
- The insight phase jumps to a cause, or one voice is setting it.
- The team cannot tell whether last time's experiment worked.
- An experiment is turning into a process or structural change that needs more than a sprint's worth of handling.

It is not a retro facilitation guide: for safety checks, the Prime Directive, confidentiality and activities, use the Field Guide, Liberating Structures and the Open Practice Library. For reading a mistake-shaped delta by error type, use `doe-hpi-handbook-vol1-retro.md`. The handbook's setting is DOE facility and engineering work; carrying it into a software team's retro is this projection's move.

## Worked Example

The scenario is operator-authored. A platform team's retro opens with experiment review. Three sprints ago they agreed to "add a deployment checklist"; it exists, nobody knows if it is used, and two deploys have failed since. The lead's first instinct is to add the checklist again with "make it mandatory".

**Phase 0.5.** The lead asks who followed up on the checklist experiment and how the team would know it worked. No one owned it, and the only measure is "failed deploys", one of which happened last week. The handbook names both problems: a post-job review with no follow-up method and no follow-up with the people involved (Source: US DOE, *HPI Handbook Vol. 2*, Sec 2, p. 55), and an outcome count read as a trend:

> Frequently, things are counted because they can be, but provide little insight as to how the organization is functioning now and no clue to future performance. [V] (Source: Sec 3, p. 77)

**Phase 2.** The team writes pluses and deltas against what they planned for the sprint, not against a blank page (Sec 2, p. 55). Several small deltas appear that people had not thought worth raising: a staging environment that drifts from production, a flaky smoke test everyone reruns, and a hunch from the newest engineer that the checklist's rollback step does not match the current tooling (Sec 1, pp. 10–11; Sec 2, p. 54).

**Phase 3.** A senior engineer says the cause is "people not using the checklist". The lead asks the group to state the problem as a gap first: deploys should either succeed or roll back cleanly, and two did neither (Sec 2, p. 61). Stated that way, "not using the checklist" is a guessed cause, and the newest engineer's hunch is evidence against it: the checklist itself may be wrong (Sec 2, p. 62). The lead gives one person the devil's advocate role for the remaining discussion (Sec 2, p. 63). The team checks the rollback step against the tooling during the retro, and it is out of date.

**Phase 4.** The team sets a goal (every failed deploy rolls back without manual steps), considers options, and picks one experiment: fix and test the rollback step in staging each sprint, owned by one person, with an abort condition if staging drift makes the test meaningless (Sec 2, pp. 63–64). For a measure they choose a leading one, whether the rollback test ran and passed each sprint, alongside the lagging count of failed deploys (Sec 3, pp. 77, 79). The "make it mandatory" option is set aside: concluding that a procedure change will always solve the problem is an at-risk practice in the handbook's list (Sec 2, p. 62).

**Phase 5.** The staging-drift delta is routed to the infrastructure team's lead, and the retro lead commits to telling the newest engineer, face to face, what happens to the rollback fix (Sec 2, pp. 55–56).

## Anti-patterns This Reference Helps Avoid

- **Deltas into the void.** Signal: issues raised in retros never come back. Diagnosis: no follow-up method or report-back (Sec 2, p. 55). Follow-up: an owner and a named report-back for each.
- **Skipping the small stuff.** Signal: only big problems make the board. Diagnosis: minor problems are latent and accumulate (Sec 2, p. 54; Sec 3, p. 70). Follow-up: ask for minor errors and nuisances explicitly.
- **Cause-first problem statements.** Signal: "the problem is we lack X". Diagnosis: a problem defined in terms of a cause (Sec 2, p. 62). Follow-up: state the gap first.
- **The confident voice wins.** Signal: one person's explanation becomes the team's. Diagnosis: an individual dominating problem solving (Sec 2, p. 62). Follow-up: a devil's advocate and corroboration before acting.
- **Rule-or-training reflex.** Signal: every experiment is a new rule or a training session. Diagnosis: an at-risk practice in problem solving (Sec 2, p. 62). Follow-up: ask what in the work would change.
- **Measuring what's easy.** Signal: the experiment's measure is whatever the dashboard already shows. Diagnosis: counting because it can be counted (Sec 3, p. 77). Follow-up: add a leading measure of the condition the experiment targets.
