The Org Topologies Map

Language of Org Design

An archetype is a common pattern for how an organizational 
unit is structured and operates, such as a cross-​functional 
team that delivers end-​to-​end. Each of the 16 archetypes on 
the OT map has distinct characteristics. They have short names 
("CAPS-1", "WHOLE-3",...), which creates a common org design 
language, enabling change agents to accurately describe both 
the current state and the desired future state of their 
organization with shared understanding.

This means some new terms – logical, easy to learn, and based 
on the visual map – to understand and communicate any 
organizational building block.

“Most of our teams are CAPS-2—​multi-​skilled yet incomplete, 
lacking certain capabilities. There are also functional TASKS-1 
parties supporting our CAPS-2 teams. This creates 
dependencies and challenges that are managed by CAPS-1 
and PART-1 groups…”

These few sentences convey a wealth of information! They 
describe an ecosystem, allowing us to grasp its dynamics 
holistically, recognize existing challenges, and begin a 
meaningful conversation about organizational improvement.

An OT ecosystem includes the structure or configuration of an 
organization’s archetypes and other components—​
departments, groups, teams, functions, and the relationships 
among them. The OT approach enables us to visualize, 
categorize, and then compare and contrast ecosystems with 
the purpose of improving any organization.

Org Topologies introduces this simple but powerful language to 
clarify organizational development without resorting to “agile” 
or other framework-​specific jargon. This makes it easier to see 
things as they are, bringing together specialists from different 
backgrounds and facilitating open, unambiguous discussions 
about the current situation and the next steps.

Horizontal Dimension of Org Design: Skills Mandate

The Horizontal Axis

Five Horizontal Levels

From left to right:

The horizontal dimension represents the scope of skills mandate 
that is given to an organizational unit. This corresponds to the 
level of autonomy a unit has in delivering items of work. The more 
rightward an organizational unit is on the map, the more that unit 
can quickly deliver without dependencies, and so the better the 
flow of value delivery.

1. Functional Units are individuals and groups specializing in a functional area, 
often resulting in the creation of functional groups (or departments). Example: 
Movie Costume Department. To produce value, work needs to be specified and 
handed over to each of the specific individuals who contribute partial steps to 
the end-​to-​end solution. The hand-​offs and information scatter increase the 
need for coordination, resulting in slow delivery and high process waste.

To make a connection to the popular software development 
approach Scrum, in that model, the horizontal axis is the 
increasing completeness of the Definition of Done.

In economic terms, this axis represents the transaction costs  
(a.k.a. overhead costs) to deliver value (decreasing from left to 
right!), and with that, how much waste from concept to cash.

2. Multi-​Skill Units (cross-​functional teams) provide more flow and 
responsiveness than Functional Units. Example: Surgical Team of doctors and 
nurses. Multi-​skill Units are incomplete, meaning they do not contain all the 
skills required to deliver value end-​to-​end. They co-​exist with functional units 
and other teams on whom they depend to deliver complete value. Example: 
The Surgical Team relying on Post-​Op Care Team, and both together needed 
for a complete service for the patient.

The lightbulb💡in the top-​right corner symbolizes the real 
problem to solve. The more capabilities the units have, the closer 
they are to the real problem, and the easier it is for them to 
create a skillful solution. Note that to start solving the real 
problem, progress along only the horizontal axis is not enough.

3. End-​to-​End Units independently do everything to deliver complete value to 
customers and thus have no dependencies. Example: Software Feature Team. 
This results in much faster flow, more responsiveness, shorter and stronger 
feedback cycles from customer to creator (since there are no middlemen), and 
less process waste. That usually leads to happier workers and customers.

4. Expanding Units are end-​to-​end units with the mandate to acquire new 
skills when needed. Example: Special-​Forces Military Team. Such teams need 
to continuously learn when preparing for new missions. Expanding Units keep 
learning whatever is needed to stay end-​to-​end capable over time.

Vertical Dimension of Org Design: Work Mandate

Five Vertical Levels

The Vertical Axis

From Bottom to top:

Whereas the horizontal axis is the scope of skills mandate, 
the vertical represents the scope of work mandate given to 
organizational units. That determines how units collaborate, 
learn, and share work. The higher a unit is on the map, the 
bigger is its field of play. At the highest levels, the scope of 
work of different units overlaps, motivating them to learn to 
co-​own and work as one super unit – “Team-​of-​Teams”.

1. Tasks Focus: Units work on the narrowest scope of work. Example: A Sous-​Chef 
preparing ingredients, or a visual designer sketching a wireframe. As input, they 
receive fine-​grained tasks that match their expertise (“chop onions”, “design 
graphic”). It’s an org design decision to limit their mandate, even though they may 
be capable of learning and doing more.

In economic terms, this axis represents the switching costs to 
work on something different (decreasing from bottom to top), 
and, therefore how easy it is for the whole organization to 
switch direction, ideally driven by feedback, innovation, 
competition, or strategic changes.

2. Capabilities Focus: Units work on a full “capability” within a product or service. 
Example: Insurance Claims-​Processing Group. For products, a capability is often 
called a feature or function. For services, it’s also called a service element. The unit 
name usually signals the capability: Surgical Team, Engine Team, Web Search Team, 
and so on. Common consequences include silo thinking and local optimizations.

The larger the scope of work the units own, the closer they 
are to holistically understanding and solving real problems.

UNBOUNDED

3. Partial-​Business Focus: Units work on a particular business vertical, clearly 
separated from the other areas. Example: Uber Ride versus Uber Eats, two separate 
profit centers within a single company. A key characteristic of partial-​business units 
(e.g. all Teams within Uber Ride) is cross-​team collaboration and learning because 
they are closely involved in business success or failure and so care about outcomes. 
The need for close collaboration between teams at this level usually leads to 
organizational simplification.

Four Organizational Characteristics

Incomplete versus Complete Archetypes

In certain organizational designs, individuals are viewed primarily as “resource units” assigned to specific 
tasks. These archetypes create partial solutions and rely on “thinkers and planners” in other archetypes 
that coordinate and prepare work to ensure full utilization of the “resources”.

This perspective—​often implicit—​can be made explicit and visual with Org Topologies mapping.

Complete 
Archetypes

Incomplete 
Archetypes

These archetypes are incomplete because they require other archetypes to deliver customer value 
collectively. Incompleteness arises whenever an organizational unit holds only part of the skills or does 
not have the mandate to apply all the skills needed to serve customers. By contrast, complete archetypes 
can independently deliver customer value without depending on other archetypes. Members of these 
archetypes typically focus on “value” and emphasize crucial concepts like “outcome” and “flow.”

One consequence of an org design with complete archetypes is that they can become independently 
operating silos. Whether that’s a virtue or vice depends.

Output versus Outcome Archetypes

There is a well-​known management principle of maximizing outcomes while minimizing outputs.

Outcome Archetypes

Outputs—​such as number of surgeries, amount of software features, number of completed design 
documents, or percentage of closed insurance claims—​are relatively straightforward leading metrics. 
However, more output does not mean more outcomes. In fact, more output usually means more cost or 
investment, which can lower profit (an outcome). Investing to increase outcomes (healthy people, profit) is 
justified; ‘investing’ to increase output is suspect.

Output Archetypes

So, an archetype can be output or outcome-​oriented. For an organizational unit to deliver outcomes, it 
needs to work in a higher-​level business context, as seen on the OT map.

People working in this quadrant of doing archetypes cannot

Driving Archetypes

arrive, and then go on. These driving archetypes are capable of 
understanding, creating, and delivering customer value in its broadest 
sense. That's where directing, doing, and delivering come together. 
These archetypes can grasp the concept of business objectives or end-​
to-​end customer journeys and deliver upon them jointly thanks to an 
overlapping, shared scope of work and a broad skills mandate. Example: 
A three-​person startup or five teams within a Team-​of-​Teams.

Org Design Fit for Purpose

Strategic Org Design

Adaptiveness
(chosen org goal)

Something important not every leader knows: (1) different org 
designs can have different goals (adaptiveness, flow, …), (2) 
organizational goal can help or hinder a business objective 
(market share, profit, …), (3) leaders can and should 
deliberately design an organization to be fit for purpose: that 
is, the org design goal (e.g. “adaptive”) should align with and 
influence the business objective (e.g. “profit”).

This is strategic org design in which every organizational 
element—​from structures to career paths to policies to 
processes—​is intentionally decided and aligned so that each 
part reinforces the others to be fit for purpose (“the org goal 
is high adaptiveness to support profit growth”).

This approach contrasts sharply with adopting a pre-​existing 
framework that promises to “solve your problems.” And it 
contrasts with the idea of senior management only defining 
business objectives; in strategic org design, leaders 
understand and design the details of their internal 
organization to be fit for purpose. They align the external 
(business objective) and internal (org design).

Horizontal & Vertical Progress on the Map

Elevating
(toward target org design)

The map on the right is for an organization with the business 
objective to increase market share in a new market. What org 
goal will likely support this? Leadership decides that in this 
unfamiliar market they need lots of customer interaction, 
learning, and, most of all, adaptiveness (easy change) to 
discover opportunities and grow.

They decide on an org design where teams work on the whole 
business challenge. This requires them to move vertically up 
the map by expanding their scope of work. And it requires 
them to expand their expertise, their scope of skill, moving 
horizontally to the right on the map.

Resource Topology:  Directing + Doing

Resource Topology

There are three common Org Topologies to know, starting 
with: The Resource Topology—​a topology characterized by 
frozen functional archetypes specialized in one domain. Its 
purpose is to ensure that each unit is 100% utilized (i.e. 
resource utilization). It is seen in “resource management” 
approaches where “resource managers” (or project 
managers) are responsible for planning and monitoring 
resource allocation and utilization.

DIRECT﻿ING

In this topology, the value-​creation work takes place among 
the Doing Archetypes in the lower left quadrant. However, 
none of these can independently produce end-​to-​end 
customer value. They depend on each other due to narrow 
specialization. Additionally, they lack a clear and holistic 
understanding of customer value, as their work mandate 
focuses solely on specific tasks or capabilities.

DOING

The Directing Archetypes in the upper left quadrant treat 
the Doing Archetypes as “resources,” matching skill demand 
with skill supply. The Delivery Archetypes do not participate 
in high-​level discovery due to ‘efficiency’ justifications. 
Consequently, analysis in the form of detailed specifications 
(a film Wardrobe Breakdown, a software Requirement Spec) 
are handed over for implementation. This topology requires 
traditional project management with significant upfront 
planning and estimation, and frequent monitoring and 
replanning in cases of high variability.

Learning in this topology is largely confined to improving 
existing skills rather than discovering new ones. The 
Resource Topology is fit for organizations that rent available 
resources for their existing defined skills, reaching their 
business objectives (e.g. making profit) by keeping 100% of 
them 100% busy. Such a topology is a management’s choice 
and justified, for example, when the relationships are 
temporary between the Directing and Doing archetypes (e.g. 
a movie Producer hiring dancers for a scene).

Delivery Topology:  Directing + Delivering

Delivery Topology

Compared to the Resource Topology, this topology upgrades 
delivery units to have fast flow by removing dependencies. 
Structurally, this typically involves forming complete cross-​
functional teams, and facilitating a smoother path to 
“completely done” work. In some domains, this topology is 
called a “Feature Factory” since Delivery units can provide a 
near-​endless stream of features. Note a strong focus on 
outputs and local efficiency.

DIRECTING

However, in order to streamline delivery, discovery must 
remain separated in space and time. Similar to the Resource 
Topology, Directing Archetypes emphasize up-​front analysis 
to supply the work, and they may direct an “analysis Doing 
unit” to discover and then write “the specs”, or they may 
create them themselves.

DELIVERING

Keeping the teams ‘fast’ and constantly ‘efficient’ in 
producing the outputs is only possible when the teams in the 
Delivery Archetypes are permanently fixed to a narrow work 
scope—​an internal component or a specific set of 
capabilities. The other side of this coin is that they cannot 
easily switch contexts. By the intentions of this org design, 
teams lack the work mandate to engage with the “big 
picture” but are allowed to expand their skill set becoming 
more and more efficient within their narrow area of expertise.

Returning to “outcome vs output”: Notice that there is no 
guarantee in this topology that the output effectively solves 
the real problem or creates outcomes people care about. 
Products and services with “feature bloat” are a common 
consequence.

The Delivery Topology is well-​suited for domains where the 
core challenge is not discovering what to deliver, but 
delivering new capabilities predictably with short lead times. 
For example, in a “diner” restaurant with short-​order cooks; 
an owner decides the menu (or directs a specialist to design 
one) and via the flow of orders, directs “quick cooks who can 
deliver complete plates.”

Adaptive Topology:  Driving

Adaptive Topology

The Adaptive Topology merges “directing, doing, and 
delivering” into one holistic unit within the Driving archetypes, 
where humans, AI agents, and robots collaborate on complex 
problems in an adaptive way, and the group explores and 
drives themselves in their ‘car’. The goal of this topology is 
adaptiveness (easy, cheap change based on learning) and 
customer-​centricity to discover and deliver customer delight.

DRIVING

The boundaries between teams are structurally removed and 
replaced by larger constructs—​such as a Team-​of-​Teams—​
where organizational units work closely together across the 
entire business (“Uber”) or a partial business domain (“Uber 
Eats”).

“Synchronicity of work” is a unique emergent quality of this 
topology: in contrast to the other two topologies where the 
Doing and Delivering archetypes own a narrow scope of work, 
here in the Adaptive Topology, the Driving archetype allows all 
parties to work in unison.

Inside the Driving archetypes of the Adaptive Topology, 
individuals (human and AI) form a network and create a 
learning organization that shares objectives and learns to 
operate as one. We refer to the archetypes in this topology as 
Elevated to distinguish them from the Resource and Delivery 
topologies. Its core features include:

Broad scope of capabilities and work.
Synchronous work, coordinated by those performing the 
work.
Continuous learning both vertically (across the scope of 
work) and horizontally (across the scope of skills), making 
it cheap and easy to switch strategic direction.

Don’t underestimate the importance of making change cheap 
and easy! We have seen countless failures due to the 
opposite. The Adaptive Topology establishes the conditions to 
discover and deliver higher-​impact customer outcomes, 
promoting long-​term business resilience. It’s fit for purpose for 
organizations where growth and learning are crucial, such as 
market disruptors and startups.

Systems thinking teaches us to focus on the interactions 
and influences between the parts, and to improve all the 
major parts in a systemically reinforcing design that 
supports a common org goal (for instance, “flow”). If each 
element (e.g. policies, structures) is optimized in isolation, 
the performance of the whole system may actually decline 
for the desired goal (flow can get worse!). By seeing the 
system as an integrated collection of interacting parts that 
need to be aligned, organizations can create change that 
is both holistic and sustainable.

By contrast, org design is internal, under our control, and 
closer to being deterministic or predictable (for instance, 
“design for fast flow”). This allows us to influence business

Designing an organization is about selecting the org goal 
that will deliver the capabilities required by the strategy 
and its objective. Possible goals include output 
predictability, utilization, flow, customer delight, 
adaptiveness, and more. The key is picking the subset that 
is best fit for purpose. Note that if more than one goal is 
chosen together for an organization, issues arise: Is some 
goal primary over others? Do they conflict? Can the org 
design support them all? These issues are beyond the 
scope of this Primer.

After identifying the desired org goal(s), the next step is to 
explore organizational designs that align with the strategy. 
If flow is the goal, the Delivery Topology becomes a 
suitable choice.

Companies may also choose different org designs for 
different parts of the company. One division might focus 
on servicing existing customers on a legacy product, while 
another is experimenting with new offerings—​each 
requiring its own goal and design.

Elevate Your Organization with Strategic AI Adoption

Any management approach that doesn’t include AI as a central part 
of the future workforce, is of the past. We are rapidly entering a world 
of AI agents and humanoid robots playing a role at work and home.

For example, a pet-​supply company creates e-​commerce software in a 
Resource topology. It’s a mature, uncomplicated market. Competition 
has delivered a better website and app that customers love. Leadership 
has decided on a business objective of retaining customers, with an org 
goal of fast flow to parity, by targeting a Delivery topology with CAPS-3 
teams.

A key insight when designing with OT: Specialization and expertise are 
vanishing as limited resources, due to intelligence as a service. That 
makes it much easier to elevate an organization! So when you are 
deciding on new teams requiring new expert knowledge, shed the old 
assumptions, “We don’t have enough experts, therefore…”. Spin up 
another 10,000 agents.

> What parts of the company are the focus of org development? 
The Product Management and R&D departments, so focus on AI 
investment there.

Evolving rightward on the horizontal axis to broader skills mandate will 
become ever easier due to AI. And likewise upward on the vertical axis 
to a broader work mandate. So, top-​right Driving archetypes will 
become more feasible.

> What archetypes are part of the target? What’s a dominant constraint 
from one to the other? 
The R&D department has mostly TASKS-1 individuals, and the target is 
CAPS-3 teams. On investigation, it’s a lack of front-​end design and 
coding skilled people. Focus time, money, and management attention on 
AIs, especially on that big bottleneck, over a generic “adopt AI.”

Obviously, “adopt AI everywhere you can” will be the general guidance, 
but given the limited resources of money, time, and attention, in 
strategic org design with OT we recommend that you ask and answer 
these questions:

> What parts of the organization are the focus of development with OT?

> Do the AIs need monitoring by humans? 
Yes, because people have decided on AI agents that autonomously 
design and code front ends, independently creating and iterating on 
variations that they deploy and measure. The small number of existing 
front-​end designers and developers joining the new CAPS-3 teams will be 
trained in agent monitoring and take on that responsibility.

Focus your AI investment in the area you are elevating, where 
competitive advantage may come from early adoption of new 
technology.

> What archetypes are part of your target, and what are the major 
bottlenecks or constraints limiting their rapid adoption?

Strategic Org Design vs. Framework Thinking

Mapping SAFe
Mapping RDHY

Framework thinking assumes that implementing an industry-​standard 
management framework is key to success. This is the pitch from consulting 
companies big and small, and naturally, it seems easier to follow the crowd over 
deeply understanding and designing your own organization.

Unfortunately, organizations apply such frameworks without understanding the 
implications, which can lead to consequences:

The Scale Agile Framework 
(SAFe) is a framework used in 
software development. Saying 
“We are doing SAFe” actually 
communicates little about 
what’s really happening.

The Haier Rendanheyi model 
(RDHY) is an innovative 
management philosophy. It 
represents a radical departure 
from traditional hierarchical 
structures by aiming to align 
every employee’s efforts directly 
with customer (or user) value 
creation.

But observe the OT map for 
SAFe; anyone can easily see 
that SAFe is mostly 
implemented with 
interdependent incomplete 
teams working on components 
at the Tasks level, and fast-​flow 
teams at the Capabilities level.

Not solving the root-​cause problems motivating the change, since it’s not 
designed for the specific case.
Lack of enthusiastic understanding and support for the change, since the 
people didn’t figure it out and create it themselves; it’s pushed on to them. 
Don’t underestimate this psychological dynamic!
And although scoffed at and ignored (at their peril) by managers new to 
change and social psychology, widespread and inadvertent promotion of a 
“cargo-​cult” mentality, leading to inappropriate and failed change, and 
Religious Wars.
