## Handling Conflict Between Two Stakeholders

Conflict between two stakeholders should be treated as a problem to understand, not a contest to suppress. Farrell and colleagues' *Systems Thinking Foundations* argues that stakeholders often hold different goals because they experience the same system from different positions. Each view may therefore be partial yet valid. The first task is not to force alignment, but to discover what each person can see, what each needs, and what structural pressures shape the disagreement.

Prepare before convening them. In separate conversations, define the disputed decision in neutral language and ask each stakeholder about desired outcomes, constraints, risks, decision authority, and prior history. OpenStax *Organizational Behavior* recommends mapping power bases, resource dependencies, and each party's best alternative to a negotiated agreement. This prevents a superficially equal conversation from concealing a serious power imbalance. It also helps select an appropriate conflict mode: collaboration for a consequential, continuing relationship; compromise when time is short and power is balanced; competition when a legitimate, urgent decision is non-negotiable; accommodation when the issue matters far more to one side; or avoidance only when the matter is genuinely trivial or premature.

Use the Open Practice Library's explicit social-contract practice: attack the issue, not the person; make assumptions explicit; permit disagreement; and clarify whether the participants are advising, deciding, or seeking ratification. OpenStax distinguishes useful substantive conflict from corrosive relationship conflict and argues that fair process can preserve commitment even when outcomes disappoint. Give each stakeholder uninterrupted time to explain interests and constraints, then exchange information rather than repeat positions. The *Liberating Structures Handbook* adds a useful discipline: ask a substantive question, allow silence, and resist filling it too quickly. Silence gives participants time to formulate a candid response. When emotions rise, pause and restate the substantive question; if personal attacks continue, use a neutral facilitator or separate the parties temporarily before resuming joint work.

Next, build a shared account of the conflict. Record agreed facts, disputed interpretations, underlying interests, and constraints where both can see them. *Systems Thinking Foundations* cautions that different language may reveal different mental models, not mere misunderstanding. Treat those differences as data. Ask what common purpose both parties serve, then generate several options before evaluating any one. An integrative solution should address interests on both sides; if no such solution exists, name the trade-off honestly instead of disguising dominance as consensus.

Close by converting discussion into a testable commitment. The Open Practice Library's Action Steps practice specifies Who, What, When, and With; Liberating Structures similarly ends Discovery and Action Dialogues with named next actions. Document the decision, owner, deadlines, dependencies, unresolved risks, escalation route, and conditions that would trigger revision. Schedule a checkpoint rather than assuming the agreement is self-executing. At that checkpoint, assess both delivery and relationship quality. Farrell and colleagues frame trust as an accumulation built or depleted over time, while OpenStax emphasises sustained liaison work. Handling conflict well therefore means not only reaching a settlement, but also keeping commitments, revisiting changed conditions, and leaving both stakeholders able to work together again.
