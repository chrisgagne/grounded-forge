## Handling Conflict Between Two Stakeholders

Conflict should be treated neither as a personal failure nor as something to smooth away. In complex settings, stakeholders often have different goals because they experience the same system from different positions; each holds a valid yet partial mental model and brings accumulated trust or distrust from prior encounters. The facilitator's first task is therefore curiosity: understand both perspectives and their histories before seeking agreement. (*ssdl-systems-thinking-foundations.md*, Key Concepts 1–4 and 9 [V])

Preparation should begin with separate one-to-one conversations. Use open-ended questions, allow silence, and create psychological safety so each stakeholder can explain needs, goals, limitations, perceived harms, and non-negotiables without immediately defending a public position. (*open-practice-library.md*, Key Concept 2) Then map the practical structure: who controls critical resources, what kind of interdependence exists, what authority each person holds, and each side's best alternative to agreement. These factors make power imbalances and walk-away choices visible. (*openstax-organizational-behavior.md*, Key Concepts 2, 4–6, and 10 [AP]; borrowed-through frameworks [BT])

In the joint conversation, state what remains open to influence, who ultimately decides, and what a successful outcome must accomplish. Establish ground rules, name any "elephant in the room," and use silent idea generation or equal-turn formats so status does not determine airtime. (*open-practice-library.md*, Key Concept 6 and Phases 1–2 [V]; Dissent Cards [BT]) Ask each stakeholder to discuss interests and constraints rather than repeat demands. Distinguish substantive or process disagreement from relationship conflict. If discussion becomes personal, pause and reframe it around specific behaviours and issues. (*openstax-organizational-behavior.md*, Phases 3–4 and "Conflict descended into personal attack")

Next, generate options jointly and test them against agreed criteria. Prefer integrative bargaining for an ongoing relationship because it seeks a larger, positive-sum solution; use distributive bargaining only where the issue is genuinely fixed and transactional. (*openstax-organizational-behavior.md*, Key Concept 9 [AP]) Choose the conflict mode deliberately: collaboration fits high-stakes integrative problems; compromise fits time pressure and roughly equal power; accommodation fits an issue that matters much more to one side; competing may fit urgent, authority-bound decisions; avoidance fits only trivial issues or disputes that are merely symptoms. (*openstax-organizational-behavior.md*, Key Concept 8 and Phase 4 [AP]; Thomas framework [BT]) Fair procedure can sustain commitment even when the final distribution disappoints one party. (*openstax-organizational-behavior.md*, Phase 4)

If direct negotiation stalls, mediation adds a trained, future-oriented facilitator while leaving control with the parties; arbitration transfers greater control to a judge-like decision-maker. (*openstax-business-law.md*, Key Concept 4) Close by recording who will do what, by when, and with whom, alongside decision rights, checkpoints, and a revision process. (*open-practice-library.md*, Key Concepts 1 and 13 [V]; *openstax-organizational-behavior.md*, Phase 5) Finally, follow through and review the agreement. Trust is an accumulated stock, so repeated kept commitments matter more than one successful meeting. (*ssdl-systems-thinking-foundations.md*, Key Concepts 9–10 [V]; Meadows lineage [BT])
