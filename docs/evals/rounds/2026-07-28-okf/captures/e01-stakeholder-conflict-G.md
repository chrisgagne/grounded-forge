Conflict between two stakeholders should begin as a clash of claims, not a contest of personalities. A stakeholder is anyone who affects or is affected by an organization's decisions, and one person or group may occupy several stakeholder roles at once. Because stakeholder claims can conflict, first state the decision in dispute, identify each party's roles, and describe each claim in neutral language. Treat both parties as ends whose interests possess intrinsic value, while acknowledging that limited resources may require prioritization (openstax-business-ethics/references/stakeholder.md).

Next, analyze before negotiating. Stakeholder management is a systematic process of identification, analysis, planning, and engagement. For each party, ask what is at stake, what they stand to gain or lose, whether they support or resist the proposed action, what power they can exercise, and what might retain support or win over resistance. This makes the conflict concrete and exposes asymmetries that a vague call to "compromise" could conceal (openstax-principles-management/references/stakeholders.md).

Power deserves explicit attention. The bundle distinguishes expert, referent, legitimate, reward, and coercive power. Expert and referent power are most associated with commitment; legitimate and reward power commonly yield compliance; coercive power is most likely to produce resistance. A facilitator should therefore clarify who has formal authority, invite relevant expertise, and avoid confusing forced compliance with genuine agreement. This is especially important when one stakeholder controls valued outcomes or can impose penalties (openstax-organizational-behavior/references/organizational_power_bases.md).

Structure the conversation around listening rather than persuasion. The Conversation Café agreements call for open-mindedness, acceptance, curiosity, discovery, sincerity, and brevity. Although its documented process is designed for groups of six to eight, its dialogue principles can inform a two-party meeting: give each stakeholder an uninterrupted opening account, allow each to deepen or respond, then open the exchange, and close by naming what challenged or changed their thinking. That adaptation is an application of, not a format explicitly prescribed by, the bundle (liberating-structures-handbook/references/conversation_cafe_dialogue.md). In facilitated learning, divergent accounts are not resolved by choosing a winner; instead they reveal perspective, context, and new understanding (lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024/references/facilitated_learning_analysis.md).

Then reframe the disagreement as a "Wicked Question": a genuine tension with no obvious answer, not a complaint disguised as inquiry. For example, "How can we protect the delivery commitment while also preserving the quality threshold?" Holding both sides together can surface hidden assumptions, differences, common ground, and creative alternatives (liberating-structures-handbook/references/wicked_questions.md).

If the conflict follows an unintended outcome, use a Just Culture lens. Seek honest accounts, examine the tools, procedures, incentives, and trade-offs shaping action, and direct system-level accountability toward the organization rather than reducing the issue to individual blame. Individual accountability means participating honestly in learning and repair; restorative justice asks affected parties to discuss impacts, needs, obligations, and repair (lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024/references/just_culture.md).

Finally, make the resolution traceable: record which claims are addressed, in what sequence, what each party may gain or lose, and how support will be retained or resistance addressed. Fairness does not remove the need to prioritize; it makes the reasoning visible. The result is transparent prioritization that weighs the parties' interests fairly and in sequence (stakeholder.md; stakeholders.md).
