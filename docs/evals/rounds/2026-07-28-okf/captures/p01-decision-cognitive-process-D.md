OpenStax's organisational-behaviour framework classifies this as a non-programmed decision: novel, consequential, ambiguous, and emotionally charged. Its decision process, combined with OpenStax Economics' sunk-cost analysis, suggests the central discipline for tomorrow:

**Do not decide whether the original vendor choice was defensible. Decide whether the next six months of this relationship are a better forward investment than the best transition-and-re-tender alternative.**

The facts provided are not enough to recommend extension or termination. They are enough to identify the questions and process needed for a defensible choice.

## Before the meeting: questions to ask yourself

1. **OpenStax Organizational Behavior, ch. 6.2–6.4 [AP]: Is this being treated as a verdict on the CTO rather than as a forward decision?**

   The formal question should be something like: "From today, which course offers the best expected strategic outcome at acceptable cost and risk?" Past advocacy, reputation, and embarrassment are outside that question except where they affect present implementation.

2. **OpenStax Economics 3e, ch. 2.1 [V]: What would we choose if we had not already invested six months?**

   The text's sunk-cost rule is to "forget about the money and time that is irretrievably gone" and compare current and future options. Ask:

   - If this vendor were presented to us today, with its current performance record, would we award it the next six months?
   - What new value is another six months expected to buy?
   - What are the forward costs of extension—including engineering workarounds, management attention, delay, dependency, and foregone alternatives?
   - Conversely, what are the genuine forward costs and risks of termination: transition, knowledge loss, procurement time, dual-running, and disruption?

3. **OpenStax Organizational Behavior, ch. 6.4–6.5 [AP]: What evidence would make me change my current preference?**

   If you cannot answer that, confirmation bias or escalation of commitment may already be operating. Write down before the meeting:

   - Your current preference.
   - Your confidence level.
   - The strongest evidence against it.
   - The evidence that would reverse it.

   Ask each principal participant to do the same privately.

4. **OpenStax Psychology 2e, ch. 8.3 [AP]: Are we using records or reconstructed memories?**

   Memory is reconstructive, especially after disappointing outcomes. Retrieve the original business case, success criteria, contract commitments, risk assumptions, decision minutes, and expected timeline. Compare those with current evidence rather than with statements such as "we always knew this would take time" or "the warning signs were obvious."

5. **Open Practice Library, "Design of Experiments" and "Start At The End" [V]: Can each option be expressed as a testable hypothesis?**

   For example:

   - **Extension hypothesis:** Given specified vendor actions and internal support, defined outcomes will improve to specified thresholds by stated dates.
   - **Termination hypothesis:** A managed exit and re-tender will produce a better capability within an acceptable transition-cost and risk envelope.

   For each, specify measures, pass criteria, major assumptions, and what failure would look like.

6. **OpenStax Economics 3e, ch. 2.1 [V], and OpenStax Organizational Behavior, ch. 6.3 [AP]: Is the apparent binary hiding better-designed alternatives?**

   Even if the final authority requires "extend or terminate," examine at least:

   - Unconditional extension.
   - Conditional extension with milestones, remedies, and stop conditions.
   - Short bridge while re-tendering.
   - Termination with staged migration or temporary partial retention.

   This is marginal analysis: "a little more or a little less," rather than an undifferentiated yes/no. It also prevents the group from stopping at two superficially opposed positions.

7. **SSDL's Systems Thinking Foundations, Brief 1.03 [AP]: What partial mental model does each constituency hold?**

   Ask separately:

   - What does the CTO believe is producing the underperformance?
   - What do engineers closest to the work believe?
   - What does the vendor believe?
   - What risks is the board responding to?
   - Who bears the operational downside but has no voice tomorrow?

   SSDL cautions that mental models are especially resistant among people not personally experiencing the negative effects. That makes the engineering team's private frustration decision-relevant evidence, not merely a morale issue.

8. **OpenStax Psychology 2e, ch. 12.1 [AP]: Are we over-attributing the result to people?**

   Test both directions:

   - Are critics treating the vendor or CTO as inherently incompetent while ignoring contract design, governance, internal dependencies, unclear requirements, or changing conditions?
   - Are defenders attributing success to their judgement but failure entirely to circumstances?

   Situational analysis should not excuse poor vendor capability; it should clarify which intervention could plausibly work.

9. **SSDL, Briefs 1.05–1.07 [AP]: Is extension a fundamental intervention or merely a symptomatic fix?**

   Look for a possible *Shifting the Burden* pattern: repeated escalation meetings, extra internal staff, revised plans, or temporary concessions make the relationship survivable while reducing pressure to solve the underlying capability or contract problem. Treat this as a hypothesis to test, not a label.

10. **OpenStax Psychology 2e, ch. 10.1 [BT]: Is the room's arousal level compatible with a complex decision?**

    The Yerkes–Dodson work carried through the text says complex tasks perform worse under excessive arousal. Since the meeting cannot simply be deferred, lower the temperature structurally: circulate a common evidence pack, collect initial views privately, clarify decision rights, and prohibit personal attribution.

## Failure modes in the meeting

- **Open Practice Library, "1-2-4-All" [V], and Psychology 2e, ch. 12.4 [BT]: the CTO or chair speaks first and anchors the room.**  
  Have everyone record a preferred option and confidence privately before senior leaders state a view. The chair and CTO should speak after operational evidence has been heard.

- **OpenStax Organizational Behavior, ch. 6.4 [V]: process conflict becomes relationship conflict.**  
  Disagreement about evidence, forecasts, or contractual remedies can improve performance; personal attack degrades it. Interrupt statements such as "you chose badly" or "engineering never supported this" and restate them as testable claims.

- **OpenStax Psychology 2e, ch. 12.4 [BT]: apparent unanimity masks conformity.**  
  Asch's studies, as carried through the text, show that even one dissenter reduces conformity. Assign someone to argue the strongest case against the emerging preference; solicit concerns from quieter participants explicitly.

- **OpenStax Organizational Behavior, ch. 6.6 [V]: people choose peace over voicing objections.**  
  The text's definition of groupthink is precisely this suppression. Treat "everyone seems comfortable" as weak evidence. Ask, "What concern would you raise if the decision-maker were not in the room?"

- **Open Practice Library, "Confidence Voting" [AP]: agreement is mistaken for commitment.**  
  Use a private 0–5 confidence check. Ask everyone at 0–2 for their bottom-line concern before committing. The aim is not forced consensus; it is visible dissent and an informed decision.

- **OpenStax Economics 3e, ch. 2.1 [V]: past investment re-enters through euphemisms.**  
  "We need to protect the relationship," "we are nearly there," and "we cannot throw away six months" may be legitimate claims, but require forward evidence. Keep a forward-cost/forward-benefit table visible.

- **Liberating Structures, "What/So What/Now What" [V]: the group jumps directly to action.**  
  Keep the sequence: What actually happened? What pattern does it imply? Now what should be done? A rush to "give them one last chance" or "terminate immediately" skips the interpretive step.

- **Liberating Structures, "25-to-10" [V]: a poll is mistaken for the decision.**  
  Votes and confidence checks surface information; they do not replace legitimate authority. State beforehand who owns the final decision and how input will be used.

- **SSDL, Brief 1.07 [AP]: missed goals are quietly redefined.**  
  This is the *Drifting Goals* pattern. Preserve the original commitments on the page. If the standard should change because conditions changed, make that an explicit new decision with a rationale.

- **Open Practice Library, "Architectural Decision Records" [AP]: the room reaches a conclusion but leaves no auditable rationale.**  
  Before adjournment record the decision, evidence, rejected alternatives, assumptions, dissent, owners, dates, measures, review point, and reversal conditions.

## A workable meeting structure

A 75-minute version would be:

1. **10 minutes:** State the precise decision, decision owner, criteria, and original commitments. No advocacy yet.
2. **10 minutes:** Silent individual response: preferred option, confidence, strongest contrary evidence, and what would change the view.
3. **15 minutes:** "What?" Review evidence and variance from the original case. Engineers closest to the work contribute before senior leaders.
4. **15 minutes:** "So what?" Separate vendor capability, contract/governance design, internal conditions, and external change. Test sunk cost, attribution error, and system effects.
5. **10 minutes:** Compare the viable forms of extension and termination. Run a brief premortem on the leading option.
6. **5 minutes:** Private confidence vote; hear the lowest scores first.
7. **10 minutes:** Decision owner commits and records rationale, owners, milestones, stop conditions, and review date.

This combines Liberating Structures' data-pattern-action sequence, OPL's silent input and confidence checks, and OpenStax's protection against groupthink and escalation.

## After the decision: patterns to monitor

Regardless of the outcome:

- **OpenStax Psychology 2e, ch. 7.3 and 12.1 [V/AP]: watch for hindsight and self-serving history.**  
  Compare later narratives with the decision record: what was actually known, assumed, and uncertain tomorrow?

- **OpenStax Organizational Behavior, ch. 6.6 [AP]: judge process separately from outcome.**  
  A sound decision can encounter bad luck; a weak process can get lucky. Review both the outcome and the quality of the reasoning.

- **SSDL, Brief 1.06 [AP]: distinguish flows from stocks.**  
  One good delivery month is a flow. Restored engineering trust, reduced dependency, accumulated capability, and sustainable vendor performance are stocks. Do not infer recovery from a temporary activity spike.

- **Liberating Structures, "Ownership versus Buy-in" [V]: watch implementation energy.**  
  If engineers comply minimally, keep concerns private, or continue shadow workarounds, the decision may have been delivered to them rather than owned with them.

- **Open Practice Library, "Disagree and Commit" [V]: preserve both commitment and the review point.**  
  Dissenters should execute the decision professionally, but their contrary evidence should remain inspectable. "Commit" must not mean "the decision can never be revisited."

If you extend, look particularly for milestone dilution, new explanations replacing evidence, increased internal rescue work, and repeated vendor promises without a changed mechanism. Those are possible escalation-of-commitment, *Drifting Goals*, or *Shifting the Burden* signals.

If you terminate, look for scapegoating the CTO, declaring all vendor use mistaken, and reproducing the same governance or contracting structure with the next supplier. Psychology's attribution-error work and SSDL's *Fixes that Fail* pattern both warn that changing the visible actor without changing the producing system can recreate the result.

One source boundary matters: the OpenStax books are introductory syntheses; many canonical findings—Asch, Janis, Kahneman and Tversky, Yerkes–Dodson—are carried through them as borrowed-through research `[BT]`, not as primary studies in this corpus. Liberating Structures and the Open Practice Library are practitioner sources used here for meeting design, not experimental proof.
