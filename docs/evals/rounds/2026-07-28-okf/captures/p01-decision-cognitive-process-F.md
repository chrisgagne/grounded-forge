The core question is not "Was the CTO's original choice right?" It is: "From today, which option produces the better next six months after accounting for future benefits, costs, risks, and opportunity costs?" Past expenditure, effort, and political capital are sunk; transition and re-tendering costs are future costs and must be included. (Econ-2)

Treat the CTO's defensiveness as a condition to design around, not as proof of bad faith. Cognitive dissonance research says threats to a positive self-image can motivate rationalization or denial, but it would be an attribution error to diagnose the individual without examining situational causes. (Psych-12)

## Before the meeting: diagnostic questions

### 1. What does "underdelivering" mean in observable terms?

Ask yourself:

- Which contracted outcomes are below target—not merely which activities, deliverables, meetings, or tickets are late?
- What were the baseline, target, actual result, trend, and data source for each outcome?
- Were the targets and responsibilities genuinely clear six months ago?
- Are averages hiding disagreement between business leadership, engineering, and users?
- What evidence suggests performance is improving, flat, or deteriorating?

The psychological-safety practice explicitly distinguishes outcomes from outputs and asks whether expectations are understood. The Experiment Canvas turns a proposition into defined outcomes, pass/fail evidence, metrics, timeframe, and ownership. (PsychSafety; Experiment)

### 2. What evidence would make me choose the opposite option?

Write down, before discussion:

- The strongest evidence for extending.
- The strongest evidence for terminating.
- The single fact that would most change your current view.
- What information is missing or unreliable.
- Whether you are overweighting one dramatic recent event, the first number presented, or evidence that confirms your prior position.

Anchoring emphasizes one initial fact; confirmation bias privileges congenial evidence; availability overweights vivid or recent examples. These biases tend to preserve existing beliefs. (Psych-7)

A useful counterfactual is: "If we had no incumbent vendor today, would we select this vendor for the next six months on these terms, given what we now know?" That removes the irrecoverable past from the forward decision. (Econ-2)

### 3. Are we explaining a system problem as a vendor or personality problem?

Ask:

- Which failures were demonstrably within the vendor's control?
- Which involved unclear specifications, slow internal decisions, governance gaps, integration constraints, skill shortages, or conflicting incentives?
- What conditions would remain unchanged after re-tendering?
- Has the engineering team had to compensate informally for defects in the formal operating model?
- What evidence distinguishes vendor incapability from a badly designed client–vendor system?

The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to overemphasize personal or dispositional explanations while underweighting situational causes. Learning-review guidance similarly warns that blaming an individual can conceal policies, processes, culture, goal conflicts, and actual work conditions. This does not exonerate the vendor; it prevents an unsupported single-cause story. (Psych-12; LearningReview)

### 4. Am I comparing forward economics, or defending past investment?

For each option, estimate only what changes from today:

- Extension: remaining fees, internal supervision, remediation effort, expected benefit, continued dependency, and the opportunity cost of delaying a market test.
- Termination: transition disruption, knowledge transfer, procurement effort, replacement cost, delivery delay, and the probability that a new vendor actually improves the outcome.
- For both: the cost of being wrong and how quickly the mistake becomes detectable.

Marginal analysis compares changes in costs and benefits between alternatives. Sunk-cost reasoning says irrecoverable expenditure should not influence the current choice, even though abandoning it can feel like admitting an earlier error. (Econ-2)

### 5. Have I heard the engineering team, or merely heard what they will say in front of the CTO?

Ask:

- What concerns appeared privately but have not appeared in formal governance?
- Do engineers believe they can raise tough issues without career or relationship consequences?
- Would their position change if collected anonymously?
- Is disagreement concentrated in particular roles or experiences?
- Have quiet contributors been explicitly invited to provide evidence?

Public responses increase conformity; private responses reduce it. In the Asch research summarized by the bundle, even one dissenter sharply reduced conformity. The psychological-safety practice recommends anonymous input and treating high variance as meaningful disagreement. The learning-review guide specifically warns that senior supervisors may suppress different perspectives and tells facilitators to listen for the "silent voice." (Psych-12; PsychSafety; LearningReview)

### 6. What could make each option fail?

Run two pre-mortems, preferably as private pre-work tonight:

1. "We extended, and six months later the relationship failed badly. What happened?"
2. "We terminated and re-tendered, and six months later that decision failed badly. What happened?"

Have people write independently before sharing. Cluster the causes, rate likelihood and impact, identify mitigations, owners, and dates. Include skeptics and domain experts. That is the bundle's strategic pre-mortem and risk-management method. (Premortem; Risk)

### 7. What is the decision rule?

I would extend only if all of these are credible:

- The important performance gaps have identifiable, correctable conditions.
- There is an owned remediation hypothesis rather than a general promise to "improve."
- Forward value exceeds the re-tender option after transition and opportunity costs are included.
- Success and failure can be distinguished with pre-agreed measures during the extension.
- High-impact risks have tolerable residual exposure and workable mitigations.

Otherwise, termination and re-tendering is the cleaner decision. This rule combines forward-looking marginal analysis, falsifiable experiment design, and likelihood/impact risk assessment. (Econ-2; Experiment; Risk)

## Failure modes during the meeting

Watch especially for these:

- **The CTO speaks first and frames the answer.** Directive leaders who disclose their view early can suppress disagreement. Have the decision leader speak after others have presented evidence. (Psych-12)

- **A public show of hands is treated as independent judgment.** Public positions are more vulnerable to conformity. Collect an initial private rating and confidence level before discussion, then repeat it afterward. (Psych-12)

- **Silence is interpreted as agreement.** Groupthink symptoms include self-censorship, shielding the leader from dissent, quashing contrary views, and an illusion of unanimity. (Psych-12)

- **The meeting becomes a referendum on the CTO.** Dissonance and self-image threat will make evidence harder to process; personal labels also invite attribution error. Separate "the original decision" from "the best decision now." (Psych-12)

- **The most recent failure dominates.** That is availability; the first cost estimate or performance narrative can also anchor the discussion. Put the complete evidence table in front of everyone before advocacy starts. (Psych-7)

- **Participants search only for evidence supporting their faction.** Explicitly ask each side to state the strongest case against its preferred option. (Psych-7)

- **Strong opening positions become more extreme through discussion.** Group polarization strengthens an initial group attitude after like-minded discussion. Mix viewpoints and use an assigned dissenter. (Psych-12; Dissent)

- **Dissenters are made to defend themselves personally.** Assign dissent as a role and respond with questions such as "What risk do you see?" and "What has the rest of us overlooked?" The Dissent Cards practice is designed to make disagreement less personal. (Dissent)

- **A single bad outcome becomes the root-cause story.** The learning-review guide warns that a single event is one data point and that causal recommendations can be highly outcome-dependent. Reconstruct context, information, incentives, and dependencies. (LearningReview)

- **Emotion is treated as either proof or noise.** Emotion includes cognitive appraisal, and different people can appraise the same event differently. Ask what each person believes is at stake—delivery continuity, credibility, engineering load, or governance—then return to evidence and criteria. (Psych-10)

- **Conditional extension ends with vague assurances.** A real experiment specifies the problem, hypothesis, desired outcomes, evidence that would make it true or false, metrics, timeframe, roles, and follow-up. (Experiment)

A sound meeting process is therefore: private initial views; common facts and criteria; engineering evidence before executive advocacy; dual pre-mortems; explicit dissent; private rescoring; then a decision with assumptions, mitigations, owners, thresholds, and review dates. Open-decision guidance also calls for recording which feedback changed the decision and why other feedback was not adopted. (Psych-12; Premortem; ODF)

## Patterns to watch after the decision

| Pattern | If you extend | If you terminate | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Confirmation and dissonance | Misses are rationalized or success thresholds drift | Every historical miss is reinterpreted as proof termination was inevitable | Freeze the evidence, assumptions, targets, and pass/fail rules now. (Psych-7; Psych-12; Experiment) |
| Hindsight bias | A recovery is described as obviously foreseeable | Transition trouble is described as obviously foreseeable | Review what was reasonably knowable at decision time separately from the eventual outcome. (Psych-7; LearningReview) |
| Self-serving attribution | Success is credited internally; failure is blamed on the vendor | Improvement is credited to re-tendering; disruption is blamed on others | Examine both personal and situational explanations and retain conflicting perspectives. (Psych-12; LearningReview) |
| Voice deterioration | Engineers learn that raising problems changes nothing | People learn that admitting uncertainty about termination is disloyal | Repeat anonymous checks on whether people can raise tough issues, ask for help, and take risks. (PsychSafety) |
| Activity replacing outcomes | Extra meetings, reports, or staffing are called "improvement" | Procurement progress is called delivery improvement | Keep the scorecard on user/business outcomes and the defined litmus-test metrics. (PsychSafety; Experiment) |
| System recurrence | Governance defects continue under the incumbent | The same defects are transferred to a new vendor | Review policies, processes, dependencies, incentives, and goal conflicts—not just supplier performance. (LearningReview) |
| Action decay | Remediation tasks lose owners or deadlines | Transition and re-tender lessons disappear | Use actions with named owners and due dates, and follow them through retrospectives. (Premortem; Retro; Blameless) |

Regardless of the result, create a short decision record containing:

- The decision and accountable owner.
- The alternatives considered.
- The forward costs, benefits, and opportunity costs.
- Evidence used and important uncertainties.
- Dissenting views.
- Assumptions and what would falsify them.
- Risks, mitigations, owners, and dates.
- Review checkpoints and pre-agreed consequences if thresholds are missed.
- Feedback incorporated—and feedback not incorporated, with reasons.

That structure follows the Open Decision Framework's requirements for transparency about problems, constraints, research, feedback, tradeoffs, and later updating. (ODF)

Finally, review the decision without blame. Reconstruct what information was available, why the choice made sense at the time, which assumptions proved wrong, and what system conditions should change. Retrospectives call for tracked improvement actions; blameless postmortems emphasize timeline, context, psychological safety, and follow-through; the learning-review guide cautions against hindsight judgment and recommends outside perspectives to uncover tacit assumptions. (Retro; Blameless; LearningReview)

The bundle does not contain comparative vendor-management research showing that extension or re-tendering is generally superior, nor a dedicated concept file on escalation-of-commitment studies. It therefore cannot determine the answer from the scenario alone. It does directly support the sunk-cost, bias, conformity, groupthink, attribution, psychological-safety, pre-mortem, and learning-review guidance above.
