## The authors’ thesis

The book presents accounting as a system for organising, analysing, and communicating information so that people can make decisions, rather than as a narrowly mechanical exercise in bookkeeping. [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Accounting Activities and Users”)

> Accounting is the process of organizing, analyzing, and communicating financial information that is used for decision-making.

[V] (Ch. 1, Key Terms)

That definition supplies the organising logic for the entire volume. Transactions must first be identified and measured, then recorded through the double-entry system, accumulated and adjusted through the accounting cycle, communicated through financial statements, and finally interpreted using ratios and other analytical tools. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.3; Chs. 3–5; Ch. 16; Appendix A)

The source repeatedly treats usefulness for decision-making as the criterion by which accounting information, procedures, controls, statements, and analytical measures should be understood. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.4)

> The ultimate goal of accounting is to provide information that is useful for decision-making.

[V] (Ch. 1, §1.2)

The pedagogical thesis is correspondingly practical: students should understand both why an accounting treatment exists and how to apply it. The chapters combine concepts, journal-entry mechanics, continuing examples, ethical cases, standards comparisons, financial-statement interpretation, and exercises rather than presenting procedures in isolation. [AR] (Preface, “About Principles of Accounting”; “Pedagogical Foundation”; “Comprehensive Art Program”)

> Students are immersed in the “why” as well as the “how” aspects of accounting in order to reinforce concepts and promote comprehension over rote memorization.

[V] (Preface, “About Principles of Accounting”)

## Scope, sequence, and teaching apparatus

The volume is the financial-accounting half of a two-semester financial and managerial accounting sequence. It is designed for accounting and non-accounting majors and begins with familiar personal and organisational decisions before moving into formal recognition, measurement, recording, reporting, control, and analysis. [AR] (Preface, “About Principles of Accounting”; “Coverage and scope”)

The sixteen-chapter sequence moves through five broad layers:

1. accounting’s social and decision-making role and the basic financial statements;
2. transaction analysis and the complete accounting cycle;
3. merchandising, information systems, fraud, and internal control;
4. recognition and measurement of receivables, inventory, long-term assets, and liabilities;
5. financing and ownership through bonds, corporations, partnerships, and the statement of cash flows. [AR] (Preface, “Coverage and scope”; chapter outlines for Chs. 1–16)

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, an applied “Why It Matters” scenario, and a chapter outline. Recurring instructional devices include Your Turn exercises, Concepts in Practice examples, Think It Through prompts, Continuing Application at Work cases, Ethical Considerations, IFRS Connection comparisons, and Link to Learning resources. Chapters conclude with key terms, summaries, multiple-choice questions, discussion questions, exercises, problems, and thought provokers. [AR] (Preface, “Pedagogical Foundation”; “Effective Art Program”; “Additional Resources”)

The source uses continuing businesses and recurring scenarios to make the accounting sequence cumulative. Printing Plus carries transaction analysis into journal entries, adjusting entries, financial statements, and closing; other worked businesses include Cheesy Chuck’s, Clip’em Cliff, Gearhead Outfitters, Acorn Lawn & Hardscapes, Propensity Company, Virtual Co., and Banyan Goods. [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.3; Ch. 3, §§3.4–3.6; Ch. 4, §§4.2–4.5; Ch. 5, §5.4; Ch. 6, “Why It Matters”; Ch. 15, §§15.2–15.5; Ch. 16, §§16.3–16.5; Appendix A)

## Part I — Accounting as decision infrastructure

### 1. The role of accounting in society

#### Accounting as a general-purpose language

The source begins by extending the familiar description of accounting as the language of business to decisions in ordinary life. Leasing or buying a vehicle, choosing between jobs in cities with different living costs, evaluating a theatre production, and deciding whether an employee should remain on call all require the comparison of financial consequences. [AE] (Ch. 1, §1.1)

> A traditional adage states that “accounting is the language of business.” While that is true, you can also say that “accounting is the language of life.”

[V] (Ch. 1, §1.1)

Accounting is therefore not confined to producing statements. It identifies relevant economic events, records and organises their effects, analyses the resulting information, and communicates it to people who must choose among alternatives. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.3)

#### Financial and managerial accounting

Financial accounting measures an organisation’s performance in monetary terms and communicates it through standardised reports, especially to parties outside the organisation. Its information is substantially historical, although users employ that history to evaluate performance and form expectations. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.2)

Managerial accounting serves internal users. It may combine financial and nonfinancial information and is not constrained by the comprehensive external-reporting conventions that govern financial accounting. The source’s examples include budgets, pricing, product-line analysis, equipment repair-or-replace decisions, make-or-buy decisions, staffing choices, and automation. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.2)

The difference is consequently one of primary audience, reporting conventions, and decision purpose rather than wholly separate subject matter. Both branches organise evidence about economic activity, but financial accounting emphasises comparable external reporting while managerial accounting adapts information to management’s particular decisions. [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2)

#### Users and information limits

Internal users include managers and employees making day-to-day and forward-looking decisions. External users include investors, financial analysts, loan officers, governmental auditors, and other stakeholders evaluating an entity from outside it. [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.2)

Historical accounting information supplies feedback and a basis for prediction, but it does not guarantee future performance. Users must interpret the reported figures in light of business conditions, organisational aims, and the information’s limits. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.2, 1.4)

#### Organisations and operating activities

The text distinguishes for-profit, governmental, and not-for-profit organisations. Within for-profit activity it differentiates manufacturing, retail, and service businesses because their resources, transactions, inventory requirements, and measures of performance differ. [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.3)

The accounting system follows the activities through which an organisation obtains resources, uses them, and supplies products or services. For-profit entities can obtain capital from profitable operations, equity financing, or debt financing; each source creates different claims and consequences for stakeholders. [AR] (Ch. 1, §§1.3–1.4)

#### Stakeholders

A stakeholder is a person or group affected by, or capable of affecting, the organisation. The source examines stockholders, creditors and lenders, governmental and regulatory bodies, customers, managers, and employees, showing that the same report can answer different questions for each group. [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.4)

Stockholders assess profitability, risk, and prospective return. Creditors assess repayment capacity. Regulators assess compliance. Customers may care about continuity and warranties. Managers use accounting to plan and control. Employees depend on organisational viability and may evaluate compensation or retirement arrangements. [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.4)

The interests of these groups may diverge, which makes neutrality, transparency, and professional integrity central to accounting’s public role. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.4–1.5; Answer Key, Ch. 1, Question 17)

#### Standards and institutions

The source places US external reporting within generally accepted accounting principles. It identifies the Securities and Exchange Commission as the federal regulator of public securities markets, the Financial Accounting Standards Board as the private-sector body that establishes US financial accounting standards, and the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board as an overseer of public-company audits. Governmental accounting is associated with the Governmental Accounting Standards Board. [BT] (Ch. 1, §§1.2–1.4; Ch. 3, §3.1; Ch. 8, §§8.5, 8.7)

International Financial Reporting Standards are issued through the International Accounting Standards Board. The source reports that more than 120 countries have adopted or permit IFRS, while the United States continues to use US GAAP; foreign issuers meeting SEC requirements may use IFRS without reconciling their statements to US GAAP. [AR][BT] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “IFRS Connection”)

#### Accounting careers

The source presents accounting education as preparation for auditing, taxation, financial accounting, consulting, accounting information systems, cost and managerial accounting, financial planning, entrepreneurship, governmental work, and not-for-profit work. [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.5)

It names the CPA, CMA, CIA, CFE, CFA, and CFP credentials and explains that their education, examination, experience, and continuing-development requirements differ. [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.5)

A cited Association of Chartered Certified Accountants and Institute of Management Accountants study is used to argue that changing technology increases the value of strategy, communication, analysis, judgement, and ethical conduct rather than removing the accountant’s role. [AR][BT] (Ch. 1, §1.5, “Personal Attributes of Accountants”)

The chapter’s examples broaden accounting beyond ledgers: NikeID illustrates customer-driven operational information, the Bernie Madoff case illustrates failures of trust and oversight, Al Capone illustrates forensic use of financial evidence, and 7-Eleven illustrates the use of timely information in a large operating network. [AE] (Ch. 1, §§1.2–1.5)

### 2. Financial statements and their interdependence

#### Business forms

The introductory reporting model distinguishes three legal structures: sole proprietorship, partnership, and corporation. Their ownership and liability arrangements differ, but each must identify assets, liabilities, revenues, expenses, and owners’ claims. [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.1)

A sole proprietorship has one owner and no legal separation comparable to a corporation. A partnership brings two or more owners together under an agreement. A corporation is a separate legal entity whose ownership is divided through shares. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1)

#### The four primary statements

The source introduces four primary financial statements in a fixed preparation sequence:

1. income statement;
2. statement of owner’s equity;
3. balance sheet;
4. statement of cash flows. [AR] (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3)

The income statement reports financial performance over a period by comparing revenues and gains with expenses and losses. The difference is net income when inflows exceed outflows and net loss when the reverse holds. [AP] (Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.2)

The statement of owner’s equity explains changes in the owner’s claim over the period. It begins with opening equity, incorporates investments and net income or loss, subtracts distributions, and produces ending equity. [AP] (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3)

The balance sheet reports financial position at a point in time through the equation assets equal liabilities plus equity. It uses the ending equity derived from the preceding statement. [AP] (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3)

The statement of cash flows reports cash inflows and outflows over the period. Because the other statements are prepared on an accrual basis, this statement addresses the distinct question of how reported activity affected cash. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1)

The statements are not independent outputs. Net income flows from the income statement into the equity statement; ending equity flows into the balance sheet; the balance-sheet cash balance must agree with the ending cash shown by the statement of cash flows. [AR] (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3)

#### Elements and classifications

The text presents ten elements of financial statements: revenues, expenses, gains, losses, assets, liabilities, equity, investments by owners, distributions to owners, and comprehensive income. [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Elements of Financial Statements”)

Assets are resources owned or controlled by an entity that have economic value. Liabilities are obligations to outside parties. Equity is the residual claim after liabilities are subtracted from assets. [AP] (Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.2)

Revenues arise from the primary activities of the business, whereas gains arise from incidental or peripheral transactions. Expenses are the costs of producing revenue; losses are reductions arising from incidental or peripheral activities. The distinction helps users separate potentially recurring operating performance from transitory events. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.2; Answer Key, Ch. 2, Question 3)

Current assets are expected to be converted to cash, sold, or consumed within one year or the operating cycle; noncurrent assets extend beyond that horizon. Current liabilities are due within the corresponding short horizon; noncurrent liabilities fall outside it. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.2)

Tangible assets have physical substance, while intangible assets do not. Both can hold value and be acquired, sold, used, or impaired. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.2; Answer Key, Ch. 2, Question 7)

#### Cheesy Chuck’s worked sequence

The Cheesy Chuck’s example demonstrates statement preparation as a connected process. The business reports $85,000 of revenue and $79,200 of expenses, producing $5,800 of net income. [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.3)

That income, combined with the owner’s investment and distributions, produces ending owner’s equity of $16,850. The balance sheet then reports $18,700 of assets, $1,850 of liabilities, and the same $16,850 of equity, preserving the accounting equation. [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.3)

The example makes the sequence itself a control: a value derived in one statement becomes an input to the next, and inconsistency signals a preparation or classification error. [AP][AE] (Ch. 2, §2.3)

#### Liquidity and earnings quality

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities, while the current ratio divides current assets by current liabilities. Both help users judge whether an entity can meet near-term obligations, but neither has a universally satisfactory value independent of industry and context. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Your Turn”; Ch. 5, §5.3)

The source also distinguishes reported earnings from high-quality earnings. Earnings are higher quality when they arise from sustainable operations rather than unusual gains, accounting choices, or transactions unlikely to recur. [AP] (Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.2)

The Roku initial public offering illustrates how reported revenue, losses, growth expectations, and financing decisions interact, while the Waste Management case illustrates how improper estimates and classifications can make performance appear stronger than it is. [AE] (Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.3)

## Part II — From transactions to completed statements

### 3. Transaction analysis and the double-entry system

#### Principles, assumptions, and concepts

The source introduces the FASB conceptual framework as the foundation for financial reporting rules and the qualities expected of useful financial information. It distinguishes the standard-setting role of the FASB from the SEC’s legal authority over public-company reporting. [AR][BT] (Ch. 3, §3.1)

The chapter applies the revenue recognition principle, expense recognition or matching principle, historical-cost principle, and full-disclosure principle. It also introduces the separate-entity concept, conservatism, monetary-measurement assumption, going-concern assumption, and time-period assumption. [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.1)

Revenue is recognised when earned through satisfaction of the relevant obligation, not merely when cash is collected. Expenses are recognised in the period in which they help produce revenue or otherwise expire. Historical cost supplies an objective acquisition measure, while full disclosure requires information needed to understand the statements. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.1)

The separate-entity concept keeps the organisation’s transactions distinct from its owners’ personal transactions. Conservatism calls for caution under uncertainty rather than deliberate understatement. Monetary measurement limits the records to matters expressible in monetary terms. Going concern presumes continued operation, and the time-period assumption divides that continuing life into reporting periods. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.1)

#### Expanded accounting equation

The basic equation is expanded to show how revenues, expenses, and dividends change stockholders’ equity. Assets remain equal to liabilities plus contributed capital and retained earnings, while retained earnings reflects accumulated revenues less expenses and distributions. [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.2)

The expansion lets a user analyse not only whether equity changed but why: owner investment, operating performance, or distributions can produce economically different changes in the same total. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.2)

A chart of accounts assigns names and often numerical codes to the accounts an entity uses. The general ledger then accumulates the individual balances that support the financial statements. [AP] (Ch. 3, §§3.2–3.3)

#### Double-entry discipline

> Each time we record a transaction, we must record a change in at least two different accounts.

[V] (Ch. 3, §3.2)

Every recorded transaction affects at least two accounts, includes at least one debit and one credit, and has equal total debits and credits. These requirements preserve the accounting equation without implying that a debit is inherently unfavourable or a credit inherently favourable. [AR] (Ch. 3, §§3.2, 3.5)

Assets, dividends, and expenses normally increase with debits. Liabilities, common stock, retained earnings, and revenues normally increase with credits. Contra accounts carry a normal balance opposite that of the account they offset. [AR] (Ch. 3, §§3.2, 3.5)

T-accounts visually separate debits on the left from credits on the right. They are explanatory and analytical representations of ledger activity, not a substitute for the journal and ledger in the formal recordkeeping process. [AP] (Ch. 3, §§3.2, 3.5; Answer Key, Ch. 3, Questions 19 and 23)

#### Initial accounting-cycle steps

The accounting cycle is presented as a ten-step process. The volume develops the first nine steps; reversing entries are an optional tenth step and are not developed in detail. [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.3; Ch. 5, §§5.2, 5.4)

The early sequence is to identify and analyse transactions, record journal entries, post to ledger accounts, and prepare an unadjusted trial balance. Later chapters add adjustments, the adjusted trial balance, statements, closing, and the post-closing trial balance. [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.3; Chs. 4–5)

Source documents provide evidence that a transaction occurred. The journal supplies a chronological record, while the ledger groups the effects by account. Posting transfers journal information to those accounts. [AP] (Ch. 3, §§3.3, 3.5)

#### Printing Plus: from events to entries

Printing Plus begins with twelve January transactions, including issuing stock, purchasing equipment on account, collecting cash before earning revenue, providing services on account, paying utilities and salaries, distributing a dividend, collecting receivables, and buying supplies on account. [AE] (Ch. 3, §§3.4–3.5)

The same transactions are analysed through the accounting equation, recorded as journal entries, and posted to T-accounts. The repetition shows that equation analysis and double-entry recording describe the same economic effects at different levels of formality. [AP][AE] (Ch. 3, §§3.4–3.5)

#### Trial balance and its limits

An unadjusted trial balance lists the ledger accounts and their debit or credit balances and tests whether total debits equal total credits. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.6)

Equality does not prove that the records are correct. A transaction can be omitted, recorded in the wrong accounts, entered twice, or recorded for the wrong equal amount without disturbing debit-credit equality. [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.6)

When totals differ, dividing the difference by two can reveal an amount entered on the wrong side, while divisibility by nine can signal a transposition or slide error. These are diagnostic aids rather than guarantees. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.6)

### 4. Adjustments and accrual-basis reporting

#### Accrual basis and reporting periods

Accrual accounting recognises revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, independently of the timing of cash. Cash-basis accounting instead recognises activity when cash changes hands. [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.1)

The source states that companies following GAAP or IFRS use accrual accounting. Public companies issue annual Form 10-K reports and interim Form 10-Q reports, applying the time-period assumption to an ongoing business. [AR][BT] (Ch. 4, §4.1)

Accrual reporting creates the need for period-end adjustments because the unadjusted records may include cash paid before an expense, cash collected before revenue, revenue earned before collection, or expenses incurred before payment. [AP] (Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2)

#### Deferrals and accruals

Deferrals begin with a recorded cash movement whose revenue or expense recognition belongs partly or wholly to another period. Prepaid expenses and unearned revenue are the principal forms. [AP] (Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2)

Accruals recognise revenue earned but not yet collected or expenses incurred but not yet paid. Accounts receivable, interest receivable, salaries payable, and interest payable can arise from such timing differences. [AP] (Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2)

Adjustments therefore move amounts between balance-sheet and income-statement accounts so that the statements report the correct period. [AP] (Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.3)

#### Three rules for adjusting entries

> Every adjusting entry will have at least one income statement account and one balance sheet account.
>
> Cash will never be in an adjusting entry.
>
> The adjusting entry records the change in amount that occurred during the period.

[V] (Ch. 4, §4.3)

Cash is excluded because its receipt or payment should already have produced an entry when the exchange occurred. An adjustment updates recognition, not the cash event itself. [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.3)

#### Printing Plus adjustments

The Printing Plus sequence adds five adjusting transactions to the twelve original transactions: supplies used, equipment depreciation, previously unearned revenue now earned, accrued interest revenue, and accrued salaries. [AE] (Ch. 4, §4.3)

After posting those adjustments, the adjusted trial balance contains the balances used to prepare the income statement, statement of retained earnings, and balance sheet. Printing Plus’s adjusted trial balance totals $35,715 on each side. [AE] (Ch. 4, §§4.4–4.5)

The ten-column worksheet places the unadjusted trial balance, adjustments, adjusted trial balance, income-statement columns, and balance-sheet columns together. It is an optional working aid, not a formal financial statement or a replacement for journalising adjustments. [AR] (Ch. 4, §4.5)

### 5. Closing and completing the cycle

#### Temporary and permanent accounts

Permanent or real accounts—assets, liabilities, and equity—carry their ending balances into the next period. Temporary or nominal accounts—revenues, expenses, and dividends—measure one period and must begin the next at zero. [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.1)

The closing process transfers the net effects of temporary accounts into retained earnings. Income Summary is a temporary clearing account used only during that process. [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.1)

#### Four closing entries

The corporate closing sequence has four entries:

1. debit each revenue account and credit Income Summary;
2. credit each expense account and debit Income Summary;
3. transfer Income Summary’s balance to Retained Earnings;
4. credit Dividends and debit Retained Earnings. [AR] (Ch. 5, §5.1)

A credit balance in Income Summary after revenues and expenses are closed represents net income; a debit balance represents net loss. Once the third entry is posted, Income Summary returns to zero. [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.1)

#### Post-closing trial balance

The post-closing trial balance is prepared after closing entries and contains only permanent accounts. It checks debit-credit equality before the next accounting period begins. [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.2)

The source distinguishes the three trial balances by timing: unadjusted before adjustments, adjusted after adjustments, and post-closing after temporary accounts have been closed. [AR] (Ch. 5, §5.2; Answer Key, Ch. 5, Question 15)

#### Classified balance sheet and liquidity

A classified balance sheet separates current from noncurrent assets and liabilities, improving a user’s view of liquidity and long-term financing. [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.3)

Working capital is the dollar excess of current assets over current liabilities. The current ratio expresses the same relationship proportionally. The source notes that ratios between roughly 1.5 and 2 are often regarded as adequate, but repeatedly cautions that industry, operating cycle, and business circumstances determine interpretation. [AR] (Ch. 5, §5.3)

#### Comprehensive cycle

The Clip’em Cliff appendix repeats the accounting process from eleven August transactions through journalising, posting, trial balance, five adjustments, statements, closing entries, and post-closing trial balance. [AE] (Ch. 5, §5.4)

The appendix’s function is integrative: no single step supplies reliable statements unless the preceding transaction analysis, recording, posting, and adjustment work is complete. [AP][AE] (Ch. 5, §5.4)

## Part III — Merchandising, systems, fraud, and control

### 6. Merchandising transactions

#### Service and merchandising businesses

A service company primarily earns revenue by performing services. A merchandising company buys inventory and resells it, adding purchase, sales, inventory, and cost-of-goods-sold transactions to the accounting cycle. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1)

Gross profit or gross margin is net sales minus cost of goods sold. Operating expenses are then deducted to reach income from operations, after which nonoperating revenues and expenses lead to net income. [AP] (Ch. 6, §§6.1, 6.6)

The merchandising operating cycle extends from purchasing inventory through selling it and collecting from customers. Its length affects liquidity and the amount of working capital tied up in receivables and inventory. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1)

#### Perpetual and periodic inventory systems

A perpetual system updates Merchandise Inventory and Cost of Goods Sold with each relevant transaction. A periodic system accumulates purchases separately and determines ending inventory through a physical count, calculating cost of goods sold at period end. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.2)

Perpetual records provide timely item and cost information but still require physical counts to detect shrinkage. Periodic systems demand fewer continuous records but supply less current information. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.2)

Under a periodic system:

`Beginning inventory + Net purchases = Goods available for sale`

`Goods available for sale − Ending inventory = Cost of goods sold` [AR] (Ch. 6, §§6.2, 6.7)

Shrinkage is the difference between recorded inventory and the physical quantity available, potentially caused by damage, error, spoilage, or theft. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.2)

#### Purchase and sale adjustments

Purchase discounts reward prompt payment. Terms of `2/10, n/30`, for example, offer a 2% discount if payment occurs within ten days; otherwise the full balance is due within thirty days. [AP] (Ch. 6, §§6.1, 6.3)

Purchase returns and allowances reduce the buyer’s inventory cost. Sales returns and allowances reduce the seller’s net sales, while a perpetual seller also restores returned goods to inventory and reverses the associated cost where appropriate. [AP] (Ch. 6, §§6.3–6.4)

Trade discounts establish the invoiced price from a list price and are not separately journalised like cash discounts. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1)

#### Freight and transfer of title

FOB shipping point transfers ownership when goods leave the seller’s premises; the buyer bears the in-transit risk and freight cost. FOB destination transfers ownership when goods reach the buyer; the seller bears those obligations until delivery. [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.5)

The title-transfer point determines which party includes goods in inventory and which party records freight as inventory cost or delivery expense. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.5)

#### Merchandising statements

A multi-step income statement separately reports net sales, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, income from operations, and nonoperating items. A simple income statement combines expenses more heavily and proceeds more directly to net income. [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.6)

The gross profit margin ratio divides gross profit by net sales. It indicates the proportion of sales remaining to cover operating expenses and profit after merchandise cost, but its meaning depends on the company, period, and industry. [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.6)

### 7. Accounting information systems

#### Input, processing, output, and storage

An accounting information system collects, records, stores, and processes data to produce information for decision-makers. The source organises the system around input, processing, output, and storage. [AR] (Ch. 7, §7.1)

Source documents provide transaction evidence and input. Examples include invoices, purchase orders, time cards, checks, and remittance advice. Turnaround documents originate with the entity, go to an external party, and return as input. [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.1)

The audit trail links a reported amount back through ledger and journal records to its originating documentation, allowing a user or auditor to trace what occurred. [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.1)

Computerised and manual systems perform the same conceptual accounting-cycle functions. Studying a manual system exposes relationships that software may otherwise conceal inside a “black box.” [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.1; Answer Key, Ch. 7, Question 1)

#### Integrated systems

Enterprise resource planning systems integrate accounting with functions such as purchasing, inventory, production, sales, human resources, and customer management. Integration can reduce duplicate entry and make information available across the organisation, but inaccurate input can also propagate widely. [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.1)

Cloud computing relocates applications and data to network-accessible infrastructure. Point-of-sale systems capture sales, payment, and inventory information at the transaction point. The SEC’s EDGAR system supplies public access to company filings. [AR][BT] (Ch. 7, §7.1)

The source emphasises the “garbage in, garbage out” problem: automation increases processing power but does not make incorrect, unauthorised, or poorly classified inputs reliable. [AP] (Ch. 7, §§7.1, 7.5)

#### Special journals

The four main special journals are:

1. sales journal;
2. purchases journal;
3. cash receipts journal;
4. cash disbursements journal. [AR] (Ch. 7, §§7.2–7.3)

The sales journal records credit sales of merchandise; the purchases journal records qualifying credit purchases; the cash receipts journal records transactions involving cash received; and the cash disbursements journal records transactions involving cash paid. Transactions not fitting a special journal go to the general journal. [AP] (Ch. 7, §§7.2–7.3)

If a transaction includes cash, the entire transaction is recorded in the applicable cash journal rather than split among journals. [AR] (Ch. 7, §§7.2–7.3)

#### Subsidiary ledgers and controls

Accounts receivable and accounts payable subsidiary ledgers hold customer- and supplier-level detail. Their totals must agree with the related control accounts in the general ledger. [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.4)

Posting detail to subsidiary ledgers promptly while posting special-journal column totals periodically reduces repetitive work without sacrificing account-level information. [AP] (Ch. 7, §§7.3–7.4)

Modern software may not display paper journals in their traditional form, but the conceptual separation of transaction types, control totals, individual balances, and audit trails remains. [AP] (Ch. 7, §§7.1–7.4)

#### Technology-related careers

The source links accounting information systems to big data, data analytics, cryptocurrency, blockchain, ERP implementation, expert systems, artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, forensic accounting, and systems auditing. [AR] (Ch. 7, §7.5)

It names credentials including CISA, CISM, CRISC, and the forensic-accounting credentials associated with fraud examination. These roles require accounting knowledge alongside systems, control, analysis, and security capabilities. [AR] (Ch. 7, §7.5)

### 8. Fraud, internal control, and financial-statement integrity

#### Fraud and the fraud triangle

The source defines fraud as intentional deception or misrepresentation used to obtain a financial or nonfinancial benefit. [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1)

Donald Cressey is explicitly credited with developing the fraud triangle after studying otherwise law-abiding people who violated positions of trust in response to what they perceived as non-shareable financial problems. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.1)

The triangle has exactly three elements: incentive or pressure, perceived opportunity, and rationalisation. The source states that all three must be present for workplace fraud. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.1)

Incentive is the reason or pressure motivating the act. Opportunity is the perceived ability to commit and conceal it, often because controls are weak or can be overridden. Rationalisation is the explanation by which the person makes the act seem acceptable. [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1)

The SCICAP example describes an employee who embezzled approximately $2.7 million over thirty-seven years while controlling critical records, avoiding vacations, and restricting access. The case illustrates how opportunity can persist where incompatible duties and access remain concentrated in one person. [AE] (Ch. 8, §8.1)

#### Internal-control purpose

An internal-control system combines the control environment, accounting system, and control activities to safeguard assets, support reliable reporting, promote efficient operations, and encourage compliance. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.2)

The source also cites the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations framework as a widely used approach to internal control. [BT] (Ch. 8, §8.2)

> A properly designed and functioning internal control system will not eliminate the risk of loss, but it will reduce the risk.

[V] (Ch. 8, §8.2)

Controls provide reasonable risk reduction, not certainty. Human error, management override, technological failure, collusion, cost constraints, and changes in operations can defeat a control that appears adequate in design. [AP] (Ch. 8, §§8.2–8.3)

#### Six recurring control elements

The chapter develops six consistent elements:

1. clearly established responsibilities;
2. adequate documentation;
3. adequate insurance;
4. separation of assets from their custody and records;
5. separation of related duties;
6. use of technology. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.3)

Separation of duties divides authorisation, custody, recordkeeping, and reconciliation so that one person cannot both commit and conceal an irregularity. Documentation supplies evidence and responsibility. Technology can enforce prices, permissions, and transaction sequences, although access and configuration themselves require control. [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.3)

The movie-theatre courtesy-cup example shows that even a low-cost physical item can become a control device when each cup corresponds to a recorded sale. The jeans point-of-sale example links scanning, price validation, payment, inventory reduction, receipt production, and record updates. [AE] (Ch. 8, §8.3)

The Equifax breach affecting approximately 143 million people illustrates that control extends to digital information and that delayed remediation can expose assets not represented by cash or inventory. [AE] (Ch. 8, §8.3)

#### Petty cash

A petty-cash fund uses an imprest amount for small expenditures that would be inefficient to process through normal disbursement procedures. One custodian should be responsible for the fund. [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.4)

The accounting distinguishes establishing the fund, increasing or decreasing its authorised size, and replenishing it for documented expenditures. Cash Over and Short records an unexplained difference between expected and actual cash. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.4)

#### Management responsibility and Sarbanes–Oxley

The Enron collapse is used to connect misleading reporting, weak governance, auditor-independence problems, and devastating stakeholder losses. The source reports that shareholders lost approximately $40 billion and that Enron’s share price fell from about $91 to less than $1. [AE] (Ch. 8, §§8.5, 8.7)

The Sarbanes–Oxley Act applies to public companies and strengthens responsibility for controls, financial reporting, audit independence, and record retention. The PCAOB oversees registered public-accounting firms auditing public companies. [AR][BT] (Ch. 8, §§8.5, 8.7)

Section 404 requires management assessment and audit attention to internal control over financial reporting. Sections 302 and related provisions require the chief executive and chief financial officers to certify reporting responsibilities. [AR][BT] (Ch. 8, §8.5)

The source frames “tone at the top” as consequential: control procedures cannot compensate fully for senior leadership that rewards manipulation or tolerates misconduct. [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.5)

#### Bank reconciliation

A bank reconciliation explains the difference between the bank’s cash balance and the company’s book balance. Common reconciling items include deposits in transit, outstanding checks, bank service charges, nonsufficient-funds checks, interest or collections recorded by the bank, and errors. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.6)

Items already recorded correctly by the company but not yet by the bank change only the bank side of the reconciliation. Items first learned from the bank require company journal entries. Errors are corrected by the party that made them. [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.6)

#### Financial-statement fraud

Financial-statement fraud deliberately misstates results or position. The source discusses channel stuffing, sham sales, bill-and-hold arrangements, improper cut-off, round-tripping, and misuse of special-purpose entities. [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.7)

HealthSouth and Enron demonstrate that fabricated revenue, concealed obligations, and management pressure can distort not only individual accounts but users’ overall assessment of performance and risk. [AE] (Ch. 8, §8.7)

The chapter’s central control position is that reliable financial statements depend on the interaction of ethical leadership, documented procedures, divided responsibilities, independent review, secure systems, and accountable external assurance. [AP] (Ch. 8, §§8.1–8.7)

## Part IV — Recognition and measurement of assets and revenue

### 9. Receivables and revenue recognition

#### Five-step revenue-recognition model

The source grounds revenue recognition in the satisfaction of a performance obligation rather than the receipt of cash. It explicitly associates the following process with the FASB. [AR][BT] (Ch. 9, §9.1)

> 1. Identify the contract with the customers.
> 2. Identify the separate performance obligations in the contract.
> 3. Determine the transaction price.
> 4. Allocate the transaction price to the separate performance obligations.
> 5. Recognize revenue when each performance obligation is satisfied.

[V] (Ch. 9, §9.1)

A six-month, $600 landscaping contract is used to show revenue recognised at $100 per month as service is provided, even though the customer pays at the end. A $200 retail equipment sale satisfies its obligation at delivery, so all five steps effectively occur together. [AE] (Ch. 9, §9.1)

The matching principle complements revenue recognition by placing costs associated with generating revenue in the same period and recognising other expenses when their benefits expire. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.1)

#### Credit sales and uncollectible accounts

Credit sales create accounts receivable and the risk that some customers will not pay. Credit-card transactions can shift collection risk and timing to a card processor in exchange for a fee. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.1)

The direct write-off method recognises bad-debt expense only when a particular account is determined uncollectible. The source states that this method generally violates GAAP’s matching requirements and is principally relevant for income-tax reporting or immaterial amounts. [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.2)

The allowance method estimates uncollectible amounts in the same period as the related credit sales. Bad Debt Expense is recorded with a credit to Allowance for Doubtful Accounts, a contra-asset, producing accounts receivable at net realisable value. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2)

#### Three estimation approaches

The source develops three estimation approaches:

1. percentage of credit sales;
2. percentage of accounts receivable;
3. ageing of accounts receivable. [AR] (Ch. 9, §§9.2, 9.7)

The sales approach focuses on the period’s expense. The receivables and ageing approaches focus on the desired ending allowance balance, with ageing assigning different expected loss percentages to receivables according to how long they have been outstanding. [AP] (Ch. 9, §§9.2, 9.7)

Writing off a specific account under the allowance method reduces both gross receivables and the allowance without changing net realisable value or producing a new bad-debt expense at the write-off date. A later recovery reinstates and then collects the account. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2)

#### Receivables analysis

Accounts receivable turnover divides net credit sales by average net accounts receivable. The number of days’ sales in receivables converts turnover into an approximate collection period. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3)

A high turnover may indicate rapid collection and strong credit customers but could also signal credit policies so restrictive that sales are lost. A low turnover may indicate weak collection, risky customers, or cash tied up in receivables. Interpretation requires trends, terms, competitors, and industry context. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3)

#### Earnings management and manipulation

The source distinguishes earnings management within the range permitted by accounting standards from earnings manipulation that violates those standards. Both can affect how users perceive performance, but manipulation crosses the boundary into misstatement. [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.4)

Estimated bad-debt expense offers management judgement, but the estimate must be supportable through evidence and capable of withstanding audit. Decreasing the estimate increases income, while increasing it reduces income and may shift reported results or tax effects. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.4)

#### Long-term and complex revenue arrangements

For qualifying long-term projects, the percentage-of-completion method recognises revenue and expense as work progresses, while the completed-contract method delays both until completion. The source notes that IFRS does not permit the completed-contract method. [AR][BT] (Ch. 9, §9.5)

The chapter also considers instalment sales, subscriptions, bundled goods and services, and contracts with multiple performance obligations. The recurring task is to identify what has been promised, allocate consideration, and recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.5)

#### Notes receivable

Accounts receivable are generally informal, short-term, and non-interest-bearing. Notes receivable are formal written promises that specify principal, interest, issue date, and maturity date. [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.6)

Interest is calculated from principal, rate, and time. At maturity the maker owes principal plus interest; a dishonoured note may be transferred back into an account receivable with accrued interest. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.6)

A business may also sell or factor receivables to accelerate cash collection, trading part of the receivable’s value for liquidity and risk transfer. [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.6)

### 10. Inventory

#### Ownership and cost

Inventory consists of goods held for sale. Beginning inventory plus purchases produces goods available for sale; the cost assigned to units sold becomes cost of goods sold, and the remainder becomes ending inventory. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

Ownership, not physical location alone, controls inclusion. Consigned goods belong to the consignor, and goods in transit belong to the party holding title under the applicable FOB terms. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

Inventory cost includes the expenditures necessary to acquire the goods and prepare them for sale, subject to the system and freight terms already established. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

#### Four cost-assignment methods

The source applies four methods:

1. specific identification;
2. first-in, first-out;
3. last-in, first-out;
4. weighted average. [AR] (Ch. 10, §§10.1–10.3)

Specific identification follows the actual cost of a particular item and is most workable for distinctive, customised, or high-value goods. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

FIFO assigns the earliest costs to units sold and leaves later costs in ending inventory. LIFO assigns the latest costs to units sold and leaves earlier costs in ending inventory. Weighted average pools available costs to calculate an average unit cost. [AP] (Ch. 10, §§10.1–10.3)

These are cost-flow assumptions and need not describe the physical movement of the goods. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

Under the source’s IFRS comparison, both US GAAP and IFRS permit specific identification, FIFO, and weighted average where appropriate, but IFRS does not permit LIFO. The source cites an AICPA estimate that roughly 35–40% of US companies use LIFO, with heavier use in some industries. [AR][BT] (Ch. 10, §10.1, “IFRS Connection”)

#### Spy Who Loves You worked comparison

The Spy Who Loves You Corporation example applies all four methods to GPS tracking devices whose acquisition costs change over the period. [AE] (Ch. 10, §§10.1–10.3)

Under the periodic system, the example produces:

| Method | Ending inventory | Cost of goods sold |
|---|---:|---:|
| Specific identification | $8,895 | $7,260 |
| FIFO | $8,955 | $7,200 |
| LIFO | $6,795 | $9,360 |
| Weighted average | $7,872 | $8,283 |

[AE] (Ch. 10, §10.2)

Under the perpetual system, specific identification and FIFO produce the same amounts as in the periodic calculations, while perpetual LIFO produces ending inventory of $8,775 and cost of goods sold of $7,380, and perpetual weighted average produces ending inventory of $8,902 and cost of goods sold of $7,253. [AE] (Ch. 10, §10.3)

The example demonstrates that the timing of purchases and sales can make periodic and perpetual results differ for LIFO and weighted average even though the physical units are unchanged. [AP][AE] (Ch. 10, §§10.2–10.3)

#### Lower-of-cost measurement and estimation

The lower-of-cost-or-market treatment reduces inventory when its recoverable or replacement-based amount falls below recorded cost, applying the source’s conservative-measurement rationale. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

The gross-profit method estimates ending inventory by applying an expected gross margin to sales. The retail method estimates cost by applying a cost-to-retail relationship. Both are estimation tools rather than substitutes for required physical evidence in every context. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

#### Inventory errors

An ending-inventory overstatement understates cost of goods sold and overstates net income and assets. An understatement has the opposite effects. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.4)

Because one period’s ending inventory is the next period’s beginning inventory, many errors reverse their income effect in the following period. That reversal does not make either period’s statements correct. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.4)

Errors can arise from miscounting, consignment mistakes, incorrect transfer cut-offs, lost records, or calculation mistakes. [AR] (Ch. 10, §10.4; Answer Key, Ch. 10, Question 13)

#### Inventory efficiency

Inventory turnover divides cost of goods sold by average inventory. Days’ sales in inventory converts turnover into an estimate of the days required to sell inventory. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.5)

Higher turnover may indicate efficient movement but can also indicate insufficient stock. Lower turnover may identify overstocking, obsolescence, or weak demand. Industry and product characteristics determine whether a result is healthy. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.5)

### 11. Long-term assets

#### Tangible and intangible assets

Tangible long-term assets have physical substance and include land, buildings, equipment, and natural resources. Intangible assets lack physical substance and include patents, copyrights, trademarks, and goodwill. [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.1)

The source describes patents as providing protection for twenty years, trademarks as renewable in ten-year periods, and copyrights as generally extending seventy years beyond the original author’s death. [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.1)

Goodwill represents value associated with a business as a whole and is recorded only when one company acquires another for more than the fair value of identifiable net assets. Internally generated goodwill is not recorded. [AP] (Ch. 11, §§11.1, 11.4)

#### Capitalisation versus expense

The acquisition cost of a long-term asset includes expenditures needed to obtain it and make it ready for its intended use. Those amounts are capitalised because their benefit extends to future periods. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.2)

Routine repair and maintenance costs that merely sustain current performance are expensed. Expenditures that extend useful life, increase capacity, or improve the asset beyond its original condition are capitalised. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.2)

The WorldCom example shows how capitalising ordinary operating costs can postpone expense recognition and overstate current income and assets. [AE] (Ch. 11, §11.2)

#### Depreciation as allocation

Depreciation is a matching allocation, not an attempt to report current market value. Historical cost is allocated over the periods expected to benefit from the asset. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.3)

> Depreciation is the process of allocating the cost of using a long-term asset over its anticipated economic (useful) life.

[V] (Ch. 11, Summary for §11.3)

The three principal inputs are historical cost, estimated salvage or residual value, and useful life in years or units. Book value is cost minus accumulated depreciation and need not equal fair value. [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.3)

The source develops exactly three main depreciation methods:

1. straight-line;
2. units-of-production;
3. double-declining-balance. [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.3)

Straight-line allocates equal depreciation each period. Units-of-production relates depreciation to measured use or output. Double-declining-balance applies an accelerated rate to beginning book value, subject to the asset’s residual-value floor. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.3)

Liam’s $10,000 silk-screen machine, with a $1,000 salvage value and five-year or 100,000-press life, produces $1,800 annual straight-line depreciation; units-of-production depreciation of $1,800 in year one and $2,700 in year two; and higher early depreciation under double-declining-balance. [AE] (Ch. 11, §11.3, “Your Turn”)

#### Natural resources and intangibles

Natural resources are depleted according to the quantity consumed. Finite-lived intangible assets are amortised, generally through straight-line allocation with no salvage value. [AP] (Ch. 11, §§11.3–11.4)

Indefinite-lived intangibles such as qualifying goodwill are not amortised but are tested for impairment. An impairment reduces the recorded amount; subsequent increases are not used to restore written-down goodwill under the treatment presented. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.4)

Research and development is generally expensed, while successful legal defence of an existing intangible right may be capitalised as part of preserving that right. [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.4)

The IFRS comparison notes component depreciation and the possibility of revaluation under IFRS, contrasting those treatments with the US GAAP model presented around depreciated historical cost. [AR][BT] (Ch. 11, §11.3, “IFRS Connection”)

#### Changes, obsolescence, and disposals

Changes in useful life or salvage value are changes in estimates and are applied prospectively to the remaining depreciable amount. Prior depreciation is not retrospectively recomputed merely because an estimate changes. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.5)

Physical obsolescence reflects deterioration, while functional obsolescence reflects declining usefulness from technology, demand, design, or organisational needs. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.5)

On disposal, accumulated depreciation is updated, the asset and its accumulated depreciation are removed, cash or other consideration is recorded, and the difference between proceeds and book value becomes a gain or loss. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.5)

## Part V — Obligations, financing, ownership, and cash

### 12. Current liabilities and contingencies

#### Classification

A current liability is due within one year or the entity’s operating cycle, whichever classification horizon applies. A noncurrent liability falls beyond that period. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.1)

Accounts payable, unearned revenue, the current portion of long-term debt, sales taxes payable, payroll obligations, and short-term notes are recurring current-liability categories. [AR] (Ch. 12, §§12.1–12.5)

Classification matters because liquidity analysis compares current obligations with assets expected to supply near-term payment capacity. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.1)

#### Accounts payable, unearned revenue, and taxes

Accounts payable commonly arises from credit purchases and may include prompt-payment discounts. It is reduced when the entity pays the supplier or returns goods under the applicable terms. [AP] (Ch. 12, §§12.1–12.2)

Unearned revenue is a liability because the entity has received consideration before supplying the promised goods or services. It becomes revenue as the performance obligation is satisfied. [AP] (Ch. 12, §§12.1–12.2)

Sales taxes collected from customers are obligations to the taxing authority, not revenue or an operating expense of the seller. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.2)

The current portion of a long-term note is the principal due within the next reporting horizon; the remaining principal stays noncurrent. [AP] (Ch. 12, §§12.1–12.2)

#### Contingencies

The source evaluates contingent liabilities using two considerations attributed to the FASB: likelihood of occurrence and the ability to estimate the loss. [AR][BT] (Ch. 12, §12.3)

It distinguishes four outcomes:

1. probable and estimable — recognise the liability and disclose it;
2. probable but not estimable — disclose without recognising an amount;
3. reasonably possible — disclose;
4. remote — ordinarily neither recognise nor disclose. [AR] (Ch. 12, §12.3)

Warranty liabilities are probable and estimable when experience supports an estimate. Matching requires recognition of expected warranty expense in the period of the related sale rather than only when repairs occur. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.3)

The IFRS comparison describes “provisions” and notes that the probability threshold used in IFRS application differs from the higher practical threshold often associated with US GAAP. [AR][BT] (Ch. 12, §12.3, “IFRS Connection”)

#### Short-term notes

A short-term note payable is a formal promise to repay principal, usually with interest, within the current classification period. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.4)

Interest expense accrues with the passage of time, so a note spanning reporting dates may require an adjusting entry even though the cash is not paid until maturity. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.4)

#### Payroll

Gross pay is an employee’s total earnings before deductions; net pay is the amount remaining after required and voluntary deductions. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.5)

Involuntary deductions include applicable federal, state, and local income taxes and the employee share of FICA. Voluntary deductions can include insurance, retirement contributions, union dues, and charitable amounts. [AR] (Ch. 12, §12.5)

The source states employee FICA rates of 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, with an additional 0.9% Medicare tax above the applicable threshold. Employers match the ordinary Social Security and Medicare amounts and separately incur federal and state unemployment taxes. [AR] (Ch. 12, §12.5)

Payroll accounting consequently records employee compensation expense, withheld liabilities, employer payroll-tax expense, net cash payment, and later remittance to the relevant parties. [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.5)

### 13. Long-term liabilities

#### Debt and its contractual forms

Long-term debt supplies capital without transferring ownership but creates enforceable repayment and interest obligations. Notes and bonds differ in form and marketability, while both establish creditor claims senior to owners’ residual claims. [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1)

A promissory note states the principal, rate, term, payment structure, and other conditions. A fully amortised note uses periodic payments containing both interest and principal so that the carrying balance reaches zero at maturity. [AP] (Ch. 13, §§13.1–13.2)

A bond indenture defines the bond issue’s face or principal amount, stated or coupon interest rate, maturity, security, and contractual features. [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1)

The source distinguishes unsecured debentures from secured bonds, term from serial maturities, and callable, putable, and convertible features. These terms allocate risk and flexibility differently between issuer and investor. [AR] (Ch. 13, §13.1)

#### Simple and compound interest

Simple interest is calculated on principal alone. Compound interest incorporates previously accumulated interest into later calculations. More frequent compounding increases the effective amount relative to the same nominal annual rate compounded less often. [AP] (Ch. 13, §§13.1, 13.4)

Time-value tables and present-value calculations translate future principal and interest cash flows into a current bond price using the market yield. [AP] (Ch. 13, §§13.1, 13.4; Appendix B)

#### Bond pricing

A bond sells at par when its stated rate equals the market rate at issuance. It sells at a premium when the stated rate exceeds the market rate and at a discount when the stated rate is below the market rate. [AR] (Ch. 13, §13.1)

> there is an inverse relationship between the market rate of interest and the price of the bond. That is, when the market interest rate increases, the price of the bond decreases.

[V] (Ch. 13, §13.4, “Summary of Bond Principles”)

The price adjustment compensates investors because the bond’s stated cash payment does not change when market yields change. [AP] (Ch. 13, §§13.1, 13.4)

#### Effective-interest amortisation

Under the effective-interest method, interest expense equals the bond’s beginning carrying value multiplied by the market rate at issuance. Cash interest equals face value multiplied by the stated rate. The difference amortises the discount or premium. [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.2)

Discount amortisation increases carrying value and interest expense relative to cash paid. Premium amortisation reduces carrying value and interest expense relative to cash paid. In both cases, carrying value converges on face value by maturity. [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.2)

The source states that US GAAP requires the effective-interest method unless the difference from straight-line amortisation is immaterial, while IFRS requires the effective-interest method. [AR][BT] (Ch. 13, §13.2, “IFRS Connection”)

#### Three-stage bond life cycle

Bond accounting follows three stages:

1. receipt of cash and recognition of the bond at issuance;
2. periodic interest payments and discount or premium amortisation;
3. repayment of face value at maturity. [AR] (Ch. 13, §13.3)

Early retirement requires removing the bond and related premium or discount and comparing the reacquisition price with carrying value to determine a gain or loss. [AP] (Ch. 13, §§13.3–13.4)

### 14. Corporation accounting

#### Corporate form

A corporation is a legal entity separate from its owners. Its continuity and ownership transfer do not depend on the participation of a particular shareholder in the way a sole proprietorship or partnership depends on its owners. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

The source gives five principal advantages:

1. separate legal existence;
2. limited liability;
3. transferable ownership;
4. continuing existence;
5. comparatively easy access to capital. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

Disadvantages include organisation costs, regulation, and taxation, including the possibility that corporate income and shareholder dividends are taxed separately. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

#### Six incorporation steps

The incorporation process is described through six steps:

1. select an available corporate name;
2. prepare the charter or articles of incorporation;
3. file the documents and pay the required fees;
4. hold an organisational meeting;
5. appoint the board and establish governance;
6. adopt bylaws and establish share terms, including par value where applicable. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

#### Securities markets and SEC responsibilities

An initial public offering is a company’s first public sale of shares. Privately held companies have restricted ownership, while publicly traded companies’ shares can trade in securities markets after issuance. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

The source presents five SEC responsibilities: informing and protecting investors, facilitating capital formation, enforcing federal securities laws, regulating securities markets, and providing data. [AR][BT] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

Investment bankers help an issuer structure and distribute securities. The primary market transfers funds to the issuer, while subsequent investor-to-investor trading occurs in the secondary market. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

#### Share classifications and rights

Common and preferred stock represent ownership but can carry different voting, dividend, and liquidation rights. Preferred stock commonly receives dividend and liquidation priority, while common stock retains the residual interest. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

The source gives common shareholders four principal rights:

1. vote in corporate governance;
2. receive declared dividends;
3. share residually in liquidation;
4. preserve proportionate ownership through a pre-emptive right where applicable. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.1)

Authorised shares are permitted by the charter. Issued shares have been sold or otherwise transferred by the corporation. Outstanding shares are issued shares held outside the corporation; treasury shares have been reacquired. [AP] (Ch. 14, §§14.1–14.2)

Par value is a nominal legal amount rather than market value. No-par shares may have a stated value assigned by the board. Amounts received above par or stated value are recorded as additional paid-in capital. [AP] (Ch. 14, §§14.1–14.2)

#### Issuance and treasury stock

Shares issued for cash are recorded at the cash received, divided between the par or stated-value account and additional paid-in capital where necessary. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2)

Shares issued for property or services are measured using the more reliably determinable fair value of the consideration received or shares issued. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2)

Under the cost method, treasury stock is a contra-equity account recorded at reacquisition cost. Reissuance above cost increases paid-in capital from treasury stock; reissuance below cost first uses that paid-in-capital balance and then retained earnings if required. Treasury-share transactions do not create income-statement gains or losses. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2)

#### Dividends and splits

The source discusses cash, property, and stock dividends, together with stock splits and reverse stock splits. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.3)

A cash dividend passes through declaration, record, and payment dates. Declaration creates the liability; the record date identifies eligible shareholders without a journal entry; payment settles the liability. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.3)

> Dividends are a distribution of a corporation’s earnings. They are not considered expenses, and they are not reported on the income statement.

[V] (Ch. 14, §14.3)

A small stock dividend—described as below 25% of outstanding shares—is recorded using market value. A large stock dividend—above 25%—uses par or stated value. [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.3)

A stock split changes shares outstanding and par value per share without a journal entry or change in total equity. A reverse split reduces the share count and increases the per-share amount proportionally. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.3)

#### Owners’ equity and retained earnings

Corporate owners’ equity contains contributed capital and earned capital. Contributed capital arises from shareholder investment; retained earnings accumulates income not distributed as dividends. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.4)

Restricted retained earnings is unavailable for dividends because of a legal or contractual restriction. Appropriated retained earnings reflects a board-designated internal purpose but does not create a liability or segregated asset. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.4)

The statement of stockholders’ equity reconciles changes in share accounts, additional paid-in capital, treasury stock, retained earnings, and total equity. Material prior-period errors are reported through restatement rather than as current-period operating results. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.4)

#### Earnings per share

Basic earnings per share is net income available to common shareholders—net income less preferred dividends—divided by weighted-average common shares outstanding. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.5)

The source states that EPS is the only ratio GAAP requires on the face of the income statement and that basic and, where applicable, diluted EPS must be presented. [AR][BT] (Ch. 14, §14.5; Answer Key, Ch. 14, Question 23)

EPS is useful for comparing a company’s earnings with the common shares participating in them, but it can increase when shares are repurchased even if total earnings do not improve. The measure should therefore be interpreted with other evidence. [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.5)

### 15. Partnership accounting

#### Partnership form

A partnership is an association of two or more persons or entities contributing money, property, or services as co-owners of a business. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.1)

The source identifies the partnership agreement, limited life, mutual agency, unlimited liability of general partners, pass-through taxation, co-ownership of property, limited capital, and participation in income or loss as defining characteristics. [AR] (Ch. 15, §15.1)

Pass-through taxation means the partnership does not pay entity-level income tax under the model presented; partners report their distributive shares. Ease of formation, combined skills and resources, flexibility, ease of structural change, and comparatively informal operation are advantages. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.1)

Disadvantages include restricted transfer of ownership, limited life, mutual agency, potentially limited capital, and the unlimited liability borne by general partners. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.1)

#### General, limited, and limited-liability partnerships

In a general partnership, general partners can bind the firm and may be personally liable when partnership assets cannot satisfy creditors. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.1)

A limited partnership has at least one general partner and one or more limited partners whose exposure is generally limited to their investment. A limited-liability partnership gives partners protection from obligations attributed to other partners, subject to the applicable legal regime. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.1)

A change in partner membership dissolves the existing legal partnership even when the business continues under a new agreement. [AP] (Ch. 15, §§15.1, 15.4)

#### Partnership agreement and formation

A written partnership agreement should address the business name, partner contributions, authority, management and decision rights, allocation of profits, losses, salaries and draws, admission or withdrawal, dispute resolution, and dissolution. [AR] (Ch. 15, §15.2)

The source also gives three external formation steps: select the state of operation, register the business name, and obtain required licences. [AR] (Ch. 15, §15.2)

Mutual agency and joint liability create a fiduciary duty to act in the interests of the partnership and other partners, avoid conflicts and self-dealing, and preserve trust. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.2, “Ethical Considerations”)

Assets contributed by a partner are recorded at fair value on the transfer date, not at the amount carried in the partner’s former records. Liabilities assumed by the partnership are recorded at their current value. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.2)

In the Acorn Lawn & Hardscapes example, Dale contributes equipment with a $5,600 book value but $6,400 fair value and receivables with a $2,000 book value but $1,600 expected collectible value. Ciara contributes $8,000 cash. Their capital credits reflect the fair values contributed. [AE] (Ch. 15, §15.2)

#### Capital accounts and income allocation

Each partner has a permanent capital account tracking contributions, allocated income or loss, and distributions, together with a temporary drawing account closed at period end. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.3)

Partnership closing resembles proprietorship closing, except income or loss and drawings must be allocated across multiple partner accounts under the agreement. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.3)

The source presents five allocation patterns:

1. a fixed ratio;
2. a ratio based on beginning, ending, or average capital balances;
3. salary allowances followed by a fixed-ratio allocation;
4. allocation based on relative capital interests;
5. a combination of these methods. [AR] (Ch. 15, §15.3)

Acorn’s first-year $50,000 income is divided equally between Dale and Ciara under their 50:50 agreement. A later $68,000 example first allows $15,000 to Dale and $10,000 to Ciara, then divides the remainder equally; the same agreed sequence is applied when the partnership incurs a $32,000 loss. [AE] (Ch. 15, §15.3)

#### Admission of a partner

A new partner enters in one of two ways: investing assets directly in the partnership or purchasing all or part of an existing partner’s interest. Direct investment changes partnership assets; a private purchase between partners ordinarily changes only the capital-account ownership recorded by the partnership. [AR] (Ch. 15, §15.4)

Admission ends the old partnership agreement and requires a new one. The incoming partner’s capital, ownership percentage, and income-sharing rights are negotiated rather than mechanically determined by the cash transferred. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.4)

A bonus to old partners arises when an incoming partner receives capital credit below the amount contributed. A bonus to the new partner arises when the capital credit exceeds the contribution, often because skills, reputation, or unrecorded value are being recognised through the negotiated ownership arrangement. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.4)

#### Withdrawal

A partner can withdraw through a private sale to existing or new partners or through payment from partnership assets. A private purchase changes capital accounts but not partnership net assets. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.4)

When the partnership pays more than the withdrawing partner’s capital balance, the excess is a bonus charged to the remaining partners according to their income-sharing ratio. If it pays less, the remaining partners receive the difference as a bonus. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.4)

Death also dissolves the partnership. The source describes valuation at the date of death and settlement with the estate, subject to provisions allowing the surviving partners to continue the business. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.4)

#### Dissolution and liquidation

The ethical discussion cites the Uniform Partnership Act for duties of loyalty and care during dissolution and for the proposition that the departing partner’s duty not to compete ends when dissolution occurs. [BT] (Ch. 15, §15.5, “Ethical Partnership Dissolution”)

Liquidation converts noncash assets to cash, pays creditors, and distributes any remainder to partners. General partners can be required to contribute additional assets if partnership assets cannot satisfy creditors. If one partner cannot eliminate a capital deficit, the remaining partners absorb it while retaining legal recourse. [AP] (Ch. 15, §15.5)

After completing the final accounting cycle, liquidation follows exactly four sequential accounting steps:

1. sell noncash assets and recognise a gain or loss on realisation;
2. allocate that gain or loss according to the income-sharing ratio;
3. pay partnership liabilities;
4. distribute remaining cash according to capital balances. [AR] (Ch. 15, §15.5)

Creditors must be paid before partner distributions. The Football Partnership example applies the sequence using a 3:2:1 allocation among Raven, Brown, and Eagle. [AE] (Ch. 15, §15.5)

### 16. Statement of cash flows

#### Purpose

The statement of cash flows reports cash receipts and disbursements for a period. It explains the change between beginning and ending cash and helps users evaluate how reported income translates into cash. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.1)

Accrual-based income and cash flow differ because revenues and expenses can be recognised in periods different from their collection or payment. The statement therefore helps users assess earnings quality, identify cash sources and uses outside the income statement, and form expectations about future cash generation. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.1)

> Companies go bankrupt because they run out of cash.

[V] (Ch. 16, “Why It Matters”)

The source does not equate a cash balance with health. Cash generated through selling goods and services differs from cash created by borrowing or disposing of productive assets, so source and use matter alongside amount. [AP] (Ch. 16, “Why It Matters”)

The chapter identifies FASB Accounting Standards Codification Topic 230 for US GAAP presentation and IAS 7 for international cash-flow reporting. [BT] (Ch. 16, §16.1, “Ethical Considerations”)

#### Three activity categories

Cash flows are reported in this order:

1. operating activities;
2. investing activities;
3. financing activities. [AR] (Ch. 16, §§16.1–16.2; Answer Key, Ch. 16, Question 3)

Operating activities arise from the activities used to produce net income, including cash from customers, cash paid for inventory and operating expenses, and the classifications assigned to interest, dividends, and income taxes under the relevant standards. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.2)

Investing activities concern acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and investments, such as land, buildings, equipment, and investment securities. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.2)

Financing activities concern obtaining or repaying debt and equity capital, including issuing shares or bonds, repaying principal, paying dividends, and purchasing treasury stock. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.2)

Noncash investing and financing transactions are excluded from cash totals but disclosed in a note or supplementary schedule because they materially change assets, liabilities, or equity. [AP] (Ch. 16, §§16.2–16.3)

The T-Shirt Pros example shows why classification matters: recording a building acquired through a $200,000 note as if financing supplied cash and investing used cash leaves total cash unchanged but distorts capital expenditures and free cash flow. [AE] (Ch. 16, §16.2, “Think It Through”)

#### Direct and indirect methods

The indirect method begins with net income and reconciles it to operating cash flow. The direct method converts individual revenues and expenses into cash collected and cash paid. [AR] (Ch. 16, §§16.1, 16.3, 16.6)

Both methods produce the same net operating cash flow and use identical investing and financing sections. They differ only in presentation of operating activities. [AR] (Ch. 16, §§16.1, 16.3, 16.6)

The source concentrates on the indirect method because it reports that the vast majority of financial statements use it; the direct method is developed in §16.6. [AR] (Ch. 16, §16.1)

#### Five-step indirect preparation sequence

The chapter gives five preparation steps:

1. determine net cash flow from operating activities;
2. determine net cash flow from investing activities;
3. present net cash flow from financing activities;
4. reconcile total net cash flow with the period’s change in cash;
5. present noncash investing and financing transactions. [AR] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

The operating reconciliation begins with net income, adds back noncash expenses such as depreciation, reverses gains or losses associated with investing disposals, and adjusts for changes in operating current assets and liabilities. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

An increase in a noncash current asset is generally subtracted; a decrease is added. An increase in an operating current liability is generally added; a decrease is subtracted. These rules remove timing differences between accrual recognition and cash. [AR] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

A disposal gain is subtracted from net income and a loss added back because the full cash proceeds belong in investing activities, while the income-statement gain or loss is only the difference between proceeds and book value. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

#### Propensity Company

Propensity Company begins with $4,340 of net income. Its reconciliation includes $14,400 of depreciation, reversal of a $4,800 land-sale gain, and adjustments for accounts receivable, prepaid insurance, inventory, salaries payable, and accounts payable, producing $13,840 of operating cash flow. [AE] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

Its investing analysis separates $14,800 received from selling land, cash paid for plant assets, and land acquired through a noncash note. Its financing analysis distinguishes borrowing, issuing common stock for cash, stock issued as an employee bonus, principal repayment, and dividends. [AE] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

The worked case demonstrates that balance-sheet changes are starting clues, not sufficient explanations: additional information is required to distinguish cash purchases, sales, gains, noncash exchanges, and other causes of the change. [AP][AE] (Ch. 16, §16.3)

#### Completed statement and cash-flow ratios

Virtual Co. provides a full indirect-method preparation using comparative statements plus information about investment sales and purchases, plant-asset purchases, and dividends. [AE] (Ch. 16, §16.4)

Free cash flow is operating cash flow reduced by expected capital expenditures and cash dividends. It represents cash remaining after supporting operations, planned long-term investment, and shareholder distributions. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.5)

The free-cash-flow-to-sales ratio divides free cash flow by sales revenue. The free-cash-flow-to-assets ratio divides free cash flow by total assets. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.5)

A negative result can identify serious liquidity pressure, but the source cautions that a planned one-time capital investment funded from accumulated resources can also produce negative free cash flow. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.5)

The Lehman Brothers example contrasts rising net income and available cash with a large negative free-cash-flow indication and exposure to subprime-mortgage securities. It is used to show why income alone did not reveal the firm’s cash risk. [AE] (Ch. 16, §16.5)

#### GAAP and IFRS classification differences

The source’s comparison reports these classifications:

| Cash flow | US GAAP | IFRS |
|---|---|---|
| Interest paid | Operating | Operating or financing |
| Interest received | Operating | Operating or investing |
| Dividends paid | Financing | Operating or financing |
| Dividends received | Operating | Operating or investing |
| Taxes | Operating | Usually operating, with scope to associate tax with other activities |

[AR][BT] (Ch. 16, §16.5, “Comparing GAAP and IFRS”)

The World-Wide Co. example shows that permitted IFRS reclassifications can change reported operating cash flow and free cash flow even when total cash movement is unchanged, reducing direct comparability unless disclosures are read. [AE] (Ch. 16, §16.5, “Comparing GAAP and IFRS”)

#### Direct-method conversions

The direct method calculates cash collected from customers by reconciling sales revenue with beginning and ending accounts receivable. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.6)

Cash paid for inventory requires two reconciliations: first derive purchases from cost of goods sold and inventory changes, then derive supplier payments from purchases and accounts payable changes. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.6)

Cash paid for salaries reconciles salary expense with salaries payable, while cash paid for insurance reconciles insurance expense with prepaid insurance. The procedure converts each accrual amount to its cash counterpart rather than reconciling aggregate net income. [AP] (Ch. 16, §16.6)

## Appendix A — Financial statement analysis

Financial-statement analysis uses reported information to support decisions about performance, budgeting, cost reduction, revenue growth, credit, investment, and capital expenditure. The source stresses comparison with prior periods, competitors, industry conditions, and expectations rather than isolated calculation. [AP] (Appendix A, “Financial Statement Analysis”)

> Three common analysis tools are used for decision-making; horizontal analysis, vertical analysis, and financial ratios.

[V] (Appendix A, “Financial Statement Analysis”)

### Horizontal analysis

Horizontal or trend analysis compares a line item across periods. The dollar change is the analysis-year amount minus the base-year amount; percentage change divides that dollar change by the base-year amount. [AP] (Appendix A, “Horizontal Analysis”)

Banyan Goods’ net sales rise from $100,000 to $120,000, a $20,000 or 20% increase. Its 50% increase in accounts receivable requires investigation because it exceeds the sales-growth rate and may reflect more liberal credit or slower collection. [AE] (Appendix A, “Horizontal Analysis”)

Multiple periods are more informative than one comparison because a trend requires direction over time rather than a single change. [AP] (Appendix A, “Horizontal Analysis”)

### Vertical analysis

Vertical analysis expresses statement items as percentages of a base within the same period. Net sales is ordinarily the income-statement base; total assets and total liabilities plus stockholders’ equity are the balance-sheet bases. [AR] (Appendix A, “Vertical Analysis”)

These common-size percentages make organisations of different sizes more comparable. Banyan’s $110,000 cash balance is 44% of $250,000 total assets, a relationship that can be compared with expectations, prior periods, and peers. [AE] (Appendix A, “Vertical Analysis”)

### Four ratio categories

The source groups ratios into four categories:

1. liquidity;
2. solvency;
3. efficiency;
4. profitability. [AR] (Appendix A, “Overview of Financial Ratios”)

#### Liquidity

Three liquidity measures are developed:

1. working capital;
2. current ratio;
3. quick or acid-test ratio. [AR] (Appendix A, “Liquidity Ratios”)

Working capital subtracts current liabilities from current assets. The current ratio divides current assets by current liabilities. The quick ratio narrows current assets to the most liquid items by excluding inventory and prepaid expenses. [AP] (Appendix A, “Liquidity Ratios”)

Banyan reports $200,000 of current assets and $100,000 of current liabilities, producing $100,000 of working capital and a 2:1 current ratio. Its quick ratio is 1.6:1. [AE] (Appendix A, “Liquidity Ratios”)

The source warns against treating maximum liquidity as automatically desirable: excessive idle current assets may indicate resources are not being invested productively. [AP] (Appendix A, “Liquidity Ratios”)

#### Solvency

The two developed solvency measures are debt-to-equity and times interest earned. [AR] (Appendix A, “Solvency Ratios”)

Debt-to-equity compares creditor financing with owners’ equity. Banyan’s 1.5 result means $1.50 of debt for each $1 of equity, which the example treats as a potential concern. [AE] (Appendix A, “Debt to Equity Ratio”)

Times interest earned divides earnings before interest and taxes by interest expense. Banyan’s operating income of $43,000 covers its interest expense 21.5 times in the example. [AE] (Appendix A, “Times Interest Earned Ratio”)

#### Efficiency

The four developed efficiency measures are:

1. accounts receivable turnover;
2. total asset turnover;
3. inventory turnover;
4. days’ sales in inventory. [AR] (Appendix A, “Efficiency Ratios”)

Accounts receivable turnover relates credit sales to average receivables. Total asset turnover relates net sales to average total assets. Inventory turnover relates cost of goods sold to average inventory. Days’ sales in inventory converts turnover into days. [AP] (Appendix A, “Efficiency Ratios”)

Banyan’s receivables turn four times, its assets generate $0.53 of net sales for every $1 of assets, its inventory turns 1.6 times, and its inventory remains approximately 243 days. The source treats these results as prompts for investigation, especially into credit and inventory management. [AE] (Appendix A, “Efficiency Ratios”)

#### Profitability

The three profitability measures are:

1. profit margin;
2. return on total assets;
3. return on equity. [AR] (Appendix A, “Profitability Ratios”)

Profit margin divides net income by net sales. Return on total assets divides net income by average total assets. Return on equity relates income available to common owners to average stockholders’ equity. [AP] (Appendix A, “Profitability Ratios”)

Banyan’s profit margin is interpreted as $0.29 of profit for each dollar of sales. The returns on assets and equity then assess how effectively the company uses its resource base and invested capital. [AE] (Appendix A, “Profitability Ratios”)

### Limits of analysis

Common-size statements and ratios reduce disparities caused by organisational size, reveal composition and trends, and help establish benchmarks. [AP] (Appendix A, “Advantages and Disadvantages of Financial Statement Analysis”)

They do not remove differences in industry, accounting policy, inflation, recession exposure, business model, or transaction classification. Past performance also does not dictate future results. [AP] (Appendix A, “Advantages and Disadvantages of Financial Statement Analysis”)

The appendix’s position is therefore conditional: financial-statement analysis is useful when its assumptions and limitations are understood, not when a calculated ratio is treated as a self-interpreting verdict. [AP] (Appendix A, “Advantages and Disadvantages of Financial Statement Analysis”)

## Positions the source explicitly frames against

### Accounting as mere recordkeeping

The source rejects the idea that accounting is only transaction entry. Identification, organisation, analysis, communication, judgement, control, and decision support are integral to the discipline. [AP] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.5)

### Cash receipt as the universal recognition point

Revenue recognition depends on performance, and expense recognition depends on incurrence or benefit expiration. Cash can precede or follow both. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.1; Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.3; Ch. 9, §9.1)

### Debit as bad and credit as good

Debit and credit indicate left and right sides of accounts. Their effect depends on the account type and normal balance. [AP] (Ch. 3, §§3.2, 3.5)

### A balanced trial balance as proof of correctness

Equal debits and credits identify some arithmetic errors but do not reveal omissions, duplicate entries, wrong accounts, or equal incorrect amounts. [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.6)

### Adjustments as unrecorded cash transactions

Cash is excluded from adjusting entries because cash exchanges should be recorded when they occur. Adjustments correct recognition and allocation across periods. [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.3)

### Inventory cost flow as physical flow

FIFO, LIFO, and weighted average assign costs. They need not describe the physical order in which units move. [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1)

### Depreciation as current valuation

Depreciation allocates historical cost over useful life; book value is not presented as current market value. [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.3)

### Dividends as operating expense

Dividends distribute earnings to owners and reduce equity; they are not costs incurred to generate revenue and do not appear as expenses on the income statement. [V][AP] (Ch. 14, §14.3)

### Internal controls as complete prevention

Internal controls reduce risk but cannot eliminate fraud, error, collusion, override, technological failure, or loss. [V][AP] (Ch. 8, §§8.2–8.3)

### Profitability as equivalent to cash solvency

Net income can coexist with weak operating cash flow, while a current loss can coexist with cash supplied by operations or financing. Users must analyse sources, uses, timing, and sustainability. [AP] (Ch. 16, §§16.1–16.5)

### A ratio as a context-free verdict

Ratio direction alone does not establish health. Industry, operating model, trend, competitors, accounting methods, and unusual transactions determine interpretation. [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3; Ch. 5, §5.3; Chs. 9–10; Ch. 16, §16.5; Appendix A)
