## Textbook's thesis (paraphrased from source)

The textbook presents business law as a layered system for defining acceptable conduct, coordinating commercial relationships, allocating losses, resolving disputes, protecting rights, and constraining economic power. The American legal system is described as having been developed to establish standards, deter violations, provide enforcement mechanisms, and resolve disputes peacefully; its “ultimate goal” is “promotion of the common good” [V] (Ch. 1, §1.1).

Business law is correspondingly broad. It governs the creation and operation of businesses and their dealings with the public, government, workers, consumers, investors, and other businesses [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.3). The text states directly:

> “Business law is a very expansive area of the law.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 1, §1.3)

Its subject matter includes contracts, sales, taxation, corporate law, intellectual property, real estate, immigration, employment, bankruptcy, criminal liability, torts, antitrust, consumer protection, administrative regulation, international law, and securities regulation [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.3).

A second organising claim is that commercial conduct is governed by several interacting sources rather than one comprehensive code. Constitutional law establishes authority and limits; legislatures enact statutes; courts develop common-law precedent; administrative agencies make and enforce regulations; states and municipalities legislate within their respective spheres; and treaties, customs, and international organisations shape cross-border dealings [AR] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.2; Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2; Ch. 10, §§10.1–10.2; Ch. 13, §§13.1–13.2). As the text concludes after surveying federal, state, local, constitutional, common-law, statutory, and administrative sources, American law “is derived from many sources” [V] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Local Law”).

A third claim is that private ordering operates inside mandatory public constraints. Parties may negotiate, mediate, arbitrate, make contracts, allocate title and risk, organise unions, or conduct international transactions; yet those choices remain bounded by constitutional rights, capacity and legality rules, tort duties, worker and consumer protections, competition law, agency rules, and disclosure requirements [AR] (Chs. 2, 4, 6–14).

A fourth recurring claim is that law works through classifications, burdens, procedures, and remedies. The legal result often depends on whether conduct is civil or criminal, intentional or negligent, material or minor, express or implied, binding or non-binding, disparate treatment or disparate impact, a primary or secondary securities transaction, or a permissible restraint rather than an unlawful one [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.3; Ch. 5, §5.2; Ch. 6, §§6.1–6.2; Ch. 7, §7.3; Ch. 8, §8.2; Ch. 9, §9.3; Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2; Ch. 14, §14.2).

## Part I: Foundations of American and business law

### Purposes of the legal system

The text identifies five related purposes of law: establishing minimally acceptable standards, promoting consistency, maintaining order, resolving disputes, and protecting liberties and rights [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.1).

Federal laws apply nationally, while state and local laws govern within their jurisdictions and may differ from federal rules where federal law has not displaced them [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Establishing Standards”; Ch. 1, §1.2, “Federal Law,” “State Law,” and “Local Law”).

Consistency is promoted through the common-law use of case law and precedent. Judges use earlier decisions in jurisdictionally and substantially similar cases to inform later rulings [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Promoting Consistency”; Ch. 1, §1.2, “American Common Law”). Every state except Louisiana is reported as having adopted a reception statute preserving English judge-made common law insofar as it does not conflict with current state law, although contemporary American cases seldom rely on English authority [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Promoting Consistency”).

The courts provide the formal mechanism for resolving conflict, while negotiation, mediation, and arbitration provide alternative dispute-resolution routes [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Resolving Disputes”; Ch. 2, §§2.1–2.3).

Constitutions and statutes also protect people against violations by private persons, companies, governments, and other entities [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Protecting Liberties and Rights”).

### Sources and hierarchy of law

The United States Constitution is presented as the pre-eminent source and “foremost law of the land” [V] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “The Constitution”). Its first ten amendments form the Bill of Rights, while its grants of authority permit federal legislation in enumerated fields such as interstate commerce [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “The Constitution”; Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2).

The textbook groups the principal sources of American law as:

- **Constitutional law:** the Constitution and its allocation and limitation of governmental powers [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2; Ch. 4, §4.1).
- **Common or judicial law:** rules developed through adjudicated cases and precedent [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “American Common Law”).
- **Legislative law:** federal and state statutes enacted by legislatures [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Statutes”).
- **Administrative law:** rules and decisions produced by agencies under delegated statutory authority [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Regulations”; Ch. 10, §§10.1–10.2).
- **State and local law:** constitutions, statutes, cases, and ordinances operating within each jurisdiction where they do not conflict with superior law [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “State Law” and “Local Law”).

Federal public laws are assigned law numbers, published through the Office of the Federal Register, and incorporated into the United States Code [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Statutes”). Administrative regulations are codified in the Code of Federal Regulations under procedures associated with the Administrative Procedure Act [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Regulations”; Ch. 10, §10.2, “Administrative Procedure Act”).

Agency regulations normally carry the force of law when they reasonably interpret an enabling statute. Agency forms, manuals, policy statements, letters, and rulings may be persuasive but do not possess the same legal force [AP] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Regulations”).

### Precedent in operation

The text uses *State v. Wayfair Inc.* to illustrate precedent. The South Dakota Supreme Court held that a state could not require an internet retailer lacking an in-state physical presence to remit sales tax; unless overruled by the United States Supreme Court, the decision would become part of South Dakota’s precedent for similar cases [AE][BT] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “American Common Law”).

The example demonstrates the textbook’s broader structural point: judicial decisions both resolve an individual controversy and create guidance for later cases within the relevant jurisdiction [AR] (Ch. 1, §§1.1–1.2).

## Part II: Disputes and dispute settlement

### Negotiation

Negotiation is treated as the simplest form of alternative dispute resolution because it does not require an outside participant [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1). Borrowing a definition from *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, the text defines it as:

> “process by which parties with nonidentical preferences allocate resources through interpersonal activity and joint decision making.”
>
> [V][BT] (Ch. 2, §2.1)

Negotiation may be dyadic, involving two parties, or conducted among a larger group. Group negotiation is generally portrayed as more complex and time-consuming [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Types and Objectives”).

The text distinguishes relational goals—building, maintaining, or repairing a relationship—from outcome goals directed at a specific result [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Types and Objectives”). Continuing relationships tend to favour integrative or “win-win” approaches, whereas one-time transactions may encourage distributive, zero-sum bargaining [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Types and Objectives”).

The Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument supplies five broad styles, organised by concern for self and concern for the other party [BT] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Style”):

- **Forcing:** high self-concern and low concern for the other party; it is the style most closely associated with zero-sum thinking [AP].
- **Collaborating:** high concern for both sides and an effort to create mutual gains [AP].
- **Compromising:** partial concern for both sides, often involving trade-offs over differently valued issues [AP].
- **Avoiding:** low concern for both sides and an effort not to negotiate [AP].
- **Yielding:** low self-concern and high concern for the other party, sometimes reflecting an unequal power relationship [AP].

The car-dealership example shows how forcing can secure an immediate advantage on a trade-in yet damage repeat business, reviews, and referrals [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Style”). Apple’s response to Chinese criticism of its warranty practices illustrates a conciliatory approach: Tim Cook apologised, acknowledged inadequate communication, and announced corrective actions, which the text says helped defuse a dispute that might otherwise have produced litigation [AE][BT] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Styles in Practice”).

The Federal Arbitration Act establishes a national policy favouring enforcement of contractual arbitration agreements; parties accepting binding arbitration relinquish recourse to ordinary court adjudication of the dispute [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Laws”). The Uniform Arbitration Act sought greater consistency among states but is reported as having been adopted in some form by about thirty-five states [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Laws”).

### Mediation

Mediation introduces a trained, impartial third party whose task is to assist settlement. The mediator facilitates rather than imposes the outcome [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.2; Ch. 2, §2.3). Courts, agencies, and private services may supply mediators, and courts often recommend mediation before trial, particularly for small claims [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Court or Agency-Connected Mediation”).

The text contrasts mediation’s forward-looking, solution-oriented character with litigation’s reconstruction of past events through discovery, testimony, and expert evidence [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.2). Its stated advantages are:

- **Cost and time:** Chevron reportedly resolved one dispute through internal mediation for $25,000, compared with estimated external legal costs of $700,000 and estimated litigation costs of $2.5 million [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.2).
- **Confidentiality:** mediation can preserve privacy where court proceedings would create a public record [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Other Benefits of Mediation”).
- **Creativity:** a mediator may identify cultural differences or options the parties have not considered [AP].
- **Control:** the parties retain greater influence over the result than they would after submitting the matter to a judge [AP].

The mediator must establish rapport quickly and act as more than an administrator. A Harvard Program on Negotiation account reports participants remembering mediators “opening the room, making coffee, and getting everyone introduced” [V][BT] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Role of the Mediator”).

The six-stage process borrowed from Nolo consists of the mediator’s opening statement, opening statements by plaintiff and defendant, joint discussion, private caucuses, joint negotiation, and closure [AR][BT] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Steps of Mediation”). Closure records either an agreement or the inability to agree and may identify a further meeting or escalation to court [AP].

The ethics discussion contrasts consequentialist reasoning, which evaluates an act through its effects in the circumstances, with deontological reasoning, which evaluates the act itself [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Ethical Issues”). An accountant withholding information to protect retirees is used to illustrate consequentialist reasoning [AE]. A parent parking in an accessible space while seeking a thermometer for a sick child illustrates the categorical-imperative question of whether the same action could be willed as a universal rule [AE][BT].

The text anticipates technological and psychological extensions to mediation. E-mediation can bridge geographic distance and give parties time to “cool down” while communicating in writing; eBay is named as a high-volume user [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Future Directions in Mediation”). Canine-assisted mediation is presented as an emerging attempt to use therapy animals’ effects on human stress markers [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.2, “Future Directions in Mediation”).

### Arbitration

The American Bar Association definition describes arbitration as a “private process where disputing parties agree that one or several individuals can make a decision about the dispute after receiving evidence and hearing arguments” [V][BT] (Ch. 2, §2.3). Unlike a mediator, an arbitrator acts more like a judge, receives evidence and argument, decides the dispute, and may grant monetary or non-monetary relief [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3).

Arbitration may be voluntary or imposed as a prerequisite to litigation. It may also be binding, making the award final except in rare circumstances, or non-binding, making the award a recommendation accepted only if both parties agree [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Types of Arbitration Agreements”).

The three principal business applications identified are labour disputes, business transactions, and property disputes [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Issues Covered by Arbitration Agreements”). Labour arbitration is divided into interest arbitration over terms for a new agreement and grievance arbitration over implementation of an existing one [AP].

The text raises an ethical objection to forced-arbitration clauses that waive access to court, appeal, or class proceedings. Although arbitration can reduce expense, businesses should not use it to hide wrongdoing or obstruct legitimate consumer redress [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Ethics of Commercial Arbitration Clauses”). The $7.2 billion recovery by Enron investors is offered as an example of the collective relief a class action can provide [AE].

Arbitration procedure ordinarily covers selection of arbitrators, establishment of a timetable, presentation of evidence, decision, and award [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Arbitration Procedures”). The commercial-lease example contrasts simple splitting of the difference with binding baseball arbitration, under which the arbitrator must select one party’s final offer without modification. The latter is said to incentivise realistic submissions [AE] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Arbitration Procedures”).

An award may be “bare bones,” stating only the decision, or reasoned, explaining the rationale and amount [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Arbitration Awards”). Confirmation converts the award into a judgment. If the losing party does not comply, enforcement may proceed through a writ of execution, garnishment, or a lien [AP] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Judicial Enforcement of Arbitration Awards”).

## Part III: Business ethics and social responsibility

### Ethics as organisational governance

Business ethics are presented as standards of acceptable behaviour that should originate in leadership and govern organisational decisions, processes, and procedures [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.1). The text’s strongest formulation is:

> “Business ethics are considered to be the blueprint for building a successful organization.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 3, §3.1)

An organisation founded on socially responsible values is portrayed as stronger than one founded on profit alone, particularly when difficult conditions test whether its values actually govern conduct [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.1).

Ethical conduct can improve recruitment, retention, customer relationships, and public reputation; unethical conduct can damage reputation, employee relations, recruitment, retention, credibility, productivity, responsiveness, and profitability [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “Why Is Corporate Ethics So Important in Business?”). Gravity Payments is offered as a positive example: after CEO Dan Price took a pay cut and announced a $70,000 wage target, the company claimed higher revenue and profit and a 91 percent retention rate [AE][BT].

The text also uses executive compensation during layoffs and the adequacy of employee pay as tests of whether stated ethics extend to the distribution of sacrifice and reward [AE] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “Why Is Corporate Ethics So Important in Business?”).

### Ethical decisions, codes, and whistleblowing

The six-step decision process attributed to Michigan State University instructs leaders to gather facts, separate known facts from assumptions, identify affected people and applicable laws or ethical standards, construct a possible solution, test it against the identified concerns, and recommend the solution and required actions [AR][BT] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “Ethical Decision-Making Policies”). The section begins from the rule:

> “No one is above the law or has special privileges when it comes to ethics.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “Ethical Decision-Making Policies”)

A code of conduct should communicate the company’s mission, values, and guiding principles and should already be rooted in organisational culture [AP][BT] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “Establishing a Code of Conduct”). The recommended content includes:

- legal rules and procedures for issues such as harassment [AP];
- value-based commitments such as environmental practices or community giving [AP];
- industry-specific regulatory expectations, including data privacy [AP]; and
- professional-behaviour standards covering work, travel, meetings, client interactions, harassment, bullying, and abuse [AP].

The code is described as a working document that should be updated, made accessible, and taught from an employee’s first day [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “Establishing a Code of Conduct”).

A credible ethics system also requires a confidential whistleblower process, a defined investigative response, protection against retaliation, and the capacity to investigate leadership as well as lower-level employees. The text suggests outside investigators when internal influence would compromise the process [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “What to Do When Something Goes Wrong”).

### Corporate social responsibility

Corporate social responsibility is defined as using the opportunity and privilege of business to improve the world through giving, volunteerism, or environmentally responsible policies [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “What Is Corporate Responsibility?”). TOMS is used to illustrate a business model that connects product sales to footwear, water, and safer-birth initiatives in developing communities [AE].

The text traces the first published book devoted to the subject to *Corporate Responsibility of the Businessman* (1953), which framed corporate giving as an investment in the future [AR][BT] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “Where Did the Concept Originate?”). It reports approximately 63 million Americans volunteering annually, with those hours valued at about $175 billion, and U.S. corporate charitable giving exceeding $18 billion annually [AP][BT].

Claimed business benefits include improved investor perception, better operational performance from environmental initiatives, value gains where executive compensation is tied to responsibility outcomes, a benevolent “halo” in consumer perception, more consistent stakeholder partnerships, talent attraction, and consumer influence [AR][BT] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “How Does Corporate Responsibility Benefit a Business?”).

The text nevertheless rejects responsibility campaigns adopted merely as marketing devices. Consumers may detect inauthenticity, damaging the brand [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “Promoting Corporate Responsibility with Marketing”). Corporate social responsibility is distinguished from social marketing: social marketing seeks to change consumer attitudes or behaviour, whereas responsibility is a sustained and measurable organisational effort [AP].

The triple bottom line evaluates responsibility through profits, people, and the planet [AP] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “The Financial Impact of the Triple Bottom Line”). Profit and participation may be directly counted, while social and environmental effects require context-specific measures such as protected acreage, housing created, people assisted, homelessness reduced, or neighbourhood effects [AE]. The final criterion is cultural alignment and authenticity:

> “An authentic cause that is backed by all is far better than one that is dreamt up purely for the sake of marketing.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “The Financial Impact of the Triple Bottom Line”)

## Part IV: Constitutional authority and protection

### Federalism, supremacy, and pre-emption

Federalism divides governing authority between federal and state governments. The Tenth Amendment reserves areas not granted to the federal government to the states, so federal commercial legislation requires a constitutional grant of authority [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “The Constitution and the Law”).

Under Article VI’s supremacy clause, conflicting state law yields to valid federal constitutional law [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “Federal Preemption”). Courts first ask whether Congress intended to displace state law. Where it did not, state-law claims may remain; where it did, federal law prevails [AR].

Federal pre-emption is limited by the powers actually granted to the federal government. Powers specifically assigned to Congress—such as coining money, establishing a military and post offices, and declaring war—cannot be independently exercised by states [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “Federal Preemption”).

### Commerce power

Article I, Section 8 grants Congress power to:

> “regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “The Commerce Clause and The Affordable Care Act”)

The commerce clause both empowers federal regulation and constrains state interference with interstate and international commerce [AR] (Ch. 4, §4.1).

The textbook portrays *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* as a turning point from a narrow interpretation requiring direct interstate trade to a broader view recognising local activity’s close and substantial relationship to interstate commerce [AE][BT] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “The Commerce Clause and The Affordable Care Act”). The Court’s reported formulation was:

> “Although activities may be intrastate in character when separately considered, if they have such a close and substantial relationship to interstate commerce that their control is essential or appropriate to protect that commerce from burdens or obstructions, Congress cannot be denied the power to exercise that control”
>
> [V][BT] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “The Commerce Clause and The Affordable Care Act”)

The text reports more than 700 statutory provisions expressly referring to interstate or foreign commerce, spanning controlled substances, mine safety, civil rights, disability, child welfare, sport, endangered species, energy, gambling, firearms, and terrorism [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “Challenges to and Reinterpretations of the Commerce Clause”).

*United States v. Lopez* supplies the limiting example: the Gun-Free School Zone Act fell outside the commerce power because the regulated activity had “nothing to do with commerce, or any sort of enterprise” [V][AE][BT] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “Examples of Federal Legislation Passed by Invoking the Commerce Clause”).

The Affordable Care Act example likewise distinguishes regulation of existing commerce from compelling new activity. The individual mandate was challenged as exceeding the commerce power, while the Act itself survived through other legal reasoning described in the text [AE] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “The Commerce Clause and The Affordable Care Act”; “Challenges to and Reinterpretations of the Commerce Clause”).

Congress’s commerce authority is organised into four areas: channels of interstate commerce; instrumentalities such as equipment, vehicles, and personnel; tangible and intangible items crossing state lines; and economic activity having a substantial interstate effect [AR] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “Police Power and the Dormant Commerce Clause”).

### Police power and dormant commerce

State police power is the residual authority to safeguard inhabitants’ welfare through measures such as zoning, building codes, and sanitation rules [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.1, “Police Power and the Dormant Commerce Clause”).

The dormant commerce clause limits state measures that discriminate against or improperly burden interstate commerce [AP]. Courts consider whether the state has a legitimate purpose and whether the interstate effect is justified by the local public interest [AR]. A Chicago spray-paint ban survived challenge because its purpose was to reduce graffiti and associated crime [AE].

### Constitutional protections in business

The Fourteenth Amendment extends Bill of Rights protections against state as well as federal infringement, giving constitutional rights substantial application to commercial regulation [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.2).

**First Amendment.** Corporate political speech receives constitutional protection; *First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti* is presented as establishing protection comparable to individual political speech [AE][BT] (Ch. 4, §4.2, “The First Amendment”). Obscenity, defamation, slander, and speech likely to inflict injury or provoke an immediate breach of peace are described as unprotected, drawing on *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire* [AP][BT]. Commercial speech is assessed under the four-part Central Hudson framework [BT]. The free-exercise clause also applies to workplace disputes over religious practice [AP].

**Fourth Amendment.** Government searches generally require probable cause and a warrant, with protection extending to businesses as well as residences [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.2, “The Fourth Amendment”). The pervasive-regulation exception permits warrantless administrative searches in industries with a long history of close regulation; the source gives stone quarries under federal mine-safety legislation as an example [AE].

**Fifth Amendment.** Substantive due process requires non-arbitrary laws rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest; procedural due process requires fair procedures before government deprives a person of life, liberty, or property [AP] (Ch. 4, §4.2, “The Fifth Amendment”). The takings clause requires just compensation, understood as market value, where private property is taken for public use; sufficiently burdensome environmental or safety restrictions may be treated as an effective taking [AP]. The privilege against self-incrimination applies to sole proprietorships not legally separate from their owners, but not to corporate custodians and agents [AP].

## Part V: Criminal and civil liability

### White-collar and business crime

The text defines the central category as follows:

> “White collar crimes are characterized by deceit, concealment, or violation of trust.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 5, §5.1, “White Collar Crime”)

They are associated with business professionals and commonly involve fraud motivated by financial gain or fear of losing status, money, or property [AP]. Fraud is the intentional misrepresentation of material facts for monetary gain and does not depend on violence or threats [AP].

The chapter surveys several forms:

- **Pump-and-dump schemes:** participants inflate stock value through false information and sell after the price rises; Jeremiah, Jehu, and Adam Hand are used as an example [AE] (Ch. 5, §5.1, “White Collar Crime”).
- **Ponzi schemes:** earlier investors receive returns funded by new investors rather than market performance; Bernie Madoff’s scheme is the principal example [AE] (Ch. 5, §5.1, “Types of Business Crimes”).
- **Larceny and embezzlement:** larceny is unlawful taking of another’s property, while embezzlement is conversion of property entrusted to the offender; Sujata Sachdeva’s $34 million theft from Koss Corporation illustrates embezzlement [AP][AE].
- **Environmental crime:** the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability Act, and Endangered Species Act may carry criminal penalties [AP]. International Petroleum Corporation’s altered samples and undisclosed hazardous-waste shipments illustrate corporate environmental offences [AE].
- **Antitrust crime:** conduct that restrains trade or creates abusive market control may attract criminal liability under statutes such as the Sherman Act [AP].
- **Racketeering:** RICO reaches loan-sharking, money laundering, blackmail, organised crime, and alleged racketeering by otherwise legitimate commercial enterprises [AP].
- **Bribery:** offering or accepting money, goods, services, information, or another benefit in exchange for favourable action; the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act prohibits corrupt payments by U.S. companies to foreign officials [AP].
- **Money laundering:** processing criminal proceeds through legitimate businesses so that the money appears lawful [AP].
- **Spam:** unsolicited commercial messages may violate laws requiring truthful sender identity, non-misleading subject lines, and disclosure of contact and commercial-source information [AP].

The text cautions that accusation is not conviction: guilt must be established before punishment, even though an accusation can harm the individual, organisation, customers, and community [AR] (Ch. 5, §5.1, “Conclusion”).

### Criminal elements and procedure

Criminal liability ordinarily requires both *mens rea*, the guilty mental state, and *actus reus*, the prohibited act [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Components of Crime”). The creating statute should specify each component and classify the offence as a felony, misdemeanour, or gross misdemeanour [AP].

The prosecutor begins with an information; arraignment establishes identity, informs the defendant of the charge, and receives the plea; discovery and trial follow [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Criminal Procedures”). Conviction requires unanimous proof beyond a reasonable doubt [AP].

The chapter highlights constitutional disputes over evidence obtained without probable cause, custodial questioning without Miranda warnings, access to counsel, and state protections exceeding the federal minimum [AR][BT] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Constitutional Rights”).

### Civil and criminal comparison

Criminal law addresses offences against the public, society, or state; civil law addresses injury to private rights in fields such as contracts, property, family relations, libel, and slander [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Criminal and Civil Law”).

Criminal cases are initiated by government prosecutors, while civil cases are initiated by injured private parties [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Initiation and Roles”). A criminal defendant is convicted as guilty; a civil defendant is found liable [AP].

The criminal burden is proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Civil liability ordinarily requires a preponderance of the evidence—more likely than not [AP]. Criminal defendants have a right to counsel and may receive appointed representation; civil litigants generally secure their own lawyers [AP].

Criminal sanctions commonly include imprisonment and fines. Civil remedies include damages and injunctions ordering action or restraint [AP]. *Liebeck v. McDonald’s* is used to illustrate civil negligence and compensatory damages [AE][BT]. Oliver Schmidt’s seven-year sentence for Volkswagen-related fraud and Clean Air Act violations illustrates individual criminal exposure arising from business conduct [AE] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Initiation and Roles”).

Professional negligence, or malpractice, applies a profession-specific standard of reasonable care, skill, diligence, and knowledge. It may apply to attorneys, health professionals, accountants, architects, engineers, surveyors, insurance brokers, real-estate professionals, and clergy [AP] (Ch. 5, §5.2, “Professional Negligence”).

## Part VI: The tort system

### Function and classifications

A tort is a civil wrong that causes another person or entity loss. Tort litigation seeks a remedy for that loss rather than criminal punishment [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Torts”). The lower civil burden means a finding of tort liability does not itself establish criminal guilt [AP].

The tortfeasor becomes the defendant, while the injured party files as plaintiff [AP]. The judge determines issues of law, including duty and legal defences; the jury determines facts, consequences, and damages [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Torts”).

The text divides torts principally into intentional torts and negligence. Intentional torts arise from conscious, purposeful conduct; negligence arises from failure to exercise legally required care [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Harm”).

### Intentional torts

Intent does not always require a purpose to cause the final injury; knowledge that harm may occur can be sufficient in the circumstances described by the text [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Intentional Torts”). Consent, defence of property, self-defence, and defence of another may excuse conduct [AP].

Named intentional torts include:

- **Assault:** causing reasonable apprehension of an intentional harmful act [AP].
- **Malicious prosecution:** initiating a groundless criminal complaint [AP].
- **Defamation:** intentionally creating and promoting malicious falsehoods; slander is spoken and libel is written or otherwise recorded [AP].
- **Invasion of privacy:** unwanted publication of negative personal information, with standards affected by whether the subject is a public figure [AP].

### Negligence and foreseeability

Negligence is both a cause of action and conduct falling below the reasonable standard of care [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Negligence”). Its elements are:

1. a duty to act or refrain from acting;
2. breach of that duty;
3. a causal connection between the breach and injury; and
4. measurable harm remediable through monetary damages [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Negligence”).

A reasonable person exercises care in light of relevant knowledge, experience, and perception [AP]. A special relationship may create a duty to aid, as with a nurse and patient or lifeguard and swimmer. A passer-by ordinarily has no duty to intervene, but someone who undertakes assistance must act responsibly [AP].

Foreseeability asks whether the defendant could reasonably predict the harmful consequence [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.1, “Foreseeability”). Helping an intoxicated person into a car is used to illustrate foreseeable probability of harm [AE].

### Strict liability

Strict liability assigns responsibility without requiring proof of intentional fault or lack of reasonable care [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.2). Under the abnormally dangerous activity standard, at least four of six factors should be present: high risk, probable substantial harm, inability to eliminate the risk through reasonable care, uncommon usage, inappropriateness to the location, and danger outweighing community benefit [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.2, “Strict Liability”).

Escaped horses provide the worked example: an owner may be liable for an accident even when electric fencing represented reasonable care and failed unexpectedly [AE]. Ownership of an animal known to be aggressive may similarly support strict liability [AE].

A trespasser generally cannot demand that an owner make property safe or discontinue lawful activity. Exceptions may arise where trespass is common, the owner knows of the trespasser, rescue is required, or a dangerous condition is an attractive nuisance to children [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.2, “Trespass”).

### Product liability

Manufacturers, wholesalers, distributors, and retailers may be defendants in product-liability actions [AP] (Ch. 6, §6.2, “Product Liability”). Where direct proof of negligence is unavailable, *res ipsa loquitor* permits an inference if the defendant controlled the product during manufacture, the product ordinarily would not cause the harm, and the plaintiff did not materially contribute to it [AP][BT].

The unreasonably dangerous product standard covers manufacturing flaws, unsafe designs where a feasible safer design existed, and inadequate warnings or instructions [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.2, “The Unreasonably Dangerous Product Standard”).

The principal defences are:

- **Assumption of risk:** the user knew of the defect and chose to proceed [AP].
- **Misuse:** the user employed the product in an unintended manner [AP].
- **Contributory negligence:** the plaintiff’s negligence bars recovery [AP].
- **Comparative negligence:** damages are reduced in proportion to the plaintiff’s responsibility; a plaintiff thirty percent responsible would receive seventy percent of the assessed damages [AP][AE] (Ch. 6, §6.2, “Defenses”).

## Part VII: Contract law

### Formation

The source supplies the foundational definition:

> “A contract is defined as an agreement between two or more parties that is enforceable by law.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 7, §7.1)

An enforceable contract ordinarily requires offer and acceptance, genuine agreement, consideration, capacity, and legality [AR] (Ch. 7, §7.1).

An offer communicates a definite and certain proposal made with contractual intent. Acceptance manifests willingness to be bound by all offered terms [AP]. Genuine agreement—the “meeting of the minds”—may be undermined by fraud, misrepresentation, mistake, duress, or undue influence [AP].

Consideration is something of value exchanged for something else of value and can consist of money, property, a promise, or a right [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.1, “Consideration and Promissory Estoppel”).

### Promissory estoppel

Promissory estoppel is an exception to the ordinary consideration requirement when one party reasonably changes position in reliance on another’s promise and suffers substantial injustice when it is broken [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.1, “Consideration and Promissory Estoppel”).

The listed elements are a legal relationship, a promise, reliance before consideration was exchanged, substantial measurable detriment, and an unconscionable or grossly unjust result [AR]. The normal remedy is reliance damages restoring the claimant to the pre-promise position, not expectation damages giving the promised performance [AP].

Bob’s cross-country move illustrates the doctrine. After rejecting another job, terminating a Miami lease, spending approximately $13,000, and renting in San Francisco in reliance on MegaCorp’s offer, he would likely recover relocation and related reliance costs when the company withdrew the job—but not necessarily receive the position or damages for the rejected alternative [AE] (Ch. 7, §7.1, “Consideration and Promissory Estoppel”).

### Capacity and legality

Contracts made by legal minors, mentally incapable persons, or sufficiently intoxicated persons may be voidable at the election of the protected party [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.2). Contracts for necessities are commonly excepted [AP].

A minor ordinarily may disaffirm before reaching adulthood, but Mary’s endorsement agreement illustrates ratification through continued performance after reaching majority [AE] (Ch. 7, §7.2, “Minors Have No Capacity to Contract”).

Mental capacity is assessed through whether the person understood the agreement’s meaning and effect [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.2, “Mental Incapacity”). Impaired judgment alone did not invalidate the patent sale in the text’s manic-depression example because the party retained understanding [AE].

Voluntary intoxication ordinarily does not excuse contractual obligations. A contract may nevertheless be voided where intoxication prevented comprehension and the other party knowingly exploited the condition [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.2, “Voluntary Intoxication”). The bank’s use of an intermediary to acquire stock for roughly 1.5 percent of its value from a visibly intoxicated owner illustrates exploitative incapacity [AE].

A contract requiring illegal conduct is void and provides neither party a remedy for breach [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.2, “Legality”). Hiring a blackjack dealer where gambling is illegal is contrasted with selling dice, an act not inherently illegal [AE]. Contracts for illegal drugs, loan-sharking, and employment of undocumented workers are identified as further examples [AP].

### Breach and remedies

A breach is an unexcused failure to perform part or all of a valid contract [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.3). Analysis asks whether a contract existed, what it required, whether it was modified, whether breach occurred, whether the breach was material, whether a defence applies, and what harm resulted [AR].

A material breach delivers something substantially different from the promised performance and permits the non-breaching party to suspend its own performance and seek remedies for the whole contract [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.3, “Material vs. Minor Breach”). Delivery of sweets instead of Christmas ornaments is the text’s example [AE].

A minor breach leaves the substantial benefit intact; a short delay where no firm date or time-is-of-the-essence clause exists may permit damages but does not discharge the other party’s duties [AE].

Available remedies include:

- **Money damages:** compensation for loss caused by breach [AP].
- **Restitution:** restoration of money or property transferred, returning the injured party to the pre-contract position [AP].
- **Rescission:** termination of both parties’ contractual duties [AP].
- **Reformation:** equitable alteration of the contract’s terms [AP].
- **Specific performance:** compelled performance where damages are inadequate, but not compelled personal service because that would constitute forced labour [AP] (Ch. 7, §7.3, “Remedies”).

## Part VIII: Sales contracts and the UCC

### Nature and governing law

A sales contract obligates a seller or vendor to transfer ownership of a good and a buyer or vendee to pay money or its equivalent [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “Features of Sales Contracts”). The six stated characteristics are consensual, bilateral, onerous, commutative, nominate, and principal [AR].

State common law and statutes historically produced divergent sales rules. The Uniform Commercial Code was created in 1952 to harmonise commercial transactions and has been adopted, with state-specific modifications, by all fifty states [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “Sources of Law for Sales Contracts”).

The UCC distinguishes goods, services, and realty. Goods are tangible and movable at identification to the contract; services are described as movable but intangible; realty is tangible but immovable [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “The Uniform Commercial Code”). Article 2 governs sales of goods and defines a sale as “the passing of title from the seller to the buyer for a price” [V] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “The Uniform Commercial Code”).

*Crown Castle Inc. v. Fred Nudd Corporation* illustrates a boundary problem: cell-phone towers were classified as goods [AE][BT]. Trade fixtures, extracted soil and clay, and crops sold before harvest can also be treated as goods [AP]. Software licences have received inconsistent treatment as goods, mixed sales, or services [AR].

The UCC imposes enhanced duties on merchants. The text’s classifications include regular sellers, people employing others to sell, employees of sellers, and entities identifying themselves as merchants [AP].

### Formation under the UCC

The UCC permits several open terms to be supplied by reasonable defaults: a reasonable price, payment when and where the buyer receives the goods, delivery at the seller’s place of business, and a reasonable duration [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “Formation of Sales Contracts under the UCC”).

Quantity ordinarily cannot be left open. Requirements contracts permit “as much as I need,” and output contracts permit “as much as you can produce,” subject to good-faith limitations [AP]. An ostensible requirements agreement may still fail where the buyer appears to have no genuine intention to purchase [AP].

A merchant’s written firm offer can remain irrevocable for three months [AP]. The source states that the mirror-image rule does not apply, permits reasonable acceptance, and says that non-matching terms are treated as a counteroffer [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “Formation of Sales Contracts under the UCC”).

Contracts for goods worth $500 or more must be in writing [AP]. Modifications require good faith but not new consideration. Unfair or unreasonable provisions may be refused as unconscionable [AP].

### Title and risk

A good title comes from an owner entitled to transfer the goods. A void title comes from someone without ownership and ordinarily cannot be cured by the buyer’s good faith. A voidable title arises where an otherwise valid transaction is affected by circumstances such as fraud, minority, or a bad cheque [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “Title”).

Entrustment creates an exception: a good-faith buyer may acquire good title where the owner entrusted goods to a merchant dealing in goods of that kind and the merchant sold them [AP]. The accidentally sold motorcycle left at a repair shop is the worked example [AE].

Title analysis concerns ownership, encumbrance, risk of loss, and insurable interest [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “Issues Associated with Title”). The UCC distinguishes:

- **Simple delivery:** title and insurable interest pass with execution; risk generally passes when a merchant seller delivers possession [AP].
- **Shipment contract:** title and risk pass when the seller transfers goods to the carrier [AP].
- **Destination contract:** title and risk remain with the seller until delivery at the stipulated destination [AP].
- **Goods in bailment:** transfer depends on whether the ownership document is negotiable, non-negotiable, or absent and whether the bailee has been notified [AP].
- **Conditional sale:** transfer depends on approval and the relevant delivery arrangement [AP].

The destroyed coffee-machine example demonstrates why title and possession matter: where the supplier is a merchant, risk remains with it until the café receives the machine [AE].

For international sales, the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods covers much of the same field as the UCC and pre-empts it in covered international transactions [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.1, “The International Sale of Goods”).

### Warranties

A warranty is a legally binding assurance that a good or service will meet specified standards, with a remedy such as repair or replacement if it does not [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Warranties”). UCC performance must be in good faith—honesty in fact and observance of reasonable commercial standards—and merchant transactions must satisfy commercial reasonableness [AP].

Express warranties arise from factual statements, descriptions, samples, or guarantees that become part of the bargain [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Express and Implied Warranties”). Examples include “Wrinkle-free shirt,” “Lifetime guarantee,” “Made in the USA,” “This orange juice is not from concentrate,” and “24k gold” [V][AE]. Puffery such as “this sandwich is the best in the world” is not ordinarily a binding warranty because buyers are expected to recognise sales talk [AP][AE].

A seller cannot defeat an established breach merely by asserting that the warranty was believed true, that due care was used, or that there was no reason to think it false [AP].

Implied warranties arise automatically from the sale. Merchant sellers may give warranties against intellectual-property infringement, fitness for normal use, merchantability, and—where the seller knows the buyer’s special purpose and the buyer relies on the seller’s judgment—fitness for that particular purpose [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Implied Warranties”).

To establish breach of the warranty of merchantability, the buyer must show a defect, lack of fitness for ordinary purpose, and resulting harm [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Breaches of Warranty”). Defects may concern design, manufacture, instructions, or warnings [AP].

The act of sale also implies good title, lawful transfer, and delivery free from undisclosed liens, subject to exclusions such as representative sales by an estate executor [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Warranty of Title”).

Buyer remedies include cancellation, reasonable and prompt cover obtained in good faith, specific performance for unique goods or inadequate legal relief, and litigation for consequential, incidental, liquidated, or—in appropriate cases—punitive damages [AP] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Remedies to Buyers under the UCC”).

## Part IX: Employment and labour law

### Employment status and worker protection

At-will employment permits termination without a stated reason unless an agreement or fixed-duration contract provides otherwise, but termination for an unlawful reason remains prohibited [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.1). Collective bargaining agreements and term contracts are the principal exceptions identified [AP].

The Occupational Safety and Health Act created OSHA to:

> “assure safe and healthy working conditions for working men and women by setting and enforcing standards and by providing training, outreach, education and assistance.”
>
> [V] (Ch. 9, §9.1, “Health and Safety”)

OSHA applies to private employers and federal agencies, while the self-employed and most state and local government workers are excluded [AP]. Specific-duty standards regulate particular work, equipment, or procedures; general-duty standards require protection against known hazards [AP].

State workers’ compensation systems provide administrative resolution and compensation for work-related physical injury, employment-related mental illness, and stress [AP]. Compensation is ordinarily the exclusive remedy against the employer, except for intentional injury, although a responsible third party may still be sued [AP].

*Kelley v. Ryan and Coca-Cola Enterprises* illustrates the scope of employment. Kelley’s injury during required team-building activities remained compensable even though colleagues’ horseplay immediately caused it [AE][BT] (Ch. 9, §9.1, “Health and Safety”).

### Wages, leave, termination, and immigration

The Fair Labor Standards Act regulates child labour, minimum wages, and overtime [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.1, “Fair Labor Standards Act”). It generally sets fourteen as the minimum age for non-agricultural work, subject to exceptions for newspaper delivery, babysitting, household chores, family businesses, and performance work [AP].

The federal minimum wage reported by the source is $7.25 per hour following the 2009 increase [AP]. Covered employees working more than forty hours in a week must receive at least one-and-one-half times their regular wage, excluding executives, administrative staff, professionals, and outside salespeople [AP].

The Family and Medical Leave Act guarantees eligible workers up to twelve weeks of unpaid leave in a twelve-month period for childbirth, adoption, foster placement, care of a seriously ill spouse, parent, or child, or the worker’s own qualifying health condition [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.1, “Family and Medical Leave Act”). It covers public and private employers with at least fifty employees and workers meeting the stated service requirements. Returning workers must be restored to the same or an equivalent position [AP].

COBRA permits terminated employees to continue group health coverage if they pay the group-rate premium; ERISA regulates employer pension plans, record-keeping, reporting, and vesting; unemployment compensation supports eligible workers who are involuntarily unemployed and available for work [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.1, “Ending Employment”).

The Immigration Reform and Control Act requires employers to inspect identity and work-authorisation evidence and complete required documentation. Knowingly employing undocumented workers can attract financial and criminal penalties [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.1, “Immigration Law”).

### Unions and collective bargaining

A trade union is an organised group of workers that advocates to employers about working conditions [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2, “What Is a Trade Union?”). The source reports approximately sixty unions representing fourteen million U.S. workers [AP].

The labour-law chronology includes:

- formation of the American Federation of Labor in 1886 [AP];
- the 1932 Norris-LaGuardia Act’s prohibition of yellow-dog contracts and restrictions on anti-strike injunctions [AP];
- the 1935 creation of the Congress of Industrial Organizations [AP];
- the 1935 National Labor Relations Act’s protection of organising, collective bargaining, and concerted activity [AP];
- the 1947 Taft-Hartley restrictions on union conduct [AP];
- the 1959 Landrum-Griffin Act’s regulation of internal union affairs and member voting rights [AP]; and
- the 1988 WARN Act’s requirement that employers with more than one hundred employees give at least sixty days’ notice of qualifying layoffs or plant closures [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2, “Timeline of Developments in Labor Law”).

The National Labor Relations Board supervises representation elections, remedies unfair labour practices, and interprets the National Labor Relations Act [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2, “The National Labor Relations Board”). Its coverage excludes groups including government and railway employees, agricultural workers, certain confidential staff, independent contractors, and people employed by a spouse or parent [AP].

Union organisation requires an appropriate bargaining unit and may involve consent, contested, or decertification elections [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2, “Organizing a Union”). Security arrangements include closed shops, union shops, and agency shops, with different requirements for membership and fees [AP].

Collective bargaining produces agreements over wages, hours, and working conditions [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.2, “Collective Bargaining”). The source states a sixty-day cooling-off period before a strike. Violent, sit-down, wildcat, and intermittent strikes are identified as unlawful [AP]. Picketing is lawful only if it avoids violence, obstruction of customers or non-striking workers, and interference with deliveries [AP].

### Equal opportunity

Title VII prohibits covered employers from discriminating in hiring, firing, and promotion because of sex, national origin, colour, religion, or race [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3, “Civil Rights Act of 1964—Title VII”). It covers employers engaged in commerce with at least fifteen employees for twenty consecutive weeks in a year [AP].

The Ann Hopkins case illustrates sex stereotyping: despite generating more consulting business than the other partner candidates, she was criticised as insufficiently feminine and ultimately recovered $371,000 in back pay and partnership status [AE][BT] (Ch. 9, §9.3, “A Landmark Case”).

**Disparate treatment** requires a prima facie case, a legitimate non-discriminatory explanation from the employer, and proof that the explanation is pretextual [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.3). **Disparate impact** concerns a facially neutral practice disproportionately harming a protected class; the employee supplies statistical evidence, the employer may assert business necessity, and the employee may show pretext [AR].

*Griggs v. Duke Power* illustrates disparate impact. Diploma and intelligence-test requirements disproportionately excluded Black applicants, and the employer failed to show an adequate job-related justification [AE][BT] (Ch. 9, §9.3).

Title VII also covers quid-pro-quo harassment and hostile work environments [AP]. *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services* established that same-sex harassment can be actionable [BT]. The Pregnancy Discrimination Act extended sex-discrimination protection to pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical conditions [AP].

Defences include a bona fide occupational qualification based on actual job necessity rather than stereotype, validated merit systems tied to performance, and non-discriminatory seniority systems [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3, “Defenses to Title VII Claims”). Race and colour cannot be BFOQs [AP].

The Equal Pay Act requires equal compensation for work demanding equal skill, effort, and responsibility under similar conditions [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3, “The Equal Pay Act”). Employers may defend differences based on seniority, merit, production quantity or quality, or another factor other than sex [AP].

The Americans with Disabilities Act prohibits disability discrimination and requires reasonable accommodation unless it imposes undue burden. A claimant must establish disability, an adverse decision because of it, and qualification for the position [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3, “Americans with Disabilities Act”).

The Age Discrimination Act described in the source protects workers aged forty or older and applies to employers with at least twenty employees [AP] (Ch. 9, §9.3, “Age Discrimination Act”).

## Part X: Administrative and market regulation

### Administrative law and delegation

Administrative law—also called regulatory or public law—governs administrative agencies [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1). Congress creates a federal agency through an organic statute defining its purpose, structure, authority, and responsibilities [AP].

Industrialisation increased economic complexity and generated demand for specialised regulation. The Food and Drug Administration, Federal Trade Commission, and Federal Reserve System are offered as examples of the resulting expansion [AR] (Ch. 10, §10.1, “Industrialization”).

Agencies affect daily life through regulation of medicine, food, the environment, finance, labour, and trade [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.1, “Everyday Impact”). The FDA regulates drugs, biological products, medical devices, food, cosmetics, supplements, infant formula, bottled water, additives, and other products [AP]. During the 2018 EpiPen shortage, it extended expiration dates for specified lots and approved a first generic version to increase supply [AE].

The FTC protects consumers, encourages competition, develops policy, works with law enforcement, and administers programmes such as the Do Not Call Registry [AP]. Its phone-scam work combines reporting, investigation, prosecution support, and public education [AE].

Congress delegates implementation because agencies possess specialised focus and expertise, but grants only authority needed to fulfil the statutory responsibility [AR] (Ch. 10, §10.2). Most agencies sit within the executive branch and are led by executive appointees [AP].

Administrative law judges manage procedure, evaluate evidence and facts, interpret agency rules and statutes, decide whether rules were violated, and award authorised relief [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.2).

### Agency power and constraint

Agencies investigate regulated entities and combine functions resembling executive enforcement, legislative rulemaking, and judicial adjudication [AR] (Ch. 10, §10.2).

The EPA example shows purpose-driven delegation: environmental pollution and the burning Cuyahoga River supported creation of an agency empowered to protect air, water, and the wider environment [AE] (Ch. 10, §10.2, “Federal Agencies”).

The AOL–Time Warner merger illustrates agency conditions imposed to preserve competition. The FTC required access for rival internet services and prohibited interference with competitors’ transmissions [AE] (Ch. 10, §10.2, “Federal Agencies”).

Agency structures vary. The FTC is organised into consumer-protection, competition, and economics bureaus reflecting its principal functions [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.2, “Agency Structure”).

The Administrative Procedure Act requires agencies to operate within constitutional due process, delegated authority, statutory limits, reasonable fact-based rulemaking, and opportunities for public participation [AP] (Ch. 10, §10.2, “Administrative Procedure Act”). It organises federal administrative functions around rulemaking and adjudication [AP].

## Part XI: Antitrust law

### Origins and rule of reason

Late nineteenth-century industrial growth produced supply beyond demand and intense competition. Large firms responded through price fixing, mergers, monopolies, and business trusts that could suppress smaller competitors [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.1, “Business Trusts”; “Unfair Business Practices”).

A business trust separates beneficial profit interests from legal ownership and management vested in trustees. Cooperating companies used trusts to consolidate market power and exclude rivals [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.1, “Business Trusts”).

Trusts could drive prices down until smaller firms failed and later exercise concentrated control over prices and output [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.1, “Unfair Business Practices”). The resulting restraint of trade harmed both competing businesses and consumers [AR].

Before comprehensive legislation, courts used the rule of reason [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.1, “Rule of Reason”). Naked restraints broadly and indefinitely suppress competition; ancillary restraints are limited in duration and geography [AP]. Courts were more hostile to naked restraints, but early treatment of ancillary restrictions was inconsistent [AR].

### Three federal antitrust statutes

The source identifies the Sherman Antitrust Act, Clayton Act, and Federal Trade Commission Act as the three principal federal statutes [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.2).

**Sherman Act.** Enacted in 1890, it prohibits contracts and combinations that unreasonably restrain interstate or foreign trade [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.2, “The Sherman Antitrust Act”). Competitive price reduction is lawful; collusive price fixing, bid allocation, and customer allocation are not [AP]. The Act is criminal. The source reports maximum penalties of $100 million for large corporations, $1 million for individuals, and ten years’ imprisonment, with possible doubling where gain or victim loss exceeds $100 million [AP].

**Clayton Act.** Enacted in 1914, it is described as a civil statute focused on mergers and acquisitions [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.2, “The Clayton Act”). It addresses price discrimination, exclusive dealing, anticompetitive mergers, and interlocking directorates where the effect is monopoly creation or substantial reduction of competition [AP]. A 1976 amendment introduced advance government notification for major mergers and acquisitions and allowed injured private parties to seek treble damages [AP].

**Federal Trade Commission Act.** Also enacted in 1914, it reaches unfair methods of competition and deceptive commercial practices [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.2, “The Federal Trade Commission Act”). Every Sherman Act violation also violates the FTC Act, while the FTC Act fills gaps by reaching other anticompetitive behaviour [AR]. The FTC or Department of Justice may seek remedies including divestiture and dissolution [AP].

Exemptions allow certain labour, agricultural, fisheries, foreign-export, and cooperative research activity, provided the arrangement does not restrain domestic trade [AP] (Ch. 11, §11.2, “Exemptions”).

The source summarises the continuing purpose of antitrust law as fostering competition that produces lower prices, more products, and a more equal distribution of wealth between producers [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.1, “Conclusion”). It explicitly rejects allowing a small number of firms to gain excessive control over the many [AR].

## Part XII: Unfair trade practices and consumer protection

### Prohibited practices

An unfair trade practice is a deceptive, fraudulent, or unethical method used to obtain a business advantage or injure a consumer [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.1). Consumer-protection law is framed as preserving consumers’ ability to make informed, rational purchasing decisions [AR].

Examples include false representations, targeting vulnerable groups, false advertising, tied selling, false prizes or gifts, deceptive pricing, and failure to meet manufacturing standards [AP].

Section 5(a) of the Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits:

> “unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce.”
>
> [V][BT] (Ch. 12, §12.1)

An unfair practice causes or is likely to cause unavoidable consumer injury not outweighed by benefits. A deceptive practice substantially misrepresents or misleads the consumer [AP]. A claimant need not prove intent if the practice itself was unfair or deceptive [AP].

### Worked examples

A guarantee must be honoured on its stated terms, and businesses may not fabricate endorsements or testimonials [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.1, “Unfair Trade Practices and Examples”).

Bait-and-switch advertising promotes one item and substitutes a more expensive, inferior, or less desirable one [AP]. Ivan’s curtains—materially different from the advertisement and supported by apparently fabricated reviews—illustrate both false advertising and false endorsement [AE].

Devin’s false claims about home surveillance, directed at customers over seventy, illustrate exploitation of a vulnerable population. The employer’s response—termination, disclosure, refunds, and allowing customers to keep the goods—is presented as appropriate remediation [AE].

Product descriptions must use defined terms accurately. The source states that “new” may refer only to a product less than six months old and that a product should not be described as wool unless completely composed of wool [AP].

Misleading prices include permanently available “limited time” offers, fictitious going-out-of-business sales, and undisclosed conditions attached to a buy-one-get-one promotion [AP]. The mobile-phone example combines a heavily advertised free-phone offer with an undisclosed service plan priced at twice its normal rate [AE].

Merchants must disclose information likely to influence the purchase, including the full price before accepting payment. Withholding a material fact can be as serious as making an express falsehood [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.1, “Failing to Disclose Pertinent Information”).

### FTC structure and remedies

The FTC was created in 1914 in response to monopolies, trusts, price fixing, output controls, and concentrated industrial power [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.2). The Wheeler-Lea Act of 1938 added a formal consumer-protection mission and authority over false advertising of food, drugs, medical devices, and cosmetics [AP].

Its bureaus divide responsibilities:

- the Bureau of Consumer Protection investigates and litigates unfair practices, educates consumers and businesses, administers the Do Not Call Registry, and addresses advertising, privacy, finance, identity protection, and telemarketing fraud [AP];
- the Bureau of Competition enforces antitrust rules and seeks to prevent anticompetitive practices [AP]; and
- the Bureau of Economics supplies analysis of the economic effects of proposed or existing FTC activity [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.2, “Bureaus of the FTC”).

The FTC may seek voluntary compliance through a consent order, file an administrative complaint, or initiate federal litigation [AP] (Ch. 12, §12.2, “FTC Activities”). It may also create trade rules addressing widespread industry practices [AP].

## Part XIII: International law and business

### Structure and sources

International law governs relationships among nations [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1, “International Law”). Unlike the domestic system, it has no single global legislature, court, or executive enforcement authority, leaving business relations dependent on decentralised rules and institutions [AR].

The stated objective is to preserve national authority over international business affairs while maximising the economic benefits of trade and cooperation [AR]. Violations may produce sanctions, severed diplomatic relations, boycotts, or war when persuasion fails [AP].

The United States Constitution supplies two principal foundations. The Foreign Commerce Clause authorises Congress to regulate commerce with foreign nations; the Treaty Clause authorises the president, with Senate advice and consent, to make treaties [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1, “U.S. Constitutional Clauses”). States cannot independently make treaties, and valid treaties become federal law overriding conflicting rules [AP].

International law’s three primary sources are customs, treaties, and organisations [AR] (Ch. 13, §13.1, “Primary Sources of International Law”).

**Customs** are established interstate practices accepted as law even without a written or ratified instrument. Diplomatic protection of ambassadors is the source’s example [AP][AE].

**Treaties** may be bilateral or multilateral. The Peru–United States Trade Promotion Agreement illustrates a bilateral arrangement; NAFTA illustrates a multilateral one [AE].

**International organisations** are treaty-based bodies through which representatives create rules and resolutions addressing shared interests [AP].

### International institutions and sovereignty

The United Nations was created in 1945 to promote peace, security, economic and social cooperation, and human rights [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1, “United Nations”). The General Assembly represents member nations, while the Security Council has five permanent members—China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States—and ten members serving two-year terms [AP].

The World Bank supplies grants, low-interest loans, and debt relief for development and humanitarian projects [AP]. The United Nations Commission on International Trade Law produced the 1980 Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods [AP].

The European Union is a regional organisation promoting peace and economic, social, and cultural development through open movement of capital, labour, goods, and services among members [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1, “European Union”).

Sovereignty allows nations to govern their territory, enter treaties, and maintain borders [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.1, “Sovereignty”). The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976 adopts qualified rather than absolute immunity: a foreign country may be sued in the United States where it waived immunity or covered commercial activity directly affected the United States [AP].

### Governing doctrines and enforcement

The CISG governs covered contracts among ratifying states and takes priority where no separate written contract controls [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.2, “Sources of International Law”). The source reports adoption by eighty-four countries accounting for more than two-thirds of world trade as of January 2018. It does not govern consumer sales or service contracts [AP].

Three doctrines structure cross-border adjudication:

- **Comity:** courts defer to foreign laws and decrees consistent with domestic law, supporting reciprocity [AP].
- **Act of state:** U.S. and English courts do not judge a recognised foreign government’s public acts performed within its own territory [AP].
- **Sovereign immunity:** foreign states are generally immune from jurisdiction, subject to exceptions such as commercial activity in the United States [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.2, “International Principles and Doctrines”).

Businesses operating abroad are subject to the host nation’s law [AP] (Ch. 13, §13.2, “International Law Enforcement”). Common-law systems develop case-based rules, whereas civil-law systems treat statutory codes as the primary source [AP].

The text identifies collective action, reciprocity, and shaming as enforcement mechanisms affecting trade [AR] (Ch. 13, §13.2, “Impact on International Trade”). Collective action pools states’ influence in defence of a shared rules-based system. Reciprocity lowers barriers in exchange for corresponding treatment but can also support retaliation. Shaming publicises violations but is portrayed as weak without concrete supporting measures [AP].

The reported persistence of U.S. imports associated with child or forced labour, despite public criticism, supplies the example of shaming’s limited independent effect [AE][BT].

## Part XIV: Securities regulation

### Liability and insider trading

The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created extensive compliance duties and potential civil, monetary, and criminal liability [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1). Insider trading is the principal criminally exposed conduct examined.

Using the SEC’s definition, insider trading is:

> “buying or selling a security, in breach of a fiduciary duty or other relationship of trust and confidence, on the basis of material, nonpublic information about the security.”
>
> [V][BT] (Ch. 14, §14.1, “Insider Trading”)

Material non-public information is information capable of affecting market price that has not been disclosed publicly [AP]. Directors, officers, and large shareholders often possess it because of their fiduciary positions [AP].

Martha Stewart’s sale of ImClone shares illustrates liability arising from an unlawful tip. She avoided a reported $45,673 loss before public disclosure that the FDA had not approved Erbitux [AE][BT] (Ch. 14, §14.1, “Insider Trading”). The example emphasises that informational advantage obtained through a broker’s breach is unfair even where the recipient does not know every underlying detail [AR].

Insider transactions are not invariably unlawful. SEC Rule 10b5-1 permits good-faith pre-arranged plans fixing future dates and amounts, providing a safe harbour where its criteria are satisfied [AP].

### Ownership and reporting duties

A person or corporation becoming beneficial owner of more than five percent of a company’s stock must file Schedule 13D within ten days, giving investors advance warning of possible takeover activity [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1, “Schedule 13D”). Qualified institutional investors receive specified reporting exceptions [AP].

Corporate insiders include officers, directors, and beneficial owners holding more than ten percent of a registered class of securities. They must file ownership and transaction reports, and companies need internal controls to secure compliance [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1, “Insider Transactions”).

Reporting companies file periodic disclosures to support informed investment [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.1, “Reporting Requirements”):

- **Form 10-K:** annual audited financial statements, risk discussion, internal controls, legal proceedings, defaults, management analysis, and other material information [AP].
- **Form 10-Q:** quarterly unaudited financial information [AP].
- **Form 8-K:** reports of specified significant events, including officer changes, mergers, and bankruptcy, filed within four business days [AP].
- **Proxy statements:** voting materials for shareholder meetings; internet-availability notices must be sent at least forty days before the meeting where that delivery method is used [AP].

### The 1934 framework

The 1929 stock-market crash is reported as destroying $25 billion in value—approximately $319 billion in the source’s comparison—and contributing to bank failures and unemployment approaching twenty-five percent by 1933 [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2, “The Securities Exchange Act of 1934”).

The Act responded to speculation, price manipulation, and sudden fluctuations and marked a move away from laissez-faire reliance on minimally regulated markets [AR]. A contemporary *California Law Review* passage described artificial securities prices as the norm and concentrated power in actors whose ethical standards resembled those of gangsters [BT] (Ch. 14, §14.2).

Section 4 created the Securities and Exchange Commission [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2, “The SEC”). The SEC has five presidentially appointed commissioners and divisions for corporation finance, investment management, trading and markets, enforcement, and economic and risk analysis [AP].

The SEC oversees self-regulatory organisations such as the New York Stock Exchange, NASDAQ, and Chicago Board of Options [AP]. The source states that companies with assets exceeding $10 million and at least 500 owners of a security class must register unless exempt [AP].

The JOBS Act permits exempt crowdfunding where no more than $1,070,000 is raised in twelve months [AP].

Primary markets concern the initial sale of a security, such as an IPO; secondary markets concern later trading among investors [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2, “Secondary Markets”). Primary prices are set in advance, while secondary prices move with supply, demand, and expectations [AP]. Facebook’s $38 IPO and subsequent trading history illustrate the distinction [AE].

Sections 12 and 15 establish registration and periodic-reporting duties, while EDGAR makes filings available to investors [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2, “Reporting Requirements”). Section 5 prohibits transactions on unregistered exchanges and requires exchange rules designed to prevent manipulation, foster fair trading and cooperation, and protect investors and the public [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2, “Registration Requirements”).

Blue sky laws are state securities laws directed against fraud [AP] (Ch. 14, §14.2, “Blue Sky Laws”). The term is traced to Kansas commissioner J.N. Dolley’s complaint that speculative ventures were backed by nothing but Kansas’s blue sky [AE][BT]. The SEC does not enforce these state laws [AP].

## Recurring legal architecture across the source

### Authority flows from source to institution to rule to remedy

The textbook repeatedly follows the same structure: a constitution or statute creates authority; an institution interprets or administers it; a rule defines lawful conduct; a procedure determines violation; and a remedy or penalty follows [AR] (Ch. 1, §1.2; Ch. 4, §4.1; Ch. 10, §§10.1–10.2; Ch. 11, §11.2; Ch. 12, §12.2; Ch. 14, §14.2).

Examples include Congress creating agencies through organic statutes, the NLRA creating rights administered by the NLRB, the FTC Act creating and empowering the FTC, and the Securities Exchange Act creating the SEC [AE] (Ch. 9, §9.2; Ch. 10, §10.1; Ch. 11, §11.2; Ch. 14, §14.2).

### Voluntary agreement is important but not absolute

Negotiation, mediation, arbitration, contracts, sales terms, collective bargaining, and treaties permit parties to order their own relations [AR] (Ch. 2; Chs. 7–9; Ch. 13). That freedom is limited where consent is defective, capacity is absent, purpose is illegal, terms are unconscionable, bargaining waives essential recourse unfairly, or the agreement restrains trade or harms protected parties [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.3; Ch. 7, §§7.1–7.2; Ch. 8, §8.1; Chs. 9, 11–12).

### Information is a central regulatory object

Several fields treat reliable information as a condition of lawful markets: contract formation requires clear offers and genuine agreement; warranties bind factual product claims; unfair-trade law prohibits misleading advertising and withheld material facts; securities law mandates public disclosure and prohibits trading on material non-public information [AR] (Ch. 7, §7.1; Ch. 8, §8.2; Ch. 12, §12.1; Ch. 14, §§14.1–14.2).

The source therefore connects transparency with both private consent and public market integrity [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.1; Ch. 8, §8.2; Ch. 12, §12.1; Ch. 14, §14.2, “Reporting Requirements”).

### Risk is allocated through duty, title, classification, and disclosure

Tort law allocates accident costs through fault, foreseeability, strict liability, and comparative responsibility [AR] (Ch. 6). Contract law allocates performance risk through formation rules, materiality, and remedies [AR] (Ch. 7). Sales law allocates goods-related risk through title, delivery terms, warranties, and buyer remedies [AR] (Ch. 8). Employment, consumer, environmental, competition, and securities rules shift selected risks away from workers, consumers, communities, competitors, and investors toward actors able or required to manage them [AR] (Chs. 9–12; Ch. 14).

## Positions the textbook explicitly frames against

- **Law as a single-source system.** American law “is derived from many sources” [V] (Ch. 1, §1.2, “Local Law”).
- **Treating litigation as the only dispute mechanism.** Negotiation, mediation, and arbitration are presented as alternatives capable of reducing cost, time, and court burden [AP] (Chs. 1–2).
- **Purely coercive or zero-sum negotiation.** Forcing may achieve an immediate gain while undermining relationships and long-term success [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.1, “Negotiation Style”).
- **Forced arbitration as concealment or denial of legitimate redress.** Arbitration clauses should not keep wrongdoing quiet or prevent justified consumer recovery [AR] (Ch. 2, §2.3, “Ethics of Commercial Arbitration Clauses”).
- **Ethics as subordinate to profit.** Organisations founded on socially responsible values are contrasted with those founded on profit alone [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.1).
- **Codes without enforcement.** A code must be accompanied by confidential reporting, non-retaliation, investigation, and action at every organisational level [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.1, “What to Do When Something Goes Wrong”).
- **Corporate responsibility as a marketing pretext.** Inauthentic responsibility campaigns can damage the brand [AR] (Ch. 3, §3.2, “Promoting Corporate Responsibility with Marketing”).
- **Unlimited federal commerce authority.** *United States v. Lopez* demonstrates that activity unrelated to commerce or economic enterprise can fall outside the commerce power [AE][BT] (Ch. 4, §4.1).
- **State regulation that discriminates against interstate commerce.** The dormant commerce clause constrains state police power where interstate burdens lack adequate justification [AR] (Ch. 4, §4.1).
- **Equating accusation with guilt.** Criminal liability requires proof and conviction [AR] (Ch. 5, §5.1, “Conclusion”).
- **Treating non-violent business crime as harmless.** White-collar crime can destroy companies, environments, and the financial stability of workers, clients, and communities [AR] (Ch. 5, §5.1, “White Collar Crime”).
- **Liability only where intent or carelessness is proven.** Strict and product liability may assign responsibility despite reasonable care or difficulty proving the precise negligent act [AR] (Ch. 6, §6.2).
- **Enforcement of illegal bargains.** Courts provide no contractual remedy where performance itself violates law [AR] (Ch. 7, §7.2, “Legality”).
- **Puffery as warranty.** Obvious sales talk does not ordinarily create an enforceable factual guarantee [AR] (Ch. 8, §8.2, “Express and Implied Warranties”).
- **Employment discretion unconstrained by law.** At-will employment does not authorise termination for an illegal reason [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.1).
- **Discrimination concealed by neutral form.** Disparate-impact doctrine reaches practices that disproportionately harm protected classes even without intentional discrimination [AR] (Ch. 9, §9.3).
- **Unrestricted agency power.** Agencies remain bound by due process, delegated authority, statutes, constitutional rights, reasoned rules, and public participation [AR] (Ch. 10, §10.2, “Administrative Procedure Act”).
- **Private restraints that suppress competition.** Antitrust law distinguishes ordinary competitive behaviour from collusion, monopoly creation, and substantial foreclosure [AR] (Ch. 11, §11.2).
- **Consumer deception excused by lack of intent.** The unfair or deceptive character of the practice can be sufficient [AR] (Ch. 12, §12.1).
- **Absolute foreign sovereign immunity.** Commercial activity and waiver can expose a foreign state to U.S. jurisdiction [AR] (Ch. 13, §§13.1–13.2).
- **Shaming as sufficient international enforcement.** The source reports little evidence of changed imports without concrete accompanying measures [AR] (Ch. 13, §13.2, “Impact on International Trade”).
- **Informational advantage based on fiduciary breach.** Securities law prohibits trading on material non-public information obtained through trust or confidence [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.1, “Insider Trading”).
- **Unregulated laissez-faire securities markets.** The 1934 framework is presented as a response to speculation, manipulation, instability, and loss of public confidence [AR] (Ch. 14, §14.2).
