# Definition

Backlog Refinement is a recurring Scrum activity—not an official Scrum event—in which participants collaboratively detail, improve, estimate, and order Product Backlog items to build shared understanding, reduce risk, and shorten Sprint Planning.

# Goals

- Maintain approximately 1½ to two Sprints of “Ready” Product Backlog items.
- Improve shared understanding of item descriptions, value, dependencies, acceptance criteria, and estimates.

# Time-box

- Spend no more than about 10% of the Sprint on refinement.
- Schedule one or more sessions during the Sprint; the guide suggests at least 1½ hours per week.
- Teams with new backlogs may need three to four hours per week to establish a longer-term view.

# Participants

- **Required:** the Product Owner and at least one Development Team member.
- **Strongly suggested:** the entire Scrum Team plus useful stakeholders or subject-matter experts.

# Input

- A Product Backlog, ideally containing items whose creators have already made a solid attempt at elaboration.

# Output

- A healthy, freshly ordered, and often estimated Product Backlog.

# Sample 1½-hour agenda

Starting from the top of the Product Backlog, spend the session refining items together:

1. Discuss each item’s description, order, and value.
2. Split large items vertically into smaller items.
3. Record the conversation as specific, testable acceptance criteria.
4. Have the Development Team estimate the item, using a technique such as Planning Poker or Affinity Estimating.
5. Let the Scrum Team propose ordering changes based on value, estimate, and dependencies; the Product Owner makes the final ordering decision.

# Operating guidance

- Send participants the candidate items in advance so they can prepare and keep the session focused.
- Time discussions and park an item when missing research or context prevents useful progress.
- Avoid detailed planning too far into the future; update the Product Backlog as new evidence arrives through Sprint Reviews and released Increments.
- Use estimation conversations to uncover differences in assumptions, knowledge, uncertainty, complexity, and effort.
- Estimate defects as well as feature work so forecasts account for their expected cost.
