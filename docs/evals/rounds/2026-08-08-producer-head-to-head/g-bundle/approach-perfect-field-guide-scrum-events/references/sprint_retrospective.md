# Definition

The Sprint Retrospective is the Scrum Team’s event for candidly inspecting its tools, process, structure, and culture and selecting improvements or experiments for the next Sprint.

# Time-box

- Hold it after the Sprint Review so observations from the review can inform the retrospective.
- Hold it immediately before Sprint Planning to minimize the gap between Sprints.
- Allow about 1½ hours for a two-week Sprint.
- Do not exceed three hours for a one-month Sprint.

# Participants

- The entire Scrum Team participates, with the Product Owner and Scrum Master acting as peers of the Development Team.
- No one else attends unless the entire Scrum Team explicitly invites them.

# Inputs

- Objective observations about progress against the Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog.
- Information about the well-being of people and relationships in and around the team.
- Evidence about the effectiveness of the team’s tools, process, structure, and culture.
- A culture of courage, commitment, respect, and openness.

# Outputs

- Product Backlog items for improvement experiments to attempt in the next Sprint.
- Changes to the Definition of “Done.”
- Where used, changes to the Definition of “Ready” and the team Working Agreement.

# Sample 1½-hour agenda

1. **Set the stage — 5 minutes:** check in, establish psychological safety, and express specific appreciation.
2. **Gather data — 5 minutes:** collect positives, desired changes, and insights as separate items, then group related items.
3. **Read and deduplicate — 3 minutes.**
4. **Vote — 2 minutes:** identify the highest-priority discussion topics.
5. **Generate insights and actions — 70 minutes:** discuss about three leading topics, explore root causes, and agree on decisions or Product Backlog items.
6. **Wrap up — 5 minutes:** reflect on the retrospective itself and identify possible improvements.

# Operating guidance

- Prefer one or two achievable improvements for the next Sprint over too many simultaneous changes.
- Use an explicit confidentiality agreement, such as the Vegas Rule or Chatham House Rule, to help create a safe setting for difficult conversations.
- When searching for causes, continue beyond obvious explanations until the team reaches an insight that can support a useful action.
