# Appropriateness

A systems mapping method is appropriate when its characteristics fit both the project and the system being studied. The choice triangulates among:

- the project’s purpose, needs, constraints, users, and available resources;
- the characteristics of the system and its context; and
- the nature, strengths, weaknesses, and requirements of the candidate method.

# Comparison fields

- **Type of map:** Whether the model is qualitative or quantitative, what network structure it permits, and how it represents causality.
- **Level of focus:** Whether it addresses a whole system or a narrower subsection such as an intervention, outcome chain, or dynamical problem.
- **Mode of construction:** How the map is initiated and built, whether through participation, data, evidence, or another process.
- **Emphasis on participation:** Whether participatory use is central to the method or one optional mode.
- **Mode of analysis:** How the completed map is analysed and what kinds of insight that analysis supports.
- **Ease of use:** How readily the method can be used without formal training.
- **Key contributions:** The method’s most important strengths and distinctive value.
- **Key constraints:** The method’s most important structural, practical, or interpretive limitations.
- **When most appropriate:** The situations in which the method is at its best.

# Combining methods

- **Sequential combination:** One method produces an output that becomes an input to another method, yielding two mapping outputs.
- **Hybrid combination:** Elements of two or more methods are brought together in a single map.

Combination should preserve awareness of each method’s constraints, especially differences in allowable factors, causal meanings, and network structures.
