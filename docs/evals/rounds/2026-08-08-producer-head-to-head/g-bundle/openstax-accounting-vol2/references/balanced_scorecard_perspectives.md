# Balanced Scorecard

A balanced scorecard translates strategic goals into a coordinated set of financial and nonfinancial performance measures. It combines measures of past financial results with operational, customer, and capability measures intended to influence future performance.

# Perspectives

| Perspective | Question | Representative measures |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | How do financial results support the strategy? | ROI, residual income, economic value added, earnings per share, revenue growth, sales growth, inventory turnover |
| Internal business | Which processes must operate well? | Defect rate, machine downtime, transaction efficiency, throughput time, units completed per employee, waste |
| Customer | How do customers perceive and interact with the organization? | Satisfaction, repeat customers, referrals, new customers, market share, on-time delivery |
| Learning and growth | How will the organization innovate and improve? | Training hours, adopted employee suggestions, employee turnover, process improvements, new products |

# Design Conventions

- Begin with the organization’s strategy and choose measures with an explicit connection to strategic objectives.
- Include both quantitative financial factors and qualitative or nonfinancial factors.
- Use measures the evaluated manager or employee can influence.
- Define the measure, data source, target, reporting period, and responsible owner.
- Balance short- and long-term incentives so improving one perspective does not damage another.
- Review measures as strategy and operating conditions change.

A scorecard is not balanced merely because it contains four headings; its measures should form a coherent account of how learning and operational improvement affect customers and ultimately financial outcomes.
