International law comprises the policies and procedures governing relationships among nations. For business, it facilitates trade without a single global legislature, court, or executive authority.

# Primary sources

- **International customs:** General practices accepted as law that guide relations among nations without requiring a written instrument or ratification.
- **Treaties:** Agreements authorized and ratified by participating nations. A bilateral treaty joins two nations; a multilateral treaty joins several.
- **International organizations:** Treaty-based bodies whose member nations cooperate on shared interests and may adopt resolutions that standardize conduct and trade rules.

# Key doctrines

- **Sovereignty:** A nation's authority to govern itself, negotiate treaties, and control activity within its territorial boundaries.
- **Principle of comity:** A nation defers to another nation's laws and decrees when they are consistent with its own law.
- **Act of state doctrine:** U.S. and English courts do not pass judgment on the public acts of a recognized foreign government performed within that government's territory.
- **Sovereign immunity:** Foreign nations are ordinarily immune from another country's jurisdiction, subject to exceptions such as waiver and qualifying commercial activity.

# International sales

The United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG) governs qualifying international sales between parties in ratifying countries. It does not apply to consumer sales or service contracts.

Businesses operating abroad remain subject to the host nation's law. International violations may also prompt sanctions, diplomatic action, boycotts, reciprocity, collective action, or reputational pressure.
