# General form

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one variable to a change in another:

$$
Elasticity_{Y,X} = \frac{\%\Delta Y}{\%\Delta X}
$$

The midpoint method makes the percentage change between two observations independent of which observation is treated as the starting point:

$$
\%\Delta X =
\frac{X_2-X_1}{(X_2+X_1)/2} \times 100
$$

# Price elasticities

- **Price elasticity of demand:** $\%\Delta Q_d / \%\Delta P$. Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, the result is negative; by convention economists usually report its absolute value.
- **Price elasticity of supply:** $\%\Delta Q_s / \%\Delta P$.

Using the reported magnitude:

- Greater than 1 is **elastic**: quantity responds more than proportionately.
- Equal to 1 is **unitary elastic**: quantity responds proportionately.
- Less than 1 is **inelastic**: quantity responds less than proportionately.
- Zero elasticity is perfectly inelastic and appears vertical.
- Infinite elasticity is perfectly elastic and appears horizontal.

# Other demand elasticities

- **Income elasticity of demand:** $\%\Delta Q_d / \%\Delta Income$. A positive value characterizes a normal good; a negative value characterizes an inferior good.
- **Cross-price elasticity of demand:** $\%\Delta Q_{d,A} / \%\Delta P_B$. A positive value indicates substitute goods, while a negative value indicates complementary goods.

# Other applications

- **Wage elasticity of labor supply:** $\%\Delta Hours\ worked / \%\Delta Wage$.
- **Elasticity of savings:** $\%\Delta Quantity\ of\ savings / \%\Delta Interest\ rate$.

The same percentage-response form can be applied to other causal comparisons, but the numerator and denominator must always be named explicitly.
