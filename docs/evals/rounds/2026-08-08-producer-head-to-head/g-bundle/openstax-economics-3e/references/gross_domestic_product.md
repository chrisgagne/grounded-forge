# Definition

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a year. It measures production inside the country's geographic boundaries, regardless of whether domestic or foreign-owned labor and businesses produced the output.

# Expenditure approach

GDP can be measured from the demand side as:

$$
GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)
$$

where:

- $C$ is household consumption.
- $I$ is business investment in new capital goods, residential construction, and inventories; purchases of stocks, bonds, and other financial assets are not included.
- $G$ is government purchases of newly produced goods and services; transfer payments are excluded because the government receives no new good or service in exchange.
- $X$ is exports.
- $M$ is imports.
- $X-M$ is net exports, also called the trade balance.

The expenditure, production, and national-income approaches describe the same aggregate: every market transaction has both a buyer and a seller, and revenue from production becomes income to labor, capital, land, and entrepreneurship.

# Production boundary

GDP counts final goods and services, not intermediate inputs whose value is already embodied in final output. This avoids double counting. It also excludes used goods produced in an earlier year, transfer payments, and nonmarket production.

# Related measures

- **GDP per capita:** $GDP / population$. This is more informative than total GDP when comparing average material living standards across countries with different populations, but it remains only a rough indicator of well-being.
- **Gross national product (GNP):** domestic production plus production by domestic labor and businesses abroad, with foreign production inside the country treated differently than in GDP.
- **Net national product (NNP):** $GNP - depreciation$.
- **National income:** the sum of income earned as wages, profits, interest, and rent; its total corresponds to the value of national output.

# Interpretation limits

GDP does not directly measure leisure, environmental quality, health and education outcomes, nonmarket activity, income inequality, product variety, technological improvement, or the social value of particular kinds of output. Comparisons over time should use real rather than nominal GDP.
