# Scope

A business structure, also called an entity selection, is the legal organization used to operate a venture. The structure affects ownership, personal liability, management, formation requirements, access to capital, and taxation.

The descriptions below use the U.S. framework presented by OpenStax. Entity and tax rules vary by state and change over time, so current legal and tax advice is necessary before selecting a structure.

# Entity reference

| Structure | Ownership and formation | Liability | Federal tax treatment described by the source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sole proprietorship | One individual owns and manages the business. It has little formal structure and generally requires no mandatory state formation filing. | The owner and business are not legally separate; the owner is personally liable for business debts. | The entity is not taxed separately. Profit and loss pass through to the owner's individual return. |
| General partnership (GP) | Two or more people or entities agree to operate a for-profit business, usually under a partnership agreement. Each partner may contribute capital, equipment, or skills. | Each general partner has unlimited liability and may be liable for obligations created by another partner on behalf of the partnership. | The partnership is a pass-through entity; partners report their shares of profit on their own returns. |
| Limited partnership (LP) | At least one general partner manages the enterprise and one or more limited partners invest in it. | The general partner has unlimited liability. A limited partner's liability is generally limited to the investment, provided the limited partner does not take a management role. | The partnership's profits pass through to the partners. |
| Limited liability partnership (LLP) | A partnership form commonly used by licensed professional firms. | Partners have limited liability for the partnership's contractual or tort obligations but remain responsible for their own malpractice. | It is treated as a pass-through partnership. |
| Limited liability company (LLC) | A hybrid of a corporation and partnership formed by filing with a state and adopting an operating agreement. Its owners are members; it may be member-managed or manager-managed. | Members generally receive limited-liability protection when the LLC is kept legally and financially separate from its owners. | A multi-member LLC defaults to partnership taxation and a single-member LLC to sole-proprietor treatment, but an LLC may elect corporate taxation. |
| C corporation | A corporation is created by filing formation documents with a state. Shareholders own stock, elect directors, and receive limited-liability protection when corporate formalities are maintained. | The corporation is a separate legal entity; shareholders ordinarily are not personally liable for corporate obligations. | The corporation pays tax on its profit, and shareholders may also pay individual tax when profit is distributed as dividends. |
| S corporation | A qualifying corporation elects federal subchapter S treatment. The source notes restrictions including no more than 100 shareholders, eligible individual owners, and one class of stock. | It retains the corporation's limited-liability structure. | Profit passes through to shareholders, who report it on their individual returns. |
| Not-for-profit corporation | A corporation organized to pursue a social cause or shared interest and to use surplus revenue for its mission rather than distribute it to owners. | It uses a corporate governance and liability structure. | Nonprofit status and federal tax-exempt status are distinct; an eligible organization must obtain the relevant tax treatment. |
| Joint venture | Two or more people or businesses cooperate on a specific for-profit objective, usually for a limited period, while keeping their other operations separate. | Liability depends on the agreement and on whether the parties form a separate corporation, LLC, or partnership for the venture. | A joint venture is not itself a single IRS entity classification; taxation follows the legal form used. |

# Corporation classifications

- **Privately held corporation:** Stock is owned by a founder's family or friends or by a private investor group rather than the investing public.
- **Publicly held corporation:** Members of the investing public can own its publicly traded stock.
- **Public corporation:** In the source, this means a government-owned or government-sponsored quasi-governmental entity, not merely a publicly traded company.
- **B corporation:** A business that meets high standards of social and environmental performance, public transparency, and accountability while balancing profit with social purpose.

# Selection factors

OpenStax identifies several questions that should inform entity selection:

- Who will own and manage the venture?
- How much personal liability can the owners accept?
- Will the venture need outside investors or publicly traded stock?
- Which formation filings, records, meetings, and ongoing formalities will apply?
- Will profit be taxed at the entity level, passed through to owners, or both?
- Where will the business operate, and what registrations will other states require?
- Does the structure support the venture's purpose, expected growth, and risk profile?
