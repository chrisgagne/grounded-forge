# Definition

Intellectual property is the legally protectable result of creative work used to turn a distinctive idea into a product or service. Different protection types cover different subject matter and require different approaches to disclosure and registration.

# Protection types

| Type | What it protects | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utility patent | A novel and useful machine, process, improvement, plant, or composition of matter. | Protects how an invention works or is used through a time-limited government grant. |
| Design patent | A new ornamental design for a manufactured product. | Protects the product's appearance rather than its functional idea. |
| Trademark | A source-identifying name, symbol, logo, jingle, character, or similar brand element used with a product or service. | Can remain protected while it stays in use and applicable renewals are maintained. |
| Copyright | Original artistic, dramatic, architectural, musical, literary, and software works. | Rights arise when an eligible original work is created; registration strengthens the owner's ability to enforce them. |
| Trade secret | Proprietary information, formulas, processes, methods, or internal knowledge that creates a competitive advantage. | Protection depends on the information remaining secret rather than on a public government grant. |

# Selection considerations

- A patent requires disclosure in exchange for a limited exclusive right; a trade secret depends on continued secrecy.
- Utility patents concern functional inventions, while design patents concern ornamental appearance.
- Trademark protection concerns the identity customers use to distinguish the source of goods or services.
- Copyright protects original expression, not a general business idea.
- Agreements such as nondisclosure and work-for-hire agreements may help a venture control confidential information and clarify ownership, but they are distinct from patents, trademarks, and copyrights.

The applicable law, eligibility rules, filing requirements, and protection periods can change. The source treats this reference as orientation rather than a substitute for current professional legal advice.
