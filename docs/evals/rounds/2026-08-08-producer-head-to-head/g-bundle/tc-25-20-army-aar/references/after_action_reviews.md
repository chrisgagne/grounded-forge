# Definition and Purpose

An after-action review (AAR) is a professional discussion of an event, focused on performance standards, that enables participants to discover what happened, why it happened, how to sustain strengths, and how to improve weaknesses. It is a feedback and learning method, not a critique or a grading exercise.

An AAR supports:

- candid examination of individual, leader, and unit performance from multiple perspectives;
- comparison of actual performance with the intended outcome and applicable standard;
- identification of strengths to sustain and deficiencies to correct;
- shared learning, task proficiency, unit cohesion, and future training decisions.

The discussion should be open, honest, specific, and focused on actions. Leaders remain responsible for immediate corrections, retraining, and unit performance.

# Types

All AARs use the same general format and exchange observations to improve proficiency. The difference is the level of planning and resources required.

## Formal AAR

A formal AAR is normally used at company level and above, though it can support smaller-unit events such as gunnery or a platoon situational training exercise. It is planned with the near-term training plan and may require:

- a reconnaissance and prepared AAR site;
- observers and controllers (OCs);
- terrain models, enlarged maps, operational graphics, photographs, recordings, or other training aids;
- coordination of support personnel and equipment;
- subordinate AARs before the higher-echelon review.

The AAR leader focuses discussion on the training objectives, reinforces the learning points at the end, and returns the discussion to those objectives.

## Informal AAR

An informal AAR is commonly used for soldiers and small units at platoon level and below, but it can also be used at higher levels when time or resources are limited. It requires less planning and few, if any, training aids. Leaders may conduct it as immediate, on-the-spot coaching while the event is still fresh, using the standard AAR format to evaluate performance against the standard, identify strengths and weaknesses, and decide how to improve.

# Four-Step Process

1. **Planning:** Integrate the AAR into training plans, select and train OCs, determine the evaluation focus and attendees, schedule stopping points, identify sites, and arrange useful training aids.
2. **Preparing:** Review doctrine, objectives, orders, and the mission-essential task list (METL); identify critical events; observe training and record detailed, time-sequenced notes; consolidate and organize observations; prepare the site; and rehearse.
3. **Conducting:** Establish the rules and a candid learning climate; review objectives and intent; establish what happened; discuss key issues, causes, strengths, weaknesses, and corrective actions; address safety; and summarize.
4. **Following up:** Apply the findings through immediate or scheduled retraining, update standing operating procedures when needed, and use results to assess proficiency and plan future training.

# Planning the AAR

The AAR plan should identify:

- who will observe the training and who will lead the AAR;
- the tasks, conditions, standards, and training objectives to evaluate;
- who will attend;
- when and where the AAR will occur;
- which training aids will directly support learning.

Observers should be able to perform the evaluated tasks to Army standards, understand the duties observed, and know current tactics, techniques, and procedures. Experience and current technical understanding may be more important than rank. OCs should have no competing duties that prevent effective observation.

Leaders should plan an AAR after each critical phase or major event when possible. Planning allowances are approximately 30–45 minutes for platoon-level AARs, one hour for company-level AARs, and two hours for battalion-level and higher AARs. The review must be long enough to address the key learning points without dwelling on dead-end issues.

The preferred site is at or near the training area, ideally overlooking the terrain where critical actions occurred. If that is not possible, use a terrain model, enlarged map, sketch, and unit graphics. Arrange participants comfortably, minimize distractions, and place seating in a horseshoe to encourage discussion.

Use a training aid only when it improves the AAR. Useful aids can include terrain models, enlarged maps, dry-erase boards, photographs, video or communications recordings, operational graphics, models, chalk, or improvised objects.

# Preparing Observations

OCs focus on events critical to accomplishing the tasks and objectives, such as:

- operation orders (OPORDs) and fragmentary orders (FRAGOs);
- troop-leading procedures;
- contact with opposing forces;
- resupply and reconstitution;
- intelligence preparation of the battlefield;
- passage of lines.

Record events, actions, and observations in time sequence. Notes should be reliable, detailed, and consistent, with times, places, names, and a date-time group when possible. A useful observation record includes:

- training or exercise title;
- event;
- date and time;
- observation location;
- player or trainer action observed;
- discussion tied to the task and standard;
- conclusions;
- recommendations for better execution or needed training.

The lead OC should collect input from subordinate, adjacent, combat support, combat service support, and opposing-force participants as applicable. Organize the combined observations chronologically, then select the events and teaching points most relevant to the training objectives.

# Conducting the Discussion

The recommended sequence is:

1. Introduction and rules.
2. Review of training objectives, the commander's mission and intent, the opposing-force commander's mission and intent when applicable, and relevant doctrine and tactics, techniques, and procedures.
3. Summary of recent events: what happened.
4. Discussion of key issues.
5. Discussion of optional issues such as soldier and leader skills, tasks to sustain or improve, and useful statistics.
6. Discussion of force protection and safety.
7. Closing summary linked to future training.

The leader should encourage participation regardless of rank, make disagreement permissible, focus on learning, and enter the discussion only when necessary. Use open-ended and leading questions to elicit participant perspectives without putting people on the defensive. For example, ask what happened at a specific moment instead of accusing someone of failing to act.

## Discussion Structures

The leader can structure the discussion in one of three ways:

- **Chronological order:** Follow the event from start to finish so participants can connect actions with later effects.
- **Battlefield operating systems:** Examine intelligence; maneuver; fire support; mobility, countermobility, and survivability; air defense; combat service support; and command and control across the exercise.
- **Key events, themes, or issues:** Focus on critical events that directly supported the training objectives, especially when time is limited.

These methods can be combined. The leader should be specific and thorough, avoid unrelated issues, focus on actions and objectives, identify corrective action, and summarize throughout.

All incidents or near incidents of fratricide should be examined in detail, including the cause, friendly-force identification, fire-control measures, and how the commander's risk assessment and intent addressed the risk. Safety should be addressed in every AAR and treated as integral to operations.

Statistics should be used only to identify critical trends or reinforce teaching points. Excessive charts or scoring can suppress candid discussion and turn the AAR into a grading exercise.

# Follow-Up

The value of an AAR comes from applying its results. Leaders should conduct a trained-practiced-untrained assessment, develop a future-training concept, and retrain deficient tasks as soon as practical. Supporting soldier and leader tasks should be corrected before the deficient collective task is retrained.

When time or mission complexity prevents immediate retraining, leaders should place the task and its deficient supporting tasks back into the training plan. Critical gate tasks must be performed to standard before the unit progresses to more complex tasks. If an AAR reveals a problem in a standing operating procedure, leaders should revise the procedure and implement the change in future training.

AARs also apply in combat when security permits. Short, focused reviews can help a unit avoid repeated mistakes, sustain strengths, and reduce the learning curve under operational conditions.
