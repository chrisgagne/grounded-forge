1.3 The Feature-Versus-Quality Conflict Between Marketing and Engineering

Project management is the process of planning and executing a project while balancing the time, cost, and scope constraints, which are known as the triple constraint. Authors in other fields sometimes consider quality separate from scope, whereas in software engineering, requirements include quality. The entire Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint, and each Increment is additive to all prior Increments and thoroughly verified, ensuring that all Increments work together and that the Increment is usable in order to provide value. Software systems have a minimum viable reliability, which is what the market will tolerate. Software systems containing likely fault experiences are shipped because it is not economically worthwhile fixing all the mistakes made during their implementation, and finding and fixing mistakes prior to release is often constrained by available resources and marketing deadlines. Software release decisions involve weighing whether the supported functionality provides enough benefit to be attractive to customers after factoring in likely costs arising from faults experienced by customers, such as from lost sales, dealing with customer complaints, possibly fixing reported problems, and making available an updated version. In an output-focused topology, there is no guarantee that the output effectively solves the real problem or creates outcomes people care about, and products and services with "feature bloat" are a common consequence. My inference: Marketing is treating quality as separate from scope while pushing marketing deadlines for a feature a week, whereas in software engineering quality is an inseparable part of scope and verified increments, and allowing marketing deadlines to constrain fault-fixing below what the market tolerates produces the customer-experienced faults driving our churn numbers. 1.5 The Customer Portal Outage, Individual Blame, and Latent System Failures

Many accident investigations do not go far enough because they identify the technical cause of the accident and then connect it to a variant of "operator error" — the line worker who forgot to insert the bolt, the engineer who miscalculated the stress, or the manager who made the wrong decision. When the determinations of the causal chain are limited to the technical flaw and individual failure, typically the actions taken to prevent a similar event in the future are also limited: fix the technical problem and replace or retrain the individual responsible, and putting these corrections in place leads to another mistake — the belief that the problem is solved. There is an overwhelming tendency to point fingers at people based on the consequence of their action and not on the intent of the action, so that if either the violation or error they committed causes an accident or an event of some kind, they are disciplined, but the very same actions that have no consequence are ignored or allowed to slide. Punishing or blaming employees involved in an accident is the surest way to kill a learning culture. "Holding people accountable and blaming people are two quite different things. Blaming people may in fact make them less accountable: they will tell fewer accounts, they may feel less compelled to have their voice heard, to participate in improvement efforts." Attempting to hold individuals "accountable" in the traditional sense for systems-level weaknesses or failures obliterates learning opportunities, and when employees are exposed to unfair treatment, the inevitable reaction is a breakdown in trust. Events triggered by human error are often symptomatic of a system failure, and instead of asking how the individual failed the organization, the question "how did the organization fail the individual?" would be more appropriate. The major difference between an RCA and a Blameless Postmortem is the way both of them are run, with the latter putting emphasis not only on establishing the root cause, but also on ensuring that there is no form of possibly harmful judgement being made along the way. A Blameless Postmortem states that "Our job is not to point fingers at an unlucky engineer that applied a wrong configuration file, our job is to figure out why he picked the wrong one and what we personally and as an organization can do to prevent it in the future." My inference: Naming the developer who merged the bad release in the postmortem and putting him on a performance plan after the four-hour customer portal outage disciplined an individual based on the consequence of his action, created the false belief that our outage risk is solved, damaged engineering trust, and left the system-level flaws in our release and verification pipeline unaddressed. 1.6 Why Buying AI Coding Tools Will Not Let Us Do Both    [CHIP: ] 

No source in this notebook covers AI coding tools. The Theory of Constraints shows us that in any process, there must be a single bottleneck that limits the total throughput of the process: in the same way that a chain has a weakest link, a process has a single slowest component. Whilst it may be easy to improve some part of the process, if that component is not the bottleneck, it will either have zero effect on the throughput because the bottleneck is upstream, or it will actually cause more pain in the system by adding pressure to the bottleneck, if the bottleneck is downstream. This is why "Any improvements made anywhere besides the bottleneck are an illusion." Factors influencing the rate of evolution of source code characteristics include being "developer limited," where bottlenecks in the development process restrict the quantity of change per unit time, such as a limited number of people with the necessary skills, change requests requiring sign-off by a handful of senior managers, or increasing developer resources required to support a growing system leading to diminishing returns from adding more developers. When existing code is changed, there is a non-zero probability of a mistake being made. My inference: Buying AI coding tools will not enable us to achieve both weekly feature velocity and high stability because writing lines of code is not our bottleneck; accelerating code generation upstream of our overloaded, multi-squad review, testing, and deployment stages will only pile up more work in progress at downstream bottlenecks while introducing more code changes that each carry a non-zero probability of mistakes. 2. What to Change First and Why That First
2.1 First Change: Executive Reset of Management Policies, Incentives, and Incident Accountability

Because the majority of the causes of events originate in the system of controls, processes, and values established by the management team, management's first reaction to events should be to look within the organization. Those who manage organizations, who make the decisions and set the standards for safety, are often removed in time and distance from the consequences of their actions, while the workers within the organization suffer the consequences of decisions made and may be blamed for causing events. As one of the Morton Thiokol engineers explained in an article after the 1986 Challenger accident: "It is no longer the individual that is the locus of power and responsibility, but public and private institution. Thus, it would seem, it is no longer the character and virtues of individuals that determine the standards of moral conduct, it is the policies and structures of the institutional settings within which they live and work". In NASA's history, upper-level managers and engineering teams held opposing definitions of risk because key managers were responding to additional demands of cost and schedule, which competed with their safety concerns, and NASA's conflicting goals put engineers at a disadvantage before new situations even arose. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board captured its views of the need to adjust management to enhance safety margins in Shuttle operations, and reaffirmed its position that without these changes, it had no confidence that other "corrective actions" would improve the safety of Shuttle operations. In 2015, Jez Humble, Joanne Molesky, and Barry O'Reilly wrote in the book "Lean Enterprise: How High Performance Organizations Innovate at Scale" that "Instead of creating controls to compensate for pathological cultures, the solution is to create a culture in which people take responsibility for the consequences of their actions". Psychological safety is the most important factor in high performing teams. When team members feel safe to focus on what matters to the business, they will make decisions that improve outcomes, even if those decisions reduce output and thus make them appear less busy. 2.2 Sequenced Operational Reforms Following the Executive Reset    [CHIP: ]

Using the Theory of Constraints to identify bottlenecks in a process follows three stages: Stage 1 is to identify the constraint by looking at where "Work In Progress" (WIP) is piling up; Stage 2 is to exploit the constraint by making quick improvements to the throughput of this stage; and Stage 3 is to subordinate the constraint by aligning the rest of the process with this stage. 3.1 Multi-Team Membership and Full Utilization vs. Single-Team Focus and Refusing to Look Busy    [CHIP: ] 

A software development organization, whether it employs one person or thousands, needs to schedule its activities to keep everyone busy, and the money flowing in. A company employing a relatively fixed number of developers may seek to schedule work so that employees always have something productive to do, and companies may assign developers to more than one project at the same time to maintain staff workload by having something else for them to do if they are blocked from working on their primary project. In the modern workplace, individuals are being tasked with multiple roles that allow them to work within many teams on many projects, and while juggling six different projects at one given time can be stressful to any employee, structuring the work within different teams gives employees a sense of autonomy and allows companies to balance stakeholder management and profits, with leadership playing a big role in combating the negative effects of multiple team memberships. Conversely, Lean and Agile sources state that "Multitasking does not work." These sources state that nobody needs to "look busy," list "Be more productive by juggling several projects at once" as part of a bad social contract, and explain that taking on new tasks while a task is waiting on external input causes people to become overloaded when blocked tasks resume, whereas working on one task at a time lets a blocked person help reduce the external dependency. My inference: The sources disagree on whether multi-squad staffing and 100% utilization are sound management practices or destructive anti-patterns—software economics and organizational behavior texts treat multi-project allocation as a practical way to keep a fixed workforce busy when primary tasks are blocked and to provide autonomy across projects, whereas Lean and Agile sources reject multitasking and busyness altogether and insist that blocked engineers help clear bottlenecks rather than start parallel work. 3.2 Demanding Performance Standards and Agreed Sprint Delivery vs. Refusing Firm Sprint Commitments

According to Katzenbach and Smith's "Discipline of Teams," practices observed in successful teams include establishing urgency, demanding performance standards, and direction so that teams live up to performance expectations, as well as setting and seizing upon immediate performance-oriented tasks and goals. In descriptions of the Scrum Agile process, during a sprint, which has a fixed elapsed time where two weeks is a common choice, the functionality agreed at the start is created. Conversely, Scrum practitioner guidance states that "The Development Team cannot and must not make a firm commitment to delivering the entire Sprint Backlog or even the Sprint Goal," warning that attempting to do so will only lead to burnout, poor quality, loss of trust, and/or attempts at "gaming the system." Furthermore, Scrum specifies that if work turns out to be different than expected during the Sprint, Developers collaborate with the Product Owner to negotiate the scope of the Sprint Backlog within the Sprint without affecting the Sprint Goal. My inference: The literature is divided over whether teams should be held to fixed short-term delivery targets—team-effectiveness and general software-engineering texts expect teams to meet demanding performance standards and deliver the functionality agreed at the start of a sprint, whereas Scrum practitioner rules forbid firm commitments to the Sprint Backlog or Sprint Goal so teams can renegotiate scope without sacrificing quality or gaming estimates. 3.3 Explicit Work-in-Progress (WIP) Caps vs. Batch-Size Reduction Without WIP Limits

In Kanban practice, a team determines the amount of in-progress work it wants to be its maximum at any given time, sets that limit as its WIP limit, and ensures throughout the sprint that it is not exceeded. Conversely, Open Kanban states that limiting WIP is a consequence of reducing the batch size of your efforts, and not the other way around, and explicitly states that Open Kanban does not ask you to limit WIP, but requests that you "Reduce the Batch Size of your Efforts". My inference: Flow-management sources disagree on the direct mechanism for stopping sprint overload—standard Kanban imposes an explicit numerical cap on work items in progress at each stage, whereas Open Kanban rejects explicit WIP limits and argues that shrinking story size and complexity is what causes WIP to drop as a consequence. 3.4 Unconditional Blameless Postmortems vs. Individual Culpability Screening

A Blameless Postmortem puts emphasis not only on establishing the root cause, but also on ensuring that there is no form of possibly harmful judgement being made along the way. Facilitated Learning Analysis (FLA) states that its key commitment is that accidents are not caused by anomalous, blundering, or deviant behavior, but are more accurately understood as unexpected combinations of normal performance variability, including both human and system performance variability. Conversely, the Department of Energy Human Performance Handbook uses a Culpability Decision Tree (CDT) in conjunction with an organization's accountability policy so a manager can ask questions to determine an individual's intent, expectations, prior knowledge, and prior performance problems associated with their recent unsafe act, thereby gauging both individual culpability (for an intentional act to cause harm, reckless violation, and negligent error) and organizational culpability (for induced violation and induced error). The FLA guide also states that if an employee harms another employee intentionally, that employee is responsible and an FLA is likely the wrong response. My inference: Incident-analysis frameworks are split between unconditional blamelessness and structured culpability testing—Blameless Postmortem and normal-variability models forbid any potentially harmful judgment of the individual during review, whereas human-performance accountability models require managers to investigate the individual's prior performance problems, intent, and potential negligence or recklessness alongside organizational factors. 3.5 High-Reliability Theory and Thorough Verification vs. Normal Accident Theory and Economic Fault Tolerance

The Columbia Accident Investigation Board studied the two dominant theories on complex organizations and accidents involving high-risk technologies, noting that each takes a different approach to understanding accidents and risk. High-reliability theory holds a view that serious accidents can be prevented through a strong safety culture, a willingness to learn from mistakes, and realistic training, and that the organization cannot afford the mindset that accidents are inevitable. Similarly, software process guides call for "quality gates" to determine whether product quality at one stage is sufficient to support moving on to the next, and Scrum requires that every Increment be thoroughly verified so that all Increments work together. Conversely, normal accident theory states that complex interactions can make organizations accident-prone, that organizations cannot put blind faith into redundant warning systems because they inherently create more complexity which in turn often produces unintended system interactions that can lead to failure, and that additional protective layers are not always the best choice. Furthermore, empirical software engineering states that software systems containing likely fault experiences are shipped because it is not economically worthwhile fixing all the mistakes made during their implementation, and that release decisions weigh customer benefit against likely fault costs relative to minimum viable reliability, which is what the market will tolerate. My inference: Our sources disagree on how far verification and protective controls should go—high-reliability theory and Scrum demand thorough verification and quality gates to prevent failures, whereas normal accident theory warns that adding protective layers and redundant checks increases system complexity that can trigger new failures, and software economics argues that shipping software with unfixed faults is rational up to the limit of what the market will tolerate.
