# Answer C

Question: I'm CTO at a 140-person logistics SaaS. Three product lines, one engineering group of about 60 in nine squads. Every sprint overflows: we plan around 40 points, ship 25, and carry the rest into the next one. Marketing wants a feature a week for the growth push and says quality is engineering's problem; engineering says the churn numbers come from bugs. My head of engineering has set a "stretch" target of 40% more throughput by Q1 and tied the squad leads' bonus to it. Most developers sit on two or three squads at once, because the CEO's rule is that nobody is idle. Last month a bad release took the customer portal down for four hours; the postmortem named the developer who merged it, and he's now on a performance plan. The CEO's answer to all of this is to buy AI coding tools so we can "do both".

I need a two-page memo for the CEO that says what's actually going on here, what to change first and why that first, and where the thinking you're drawing on disagrees with itself.

The demo corpus has no software-delivery research linking quality practice, throughput and customer churn, no evidence at all on AI coding assistants, and nothing direct on the cost of people working across several teams at once. It holds no primary text by Goldratt, Deming or Reinertsen on constraints and batch size, and no blame or postmortem guidance written for software teams. Ingestion candidates that would let the corpus answer this directly: Forsgren, Humble and Kim's Accelerate (or the DORA State of DevOps reports); Reinertsen's The Principles of Product Development Flow; METR's 2025 randomised trial of AI tools on experienced developers (held in the agent-built-software corpus, not in demo).

## What is actually going on: a system built to keep everyone busy

Your teams are not failing to reach 40 points. They reliably deliver about 25, and the plan ignores it. The design produces that gap. The rule that nobody is idle is the founding rule of a particular kind of organisation: “Its purpose is to ensure that each unit is 100% utilized (i.e. resource utilization).” Putting developers on two or three squads is how that rule gets met, and focus is what it costs. “Research in the way the mind works, and countless experiences from Lean, the Theory of Constraints and Kanban confirm that to deliver value faster, with better flow and good team morale we need to focus and limit the number of things we do. Multitasking does not work.” The 15 points carried each sprint do not vanish. They pile up, and the pile starts to drive behaviour. “It matters a lot more to know that those three students joined a group of 20 students that have accumulated on a waiting list for therapy. The system will react to the accumulation, not only the input.” Each local choice, staff every squad and keep every person loaded, can be right for one squad and wrong for the whole: “If each element (e.g. policies, structures) is optimized in isolation, the performance of the whole system may actually decline for the desired goal (flow can get worse!).” A pace that overcommits people every sprint is a reliability risk on its own terms. Hurtado ties respect for people to sustainable pace and calls an exhausted developer, manager or team a recipe for disaster.


## Marketing and engineering are both right, which is the problem

A feature a week and fewer bugs are two goals drawn on one capacity, and nobody has said which one wins. The Columbia board found the same shape at NASA: “NASA had conflicting goals of cost, schedule, and safety. Safety lost out as the mandates of an "operational system" increased the schedule pressure.” No single decision looks reckless, which is why the drift goes unnoticed: “When a program agrees to spend less money or accelerate a schedule beyond what the engineers and program managers think is reasonable, a small amount of overall risk is added. These little pieces of risk add up until managers are no longer aware of the total program risk, and-are, in fact, gambling. Little by little, NASA was accepting more and more risk in order to stay on schedule.” The same contest runs inside each developer every day: “Production and prevention (error and event) practices always compete in the minds of workers.” Marketing counts features shipped, which is output. Churn is an outcome, and the two can move apart. “However, more output does not mean more outcomes. In fact, more output usually means more cost or investment, which can lower profit (an outcome). Investing to increase outcomes (healthy people, profit) is justified; ‘investing’ to increase output is suspect.” The primer adds that an organisation tuned for output has no guarantee its output solves the real problem, and names feature bloat as a common result. None of this makes the growth ambition wrong. The Columbia board itself calls aggressive schedules a sign of a healthy institution, provided the pressure they put on safety is carefully monitored. The fault is that the trade-off is being settled by default, inside the squads, instead of by you and the CEO.


## The postmortem made the next outage more likely

Naming the developer who merged the release, and putting him on a plan, was the most expensive thing that happened last month. The Columbia board describes the move exactly: “They identify the widget that broke or malfunctioned, then locate the person most closely connected with the technical failure: the engineer who miscalculated an analysis, the operator who missed signals or pulled the wrong switches, the supervisor who failed to listen, or the manager who made bad decisions. When causal chains are limited to technical flaws and individual failures, the ensuing responses aimed at preventing a similar event in the future are equally limited: they aim to fix the technical problem and replace or retrain the individual responsible. Such corrections lead to a misguided and potentially disastrous belief that the underlying problem has been solved.” One merge taking the portal down for four hours says more about the controls around the merge than about the person who made it: “Whereas human error typically triggers an event, it is the number of controls and the weaknesses of those controls that dictate the severity of the event.” What follows a named culprit has a name: “Events will continue as long as event investigations stop prematurely at the active human error. The true causes (typically organizational weaknesses) will not be discovered—will remain latent or hidden—and errors and events will persist.” Why the cost compounds: “If an individual believes his or her errors will be punished, then information related to those errors will likely remain obscure. In a just environment, the likelihood that a problem will be reported increases.” Every engineer watched what happened to a colleague, and the cheapest lesson on offer was to report less. “Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate - most patient safety issues have deeper causes and require wider action.” The DOE handbook asks leaders to turn the question round, from how the individual failed the organisation to how the organisation failed the individual. The Forest Service guide is blunter: punishing or blaming the people involved in an accident is the surest way to kill a learning culture.


## The stretch target and the bonus will move the number, not the system

A 40% throughput target tied to the leads' pay, in a system that already plans 60% above what it delivers, invites the result it is meant to prevent. An example from schools maps closely: “But there is an unintended consequence: with the added pressure on testing, teachers become burnt out and students become less engaged in learning. Ultimately, the test scores drop further.” Points are especially easy to inflate without delivering more. OpenStax's organisational behaviour text lists the side effects of goal setting: “Second, goal setting sometimes has unintended consequences. For example, employees set easy goals so that they look good when they achieve them. Or it causes unhealthy competition between employees. Or an employee sabotages the work of others so that only she has goal achievement.” “Perhaps the key caution about goal setting is that it often results in too much focus on quantified measures of performance. Qualitative aspects of a job or task may be neglected because they aren’t easily measured.” Here the qualitative part being neglected is quality, the thing engineering says drives churn. OpenStax also warns that individual goals in a teamwork setting can backfire, and prefers group goals where people depend on one another. With developers spread across squads, every lead's number depends on people that lead does not control. A target 40% above a plan the teams already miss reads like this: “An ideal standard level is set assuming that everything is perfect, machines do not break down, employees show up on time, there are no defects, there is no scrap, and materials are perfect. This level of standard is not the best motivator, because employees may see this level as unattainable.” The DOE handbook lists among flawed cultural controls a gap between what managers say they want and what they reward, alongside disciplining honest mistakes and bonuses based solely on productivity. All three are in play in your organisation now. “The organization is perfectly tuned to get the performance it receives from the workforce.”


## Why AI tools will not let you do both

Buying coding assistants is the easiest move on the table, and that is the warning sign. “However, the quick-fix to the symptoms may actually impede our ability to invest time and energy to the fundamental solution. This makes it even harder to apply a fundamental solution and even more tempting to rely on quick fixes. Thus, the problem cannot be completely solved.” If the constraint is overloaded people and unowned priorities, faster code generation adds more work in flight and more merges through the same thin review and release controls that failed last month. Even authors who are bullish on AI tell leaders to aim it: “On investigation, it’s a lack of front-end design and coding skilled people. Focus time, money, and management attention on AIs, especially on that big bottleneck, over a generic “adopt AI.”” The same authors expect AI to create new human work, predicting that monitoring it will become an important responsibility. “Changes in organizational structure should be made only with careful consideration of their effect on the system and their possible unintended consequences. Changes that make the organization more complex may create new ways that it can fail.48 When changes are put in place, the risk of error initially increases, as old ways of doing things compete with new.” Dropped into the current system, the tools are likely to become the next fix that fails: The SSDL briefs describe a fix that solves the problem in the short run while its unintended consequences make the original problem worse. Bought after the system is fixed and aimed at the constraint, they could help. Bought instead of fixing it, they will hide the problem for a quarter.


## What to change first, and why that first

Change the staffing rule first: one squad per developer, and plan each sprint to the 25 points the teams actually deliver. It comes before quality gates, the target or the tools because it is the constraint the others run through. OpenStax's accounting text summarises Goldratt's cycle as identifying the system constraint, exploiting it with quick changes using existing resources, subordinating everything else to it, then elevating it. Multi-squad assignment is a policy constraint, not a physical one, so it can be lifted this sprint at no cost. Cutting what is in flight is the mechanism: “Limiting WIP is a consequence of reducing the batch size of your efforts, and not the other way around. However doing either will result in improvements in efficiency and productivity.” Hurtado, drawing on Deming and Goldratt, holds that no single part of a system can bring overall improvement. It is also first because it is yours and the CEO's to change, unlike churn or the market: “By contrast, org design is internal, under our control, and closer to being deterministic or predictable (for instance, “design for fast flow”). This allows us to influence business success by at least gaining influence over the internal factors of the design.” And while every person is fully loaded, quality work will lose every time: “Production behaviors naturally take precedence over prevention behaviors unless there is a strong safety culture—nurtured by strong leadership.” Two moves cost nothing and should go with it: suspend the link between the stretch target and the leads' bonuses, and reopen last month's postmortem without a name, asking what let one merge take the portal down for four hours. Responsibility for the conditions sits at the top: “Leaders create culture. It is their responsibility to change it. Top administrators must take responsibility for risk, failure, and safety by remaining alert to the effects their decisions have on the system. Leaders are responsible for establishing the conditions that lead to their subordinates' successes or failures. The past decisions of national leaders — the White House, Congress, and NASA Headquarters — set the Columbia accident in motion by creating resource and schedule strains that compromised the principles of a high-risk technology organization.”


## Where the thinking disagrees with itself

Not everything points one way, and the CEO should hear where it splits. First, on stretch goals: “It remains true that difficult, specific goals result in better performance than easy and vague goals, assuming they are accepted.” OpenStax adds that money raises motivation when tied to goal achievement, by strengthening goal commitment. Yet the same textbook reports a finding from another school that runs the other way: “researchers have consistently found that as the level of extrinsic rewards increases, the amount of intrinsic motivation decreases. That is, SDT posits that extrinsic rewards not only do not provide intrinsic motivation,” OpenStax holds that goals draw full effort only when they are difficult, specific, accepted and committed to, and that full performance also needs enough ability and no blocking constraints. A 40% stretch above a missed plan meets none of those conditions. Second, on blame. Even the source most opposed to stopping at the person keeps individual responsibility: “Third, the Board's focus on the context in which decision making occurred does not mean that individuals are not responsible and accountable. To the contrary, individuals always must assume responsibility for their actions. What it does mean is that NASA's problems cannot be solved simply by retirements, resignations, or transferring personnel.'” The board kept personal accountability, but in a process separate from the safety investigation. The Forest Service guide redefines the word instead: “In our Just Culture, accountability means creating the space for each person involved to provide an honest account of what happened from his or her perspective. The agency is then held accountable to learn about the conditions that influenced the outcome and make improvements to the overall system.” Third, on finding the cause, the DOE and Forest Service guides part ways: The DOE handbook rejects inattention and procedure-breaking as root causes, but still defines a root cause as the one whose correction prevents recurrence. The Forest Service guide sets root cause analysis aside and relies on networked causality, the conditions and context surrounding the event. For the portal review, that choice decides whether it ends at one fix or maps the conditions. Fourth, on AI, one source is openly optimistic: “A key insight when designing with OT: Specialization and expertise are vanishing as limited resources, due to intelligence as a service. That makes it much easier to elevate an organization! So when you are deciding on new teams requiring new expert knowledge, shed the old assumptions, “We don’t have enough experts, therefore…”. Spin up another 10,000 agents.” Set that against the quick-fix risk above; nothing in this library measures AI coding tools directly. Fifth, on whether structure must change: “Although Kanban starts where you are, and does not need to modify any titles or roles in an organization, Kanban cannot work without a team to deliver value.” The primer takes the other side: The Org Topologies primer warns that a single high-performing team cannot reach its potential while the structures and processes around it stay unchanged. Finally, a caution on this memo's own diagnosis: The SSDL briefs warn that system archetypes are not to be applied as labels, since jumping to a pattern can erase what is unique about a situation. Test it with the squads before acting on it.
