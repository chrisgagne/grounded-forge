# Answer F

Question: I'm CTO at a 140-person logistics SaaS. Three product lines, one engineering group of about 60 in nine squads. Every sprint overflows: we plan around 40 points, ship 25, and carry the rest into the next one. Marketing wants a feature a week for the growth push and says quality is engineering's problem; engineering says the churn numbers come from bugs. My head of engineering has set a "stretch" target of 40% more throughput by Q1 and tied the squad leads' bonus to it. Most developers sit on two or three squads at once, because the CEO's rule is that nobody is idle. Last month a bad release took the customer portal down for four hours; the postmortem named the developer who merged it, and he's now on a performance plan. The CEO's answer to all of this is to buy AI coding tools so we can "do both".

I need a two-page memo for the CEO that says what's actually going on here, what to change first and why that first, and where the thinking you're drawing on disagrees with itself.

The demo corpus carries no research on software-delivery performance (lead time, change failure rate, deployment frequency and how they relate to quality and growth), no primary source on flow economics or the cost of multitasking in product work, no evidence on what AI coding tools do to a team's throughput and defect rate, and nothing on how SaaS companies split decision rights between marketing and engineering. The memo's mechanism is assembled from organisation-design, Kanban, human-performance and accident-investigation sources, applied by analogy. Three ingestion candidates would let the corpus answer this directly: Forsgren, Humble and Kim's Accelerate (or the DORA State of DevOps reports); Reinertsen's The Principles of Product Development Flow; and METR's 2025 randomised trial of AI tools with experienced open-source developers.

## What is going on: one loop, not three problems

The overflowing sprints, the bugs and the fight with marketing are one loop, and it starts with the rule that nobody is idle. With most developers on two or three squads, engineering is organised to keep people busy rather than to finish work. “Its purpose is to ensure that each unit is 100% utilized (i.e. resource utilization). It is seen in “resource management” approaches where “resource managers” (or project managers) are responsible for planning and monitoring resource allocation and utilization.” Spreading each person across squads works against finishing anything. Hurtado argues that research and experience from Lean, the Theory of Constraints and Kanban show that faster delivery, better flow and good morale come from limiting how many things a team does at once, and that multitasking does not work. The same overload is where the bugs come from. The DOE handbook's list of the most common error precursors, compiled from INPO's event database, is led by time pressure, high workload and simultaneous multiple actions. “If you respect your team you will not work them to death, or subject any worker to intellectual or physical demands that make it nearly impossible to succeed. An exhausted developer, manager or team are the perfect recipe for disaster.” Each squad can look fully loaded while the whole group gets slower. “If each element (e.g. policies, structures) is optimized in isolation, the performance of the whole system may actually decline for the desired goal (flow can get worse!).” A feature a week is a strategy of speed, and the current design is built for something else. “In the mapping on the right, there’s a mismatch: the strategic ambition requires an org design with the goal of speed rather than predictability and resource utilization.” Planning 40 points and shipping 25, sprint after sprint, is what that mismatch looks like from the inside.


## The stretch target will make the numbers lie

A 40% throughput target tied to squad leads' bonuses lands on the one number the squads control: points. “If the measurements collected were actively used to control or evaluate the development team (for instance), then developers have a motivation to cause the measure- ments to move in a direction favorable to themselves.” The DOE handbook lists bonuses based solely on productivity measures, and disciplinary action for honest mistakes, among flawed cultural controls. Points are output, and more of them is not what the growth push needs. “However, more output does not mean more outcomes. In fact, more output usually means more cost or investment, which can lower profit (an outcome). Investing to increase outcomes (healthy people, profit) is justified; ‘investing’ to increase output is suspect.” Pay works as a motivator only under conditions this target does not meet. OpenStax's organisational behaviour text, after Lawler, says pay motivates strongly only when trust is high, individual performance can be measured accurately, rewards differ substantially and good performance carries few negative consequences. With points visible and quality unmeasured, the predictable trade is quantity for quality, which is the churn problem in another form. “If goals conflict, employees may sacrifice performance on important job duties. For example, if both quantitative and qualitative goals are set for performance, employees may emphasize quantity because this goal achievement is more visible.”


## The postmortem fixed the wrong thing

Naming the developer who merged the release and putting him on a performance plan treats a system failure as a personal one. “When causal chains are limited to technical flaws and individual failures, the ensuing responses aimed at preventing a similar event in the future are equally limited: they aim to fix the technical problem and replace or retrain the individual responsible. Such corrections lead to a misguided and potentially disastrous belief that the underlying problem has been solved.” “Events will continue as long as event investigations stop prematurely at the active human error. The true causes (typically organizational weaknesses) will not be discovered—will remain latent or hidden—and errors and events will persist.” One question the postmortem skipped: “Are there indications that other individuals from the same peer group, with comparable experience and qualifications, would behave in the same way in similar circumstances? 4b. Was the individual missed out when relevant training was provided to their peer group?” A developer split across three squads, in a sprint that always overflows, was in the position any peer would have been in. “Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate - most patient safety issues have deeper causes and require wider action.” The cost of the plan falls on everyone else, who now know what reporting a mistake earns. “Punishing or blaming employees involved in an accident is the surest way to kill a learning culture.” The DOE handbook calls a just culture a prerequisite for a reporting culture. A four-hour outage is also a question about what stood between one merge and every customer. The DOE handbook says reducing error lowers the frequency of events but only controls reduce their severity. The better question for the review is this one: “Instead of asking how the individual failed the organization, the question “how did the organization fail the individual?””


## Marketing versus engineering is a missing decision

When the growth push and quality pull against each other, nobody owns the choice between them, so the calendar decides by default. “Production behaviors naturally take precedence over prevention behaviors unless there is a strong safety culture—nurtured by strong leadership.” The DOE handbook argues that because people follow the production focus of their supervision and work group, leadership is itself a defence. The Columbia board saw the same drift: “accident, institutional practices that were in effect at the time of the Challenger accident — such as inadequate concern over deviations from expected performance, a silent safety program, and schedule pressure — had returned to NASA.” Part of the Columbia board's remedy was structural: it urged NASA's Human Space Flight Program to adopt the characteristics of high-reliability organisations. The Columbia board urged separating technical authority from the management of schedule and cost. Here that means the authority to hold a release sits apart from the growth calendar. For the order of work, the answer is one owner per product line. “The Product Owner is one person, not a committee. The Product Owner may represent the needs of many stakeholders in the Product Backlog. Those wanting to change the Product Backlog can do so by trying to convince the Product Owner.” The Scrum Guide says Product Owners succeed only if the whole organisation respects their decisions. Marketing then argues for its feature a week with that owner, in the open, against the defect cost, and the CEO backs the owner's call.


## AI coding tools: where they help and where they hide the problem

Buying tools so the group can do both leaves the loop above untouched. “When new ways are layered onto existing elements without addressing deeper organizational patterns, the result is often increased complexity rather than improved performance.” The tools are a quick fix that competes for the attention the real fix needs. “However, the quick-fix to the symptoms may actually impede our ability to invest time and energy to the fundamental solution. This makes it even harder to apply a fundamental solution and even more tempting to rely on quick fixes.” If tools are bought, they belong at a named bottleneck. Krivitsky, Larman and Flemm advise focusing AI money and attention on the specific bottleneck that blocks the target design, not on a generic adoption of AI. In this company the likely bottlenecks are review, testing and release, not writing code: more code poured into the same overloaded squads adds work in progress and more to review before each release.


## What to change first, and why that first

Change the rule the CEO owns: one developer, one squad, and each squad plans only what it can finish. It comes first because every other problem runs through it: the overflow, the overload behind the bugs, the gaming the bonus invites, the conditions the postmortem missed. It is also the one change nobody below the CEO can make. “Leaders create culture. It is their responsibility to change it. Top administrators must take responsibility for risk, failure, and safety by remaining alert to the effects their decisions have on the system. Leaders are responsible for establishing the conditions that lead to their subordinates' successes or failures.” The planning half of the change is small batches. “Reduce the complexity and the quantity of things you do at any stage of the value chain. In software development this would mean: reduce the number of large stories (epics) you create, and do your best to keep stories simple; also reduce the volume of stories you work on any stage of the Software Development Life Cycle; this way your team will focus, and deliver more value.” For the next month: put each developer on one squad, even if some squads shrink or pause; plan each sprint at what the last three actually shipped; suspend the throughput bonus until a better measure replaces it; reopen the postmortem without the name and lift the performance plan while it runs; and track lead time and escaped defects instead of points. Idle time on a whiteboard will look like waste. It is the slack that lets work finish.


## Where the sources disagree

Stretch targets are the first open question. The case for the target: “The second basic premise is that difficult goals result in better performance than easy goals. This does not mean that difficult goals are always achieved, but our performance will usually be better when we intend to achieve harder goals.” The OpenStax text reports research that money raises motivation when it is tied to goal achievement, by raising goal commitment. The case against, from the same textbook: “The employees may fully understand what is wanted, yet if they feel the order is unreasonable or impossible, they may not exert much effort to accomplish it.” The OpenStax text reports self-determination theory's finding that intrinsic motivation falls as extrinsic rewards rise. The OpenStax text says letting people take part in setting goals often raises commitment, through ownership. A 40% target set from above, against a team that already misses its plan, sits on the wrong side of that split. Accountability is the second question. NHS Improvement's guide keeps a path to management action against the individual, including performance management and discipline, when no test excuses the act. “Third, the Board's focus on the context in which decision making occurred does not mean that individuals are not responsible and accountable. To the contrary, individuals always must assume responsibility for their actions. What it does mean is that NASA's problems cannot be solved simply by retirements, resignations, or transferring personnel.'” Against that, the Forest Service guide drops the hunt for a cause altogether: “The old investigation paradigm has been that we prevent accidents by discovering their root cause, and then fixing the cause to prevent a repeat of the accident. This perspective is problematic in complex systems and highly subjective because they are always outcome dependent. There can always be room for improvement, but we must remember that an accident represents a single data point with a single outcome. FLAs must avoid causal statements.” The Forest Service guide warns that recommendations, once implemented, often add complexity to the workplace and so increase risk. The DOE handbook keeps root cause analysis but aims it at what could have prevented the event, not at who caused it. Nothing in this incident suggests recklessness, so the performance plan has no case to rest on here. The third question is whether to restructure or start where you are. Hurtado's Kanban starts where you are, without changing titles or roles. Krivitsky, Larman and Flemm hold that every organisation already has a design, deliberate or accreted, that shapes its performance and culture. The first move above restructures on one point only, the multi-squad rule, and that is cheap to undo. The fourth is AI itself. “A key insight when designing with OT: Specialization and expertise are vanishing as limited resources, due to intelligence as a service. That makes it much easier to elevate an organization! So when you are deciding on new teams requiring new expert knowledge, shed the old assumptions, “We don’t have enough experts, therefore…”. Spin up another 10,000 agents.” More agents help where scarce expertise is the constraint. Where the constraint is too much work in progress, as here, more agents add more work in progress.
