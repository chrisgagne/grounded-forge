# Answer B

# What the war, shuttle, nuclear and wildfire manuals say about running a company

**A brief for a sceptical CEO**

None of these sources was written for a 300-person company. The Marine Corps, the Columbia Accident Investigation Board, the US Department of Energy and the US Forest Service all deal with work where mistakes kill people. Applying them to your company is my inference, not their claim.

Also, the management textbook is less naive than your playbook. OpenStax's *Principles of Management* already says that as organisations face uncertainty and fast change, "planning and controlling move closer to the point in the organization where the plans are implemented." Your playbook (cascaded KPIs, quarterly targets, reviews, a steering committee) uses the control-oriented half of the textbook and skips the half that agrees with the soldiers.

## 1. Cascading purpose versus cascading targets

The textbook says specific, difficult goals outperform loose direction. Its example is Weyerhaeuser truckers told to load to 94% of legal weight. The Marine Corps doctrine manual *MCDP 1: Warfighting* agrees that a mission has a task, then adds: "Of the two, the intent is predominant." When the situation changes, the task can become obsolete while the intent still holds. The manual's rules:

- The senior "prescribes the method of execution only to the degree that is essential for coordination."
- Subordinates "inform the commander of what they have done, but they do not wait for permission."
- Subordinates should understand the intent "at least two levels up."

**Why it matters for you.** A KPI cascade sends tasks down and leaves the purpose at the top. When the market moves, people keep hitting a number that no longer serves anything, and they're right to, because the number is what gets reviewed.

**The cheap fix.** Write every cascaded objective as "[task] in order to [purpose]", which is the Marines' own format. In quarterly reviews, ask whether people served the purpose, not only whether they hit the figure.

**Where the textbook still wins.** For stable, repeatable work like truck loading, plain targets work fine.

## 2. Targets and success distort what you hear

This is the sharpest contradiction. The textbook treats measurement as how the management cycle closes. It does list risks of control systems ("inappropriate goals", "inappropriate behaviour"), but as design faults you can fix.

The Columbia Board treats them as how the accident happened:

- A fixed launch-schedule date put pressure on the whole programme. No one wanted to say "We can't make that date."
- Managers "used past success as a justification for future flights."
- Each acceptance of foam damage made the next one easier. The Board writes: "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct."
- In the top decision meeting, "Mission management welcomed this opinion and sought no others."
- The Board's harshest line: "NASA's blind spot is it believes it has a strong safety culture."

The Army's guide to after-action reviews (TC 25-20) makes the same point at small scale: "Chart after chart of ratios, bar graphs, and tables quickly obscures any meaning". Charts used to grade units stifle the discussion. Its rule is that "An AAR does not grade success or failure."

**Why it matters for you.** Your steering committee is where the summarised green status lands. Hitting the quarter is your version of a successful landing: it tells you the risk didn't show up this time, not that it isn't there. The Board's suggested question translates directly. Ask "prove to me this hasn't been harmed," and don't wait for someone to prove that it has.

## 3. Performance reviews assume the individual is the unit of performance

The textbook's motivation theory (expectancy theory) needs rewards tied to individual performance, and it endorses tools like the 9-box talent grid. It does record that GE dropped forced ranking.

The DOE nuclear handbook starts from a different premise: "The organization is perfectly tuned to get the performance it receives from the workforce." It estimates that 85% or more of event causes come from organisational processes and culture, and that perhaps 90% of unsafe acts are blameless. Retraining someone after an absent-minded slip is "a waste of time and is an insult to the worker." The NHS just-culture guide says: "Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate."

**Why it matters for you.** Your best early-warning data comes from people admitting mistakes. The DOE handbook says "A just culture is a prerequisite for a reporting culture." If what someone admits in a lessons-learned discussion can end up in their annual review, you will stop hearing about mistakes.

**The cheap fix.** The Forest Service guide insists on a firewall between learning reviews and disciplinary action. Before an incident goes into anyone's appraisal, apply the NHS "substitution test": would comparable peers have done the same thing in the same situation?

## 4. Where the sources disagree with each other

**Boldness versus proving it safe.** The Marines want boldness: errors "stemming from overboldness are a necessary part of learning," there must be "no "zero defects" mentality," and timidity is punished more harshly than overboldness. The Columbia Board wants the opposite burden of proof: operations must be shown to be safe before they continue. Both condemn silence, so they aren't opposites.

My reconciliation (not theirs) is that the right posture depends on whether a decision can be undone. Your playbook uses one risk posture for everything. Sort decisions into two kinds:
- **Shuttle decisions** (irreversible): safety, customer data, cash runway, regulatory exposure. Require proof it's safe.
- **Battlefield decisions** (reversible): product bets, pricing tests, sales tactics. Be lenient on overboldness and hard on inaction.

**Root causes and action plans.** The DOE handbook runs root-cause analysis and corrective action programmes. The Columbia Board ends with hard structural recommendations, such as an independent technical authority. The Army guide says retrain immediately and revise the standard procedures.

The Forest Service guide disagrees: "FLAs must avoid causal statements" (an FLA is its facilitated learning review). It also warns that recommendations "add complexity to the workplace, which has the paradoxical effect of increasing risk." Managers jump to the action plan and believe the problem is solved.

Your change programme sits firmly with DOE and Columbia. The Forest Service warning is worth testing against it: count how many past action items added a process step and how many removed one.

**How blameless is "blameless"?** There's no consensus:
- The NHS and DOE decision trees still end in management action against the individual for some cases.
- The Forest Service sets the bar at reckless, wilful disregard, and calls punishing people after an accident "the surest way to kill a learning culture."
- The Marines counsel mistakes and give no "free license to act stupidly or recklessly."
- The Army expects soldiers to know when they fell short of the standard, and to retrain.

Anyone selling you "blameless culture" as settled doctrine is overselling it.

## What I would actually change

1. **Rewrite the cascade as task plus purpose,** and review against the purpose.
2. **Take learning conversations out of the appraisal file,** protected by a written firewall.
3. **Change how the steering committee works.** Columbia's lesson is that leaders own the culture ("Leaders create culture. It is their responsibility to change it."), so treat missing dissent as a warning sign and ask for dissenting views.
4. **Sort decisions into reversible and irreversible,** and set the risk posture for each kind openly.

Leave the targets on stable, routine work alone. The textbook is right about that.
