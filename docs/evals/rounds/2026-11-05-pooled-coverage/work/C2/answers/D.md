# Answer D

Question: Our executive team runs on the standard management playbook: strategy cascaded into KPIs, quarterly targets, performance reviews, a change programme with a steering committee. I've been reading things written for very different worlds, armies at war, shuttle launches, nuclear plants, wildfire crews, and I keep feeling they'd run a company differently. What surprising insights come from reading those traditions against the management textbooks? Where do they flatly contradict what the textbooks teach, and where does the contradiction actually matter for someone running a 300-person company?

Write it for a sceptical CEO, about two pages. Say where the sources disagree with each other, not only with the textbook.

The corpus lacks a second document for any of these traditions and lacks the strongest advocates of the textbook playbook. Nothing in the corpus tests whether these practices transfer to a 300-person commercial firm; every move from shuttle or battlefield to company is reasoning, not evidence. The corpus also lacks the primary high-reliability research, and lacks the business-side critics of targets. Ingestion candidates: Weick and Sutcliffe's Managing the Unexpected; Deming's Out of the Crisis; the US Army's ADP 6-0 Mission Command.

## Start with what the textbook already concedes

Before the contradictions, one surprise. On cascades: OpenStax's management text has organisations set goals at organisational, divisional, departmental and job level, and tells managers to make sure the lower-level goals add up to the higher ones. Then it adds a condition: “As organizations confront increasing environmental or technology-induced uncertainty, rapid environmental change, and turbulence, planning and controlling move closer to the point in the organization where the plans are implemented and carried out on a day-to-day basis.” The same text's rule of thumb is that the more complex a change, the more employees need to be involved in planning and carrying it out. On appraisal it reports the field's own doubts: “In a recent public survey by Deloitte Services, 58% of executives surveyed believed that their current performance management approach drives neither employee engagement nor high performance.” So the contradictions below are with the playbook as most companies run it.


## Cascade purpose, not only targets

A cascade assumes the top can see far enough to specify the work below it. MCDP 1 puts the opposite case: “Efforts to fully centralize military opera- tions and to exert complete control by a single decisionmaker are inconsistent with the intrinsically complex and distributed nature of war.” Its remedy is to hand down the purpose with the task: “The task denotes what is to be done, and sometimes when and where; the intent explains why. Of the two, the intent is pre-dominant. While a situation may change, making the task ob-solete, the intent is more lasting and continues to guide our actions.” MCDP 1 adds that the further ahead a plan looks, the less influence its author has, so the less precision it should try to impose. MCDP 1 also warns that information technology tempts seniors to keep precise, positive control over subordinates, which it calls incompatible with its philosophy of command. For a quarterly cascade, the practical reading is that a target handed down without its purpose goes stale the moment conditions change, and that a real-time dashboard is a temptation as much as a tool.


## Targets change the decisions they measure

The case for specific targets is real. OpenStax cites Weyerhaeuser, whose log drivers, told to load to 94 percent of legal weight rather than to do their best, competed to hit the figure and saved the company about $250,000 in nine months. The Columbia board watched the same mechanism with a worse payload: “Days of margin in that schedule were one of the principle metrics by which both programs came to be judged.” “Little by little, NASA was accepting more and more risk in order to stay on schedule.” The top is the last to see it: “Various members of NASA upper management gave a definite "no." In contrast, the workforce within both programs thought there was considerable management focus on Node 2 and resulting pressure to hold firm to that launch date, and individuals were becoming concerned that safety might be compromised. The weight of evidence supports the workforce view. Employees attributed the Node 2 launch date to the new Administrator, Sean O'Keefe, who was appointed to execute a Space Station management plan he had proposed as Deputy Director of the White House Office of Management and Budget.” The nuclear handbook names the leadership habit underneath: “It is a normal human behavior to want to “get the job done” rather than taking the time to do the job safely due to overconfidence, underestimating risks, and so on. This is especially true when supervisors expect and reward the results and are silent about behaviors needed to stay safe.” The DOE handbook defines performance as behaviour plus results, because good results can come from questionable behaviour and bad results from compliant behaviour. The wildfire guide goes further: “Our natural intuition (our common sense) is to let outcomes draw the line between success and failure and to base safety programs on outcomes. This is shortsighted and eventually dangerous.” The Army's AAR manual warns that chart after chart of ratios turns a review into a grading of the unit, which stifles discussion. A target works well on a stable, repeatable task like loading trucks. On uncertain work, the quarterly number teaches people which risks get rewarded, and the people furthest from the steering committee usually see the cost first.


## Performance reviews ask the wrong question

An appraisal cycle treats performance as a property of the person. The Columbia board names the habit this produces: “Many accident investigations make the same mistake in defining causes. They identify the widget that broke or malfunctioned, then locate the person most closely connected with the technical failure: the engineer who miscalculated an analysis, the operator who missed signals or pulled the wrong switches, the supervisor who failed to listen, or the manager who made bad decisions.” The nuclear handbook turns the question round: “Instead of asking how the individual failed the organization, the question “how did the organization fail the individual?”” The NHS guide sets the default: “Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate - most patient safety issues have deeper causes and require wider action.” What the wrong question costs is information. The DOE handbook warns that people who expect to be punished for their errors keep the information about those errors hidden. The Forest Service guide calls punishing or blaming the people involved in an accident the surest way to kill a learning culture. The Marines apply the same logic to their own juniors: “These traits carried to excess can lead to rashness, but we must realize that errors by junior leaders stemming from overboldness are a neces-sary part of learning.6 We should deal with such errors leni-ently; there must be no "zero defects" mentality. Abolishing "zero defects" means that we do not stifle boldness or initia-tive through the threat of punishment.” MCDP 1 asks that critiques dwell less on what was done than on why it was done and why it produced the result it did. A review built to rank 300 people against their targets cannot also be the place where they tell you what went wrong.


## Change programmes add complexity and filter the news

Start with the best-known textbook change model: In Kotter's model as OpenStax presents it, top managers open by convincing leaders and staff that change is unavoidable, the burning platform. The Columbia board was warier of the programme itself: “Changes in organizational structure should be made only with careful consideration of their effect on the system and their possible unintended consequences. Changes that make the organization more complex may create new ways that it can fail.48 When changes are put in place, the risk of error initially increases, as old ways of doing things compete with new.” The wildfire guide says the same of fixes: “In many circumstances, recommendations actually interfere with learning. Often recommendations, when implemented, add complexity to the workplace, which has the paradoxical effect of increasing risk.” A steering committee's particular weakness is what reaches it. “Generally, the higher information is transmitted in a hierarchy, the more it gets "rolled-up," abbreviated, and simplified. Sometimes information gets lost altogether, as weak signals drop from memos, problem identification systems, and formal presentations.” Sign-off by committee can dissolve ownership too: “In fact, the Rogers Commission found that the Flight Readiness process only affirmed the pre-Challenger engineering risk assessments.'-' Equally troubling, the Board found that the Flight Readiness process, which is built on consensus verified by signatures of all responsible parties, in effect renders no one accountable.” None of this rules a committee out. The DOE handbook has the senior team sit as a steering committee that holds department managers to account for human performance through measures, self-assessments and corrective-action data. The difference is the committee's job: hunting for bad news and weak signals, rather than collecting green status reports and signatures.


## The traditions disagree with each other about risk

Acting under uncertainty comes first. MCDP 1: “Thus, as a basis for action, any decision is generally better than no decision.” The nuclear handbook sets the opposite default: “The bias is set on proving work activities are safe before proceeding, rather than proving them unsafe before halting. Personnel do not proceed and do not allow others to proceed when safety is uncertain.” The Columbia board holds that high-risk operations must be proved safe rather than proved unsafe, and faults NASA for inverting that burden. Yet the same board calls the ability to run centralised when that fits, and decentralised when that fits, the hallmark of a high-reliability organisation. On what the organisation is for, the DOE working definition reads: The DOE handbook's working definition of safety culture makes safe performance of work the overriding priority. The Forest Service guide: “While safety is an essential business practice, our agency does not exist to be safe or to protect our employees. We exist to accomplish a taxpayer-funded mission as efficiently as possible—knowing that many activities we choose to perform are inherently hazardous (for example, firefighting, driving, flying in helicopters, horseback riding, tree cutting, and even walking through a forest).” For a company the useful move is to sort decisions rather than pick a school: the prove-it-safe default for the few that cannot be undone, such as a bet-the-firm contract or a data migration, and the act-now default for the many that can.


## They disagree about causes and blame

Next, what an investigation should find. The DOE handbook: The DOE handbook rejects inattention to detail and not following procedures as root causes, but keeps the idea of a root cause, the one whose correction prevents recurrence. The Forest Service guide: “There can always be room for improvement, but we must remember that an accident represents a single data point with a single outcome. FLAs must avoid causal statements.” On blame, the NHS guide keeps a door open: The NHS guide still provides for management action, from individual training and performance management to role changes, increased supervision and, where warranted, disciplinary processes. The Columbia board keeps individuals responsible for their actions while insisting that NASA's problems cannot be solved by retirements, resignations or transfers. For an executive team, the useful move is to use each where it fits: keep root-cause discipline for process and equipment failures, use the reconstruct-the-conditions approach for judgement calls made under pressure, and keep individual consequences for the rare knowing departure from a rule that was workable.


## Where the contradiction matters at 300 people

Three contradictions earn a change at your scale. Cascade the purpose with every target, and let teams change the target when the purpose demands it. Separate the conversation about pay and promotion from the conversation about what went wrong, or the second one dies. Judge the change programme by whether bad news reaches the steering committee faster than it used to. The Columbia board puts the ownership plainly: “Leaders create culture. It is their responsibility to change it. Top administrators must take responsibility for risk, failure, and safety by remaining alert to the effects their decisions have on the system.” The Forest Service guide reports that its own reviews tended to stop at the people on the scene and seldom examined the decisions of the officials who commissioned them. That is the sceptic's test for all of this: whether the executive team's own targets, schedules and sign-offs sit inside the review or above it. On stable, repeatable work your standard playbook holds up; the contradictions bite on uncertain work and on decisions that cannot be undone.
