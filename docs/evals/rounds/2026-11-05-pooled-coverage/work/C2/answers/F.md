# Four lethal trades read against the management textbook: a brief for a sceptical CEO

**First, a caveat.** These sources were written for work where a mistake kills people and can't be undone: the Marine Corps doctrine manual *Warfighting* (MCDP 1), the Columbia Accident Investigation Board report, the US Department of Energy's nuclear *Human Performance Improvement Handbook*, the US Forest Service's wildfire *Learning Review* guide, and the Army's after-action review manual (TC 25-20). None of them was written about companies. When I say something applies to a 300-person firm, that is my judgement, not theirs.

**Second, the textbook is not your playbook.** The OpenStax *Principles of Management* text says vague, direction-only planning ("domain planning") belongs at the top of an organisation, where uncertainty is high. Precise goal-setting, it says, belongs in "the technical core, where there is less uncertainty." Cascading measurable targets from the top down to everyone goes further than the textbook does.

## 1. Pass down the purpose, not just the target

The textbook's case for specific targets is real: "Research suggests that goal planning results in higher levels of performance than does domain planning alone."

*Warfighting* builds the hierarchy around something else. Every order has two parts: "The task describes the action to be taken while the intent describes the purpose of the action." The purpose takes priority, because events can make a task pointless while the purpose still holds. Subordinates should understand the purpose of the commander two levels above them. And "the senior prescribes the method of execution only to the degree that is essential for coordination." The manual even warns that good information technology "can tempt us to try to maintain precise, positive control over subordinates." That describes a live KPI dashboard well.

**Why it matters to you:** a KPI is a task. When the market moves, a team that holds only the number will either chase a target that no longer makes sense or stall. Each top-level objective needs an "in order to…" sentence that two layers down can repeat.

## 2. Targets and dates teach people to accept warning signs

The Columbia board found that the shuttle programme did not fail through one bad decision. Foam kept breaking off the fuel tank on launch, and each flight that came back safely made it look acceptable: "With no engineering analysis, Shuttle managers used past success as a justification for future flights." "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct." The board found that there was real pressure to hold a launch date, and that "The Program was operating too close to too many margins."

The nuclear handbook makes the measurement point directly: "things are counted because they can be," and results are lagging indicators that don't show whether performance will continue. The Army manual bans grading in reviews: "Chart after chart of ratios, bar graphs, and tables quickly obscures any meaning."

**Where this flatly contradicts the textbook:** goal theory says hard, specific targets raise performance. These sources say a hard target plus a run of good results is exactly how organisations come to treat warning signs as normal. Both can be true. The danger is the quarter you hit despite a warning sign, not the quarter you miss.

The board's fix is to move the burden of proof: "operations must be proved safe, rather than the other way around." Its example question is "Prove to me that Columbia has not been harmed." Ask the same of a forecast that looks green.

## 3. Performance reviews decide what you will never be told

The nuclear handbook says "A just culture is a prerequisite for a reporting culture." It also says "The organization is perfectly tuned to get the performance it receives from the workforce." The wildfire guide is blunter: "Information is the lifeblood of safety." It asks whether learning should depend on luck: "Should a chance occurrence of two inches be the determining factor for organizational learning?" (A falling tree missed a firefighter by that much.) The NHS Just Culture guide starts from the view that "Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate."

The textbook's expectancy theory says the opposite: pay and reward should depend on results. Judging by results is what these sources call outcome bias. It also teaches staff to hide near misses, because near misses don't hurt their numbers but admitting them does.

To be fair, the textbook already argues against punishment as a main tool and against forced ranking. The gap is narrower than it looks, but your review cycle probably sits on the wrong side of it.

## 4. The steering committee is part of the system it is steering

The Columbia board found that information was condensed and simplified as it rose through management. It warned that fixing only the broken part or the nearest person creates a "belief that the underlying problem has been solved." It noted that NASA's post-Challenger reforms had faded by the time of Columbia. And: "NASA's blind spot is it believes it has a strong safety culture."

The wildfire guide adds two warnings:
- Leaders who receive a review tend to jump to the recommendations, write an action plan, and believe the problem is solved.
- Recommendations often "add complexity to the workplace, which has the paradoxical effect of increasing risk."

It also found that reviews rarely examine the decisions of the senior official who ordered them.

The textbook itself says top-down change "rely[s] on mechanistic assumptions about the nature of an organization." The Columbia board's version: "Leaders create culture. It is their responsibility to change it." Put the steering committee's own decisions on the review agenda. Treat green status reports as something to test, not something to accept.

## 5. Where the sources disagree with each other

- **Bold or cautious?** *Warfighting* wants "no 'zero defects' mentality." It deals gently with errors of overboldness and harshly with inaction. The nuclear handbook aims for zero significant events and tells workers to pause when unsure. It wants conservative decisions that put safety ahead of near-term production. My reading is that the difference is about whether the damage can be undone and whether there is an opponent to beat on speed. Your sales team and your payroll or security teams should not run under the same rule.
- **Retraining.** The Army manual makes retraining routine: soldiers must understand "they did not perform the task to standard." The nuclear handbook calls retraining people for automatic slips "a waste of time and is an insult to the worker." The wildfire guide lists "forced retraining" among the disciplinary actions it promises not to take.
- **Causes and recommendations.** The nuclear handbook runs root-cause analysis. The Columbia board issued recommendations and new independent authorities. The wildfire guide says its reviews "must avoid causal statements," and only issues recommendations through a separate, vetted process.
- **Where blame starts.** The NHS and nuclear guides use step-by-step tests to decide when one person is at fault. The wildfire guide sets the bar at reckless and wilful disregard. *Warfighting* tolerates honest error but says people who simply agree with the boss to get ahead ("yes-men") will not be tolerated. The Columbia board left personal accountability to a separate process from its investigation.

## What I would change on Monday (my synthesis)

1. Add a purpose ("in order to…") to every top objective, and test whether people two levels down can state it.
2. In quarterly reviews, ask what deviation you got away with, not only whether the target was hit.
3. Separate learning reviews from performance reviews, with a written promise that what people say in one won't be used in the other.
4. Judge decisions on what was known at the time, not on how they turned out.
5. Have someone outside the steering committee review the committee's own calls.

**Where it matters less.** None of these sources gives evidence that the ideas work in commercial firms. The wildfire guide's reluctance to make recommendations may be impractical when you need a decision by Friday. The textbook's planning-to-returns evidence is thin (it says so itself), but so is the evidence against it for firms like yours.
