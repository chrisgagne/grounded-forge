# Answer G

Question: Our executive team runs on the standard management playbook: strategy cascaded into KPIs, quarterly targets, performance reviews, a change programme with a steering committee. I've been reading things written for very different worlds, armies at war, shuttle launches, nuclear plants, wildfire crews, and I keep feeling they'd run a company differently. What surprising insights come from reading those traditions against the management textbooks? Where do they flatly contradict what the textbooks teach, and where does the contradiction actually matter for someone running a 300-person company?

Write it for a sceptical CEO, about two pages. Say where the sources disagree with each other, not only with the textbook.

The demo corpus carries one management textbook (OpenStax Principles of Management) and four operational traditions (Marine Corps doctrine, the Columbia accident report, the DOE human performance handbooks, the Forest Service learning review guide, plus the Army's AAR circular). None of them studies a company of 300 people, none tests corporate KPI or OKR systems directly, and the wildfire material covers how crews learn after an accident, not how they are led day to day. Everything said here about a company is inference from those sources, not a finding in them. Three ingestion candidates would let the corpus answer more directly: Stephen Bungay's The Art of Action (mission command applied to business strategy), Weick and Sutcliffe's Managing the Unexpected (high reliability practice for ordinary organisations), and Jerry Muller's The Tyranny of Metrics (evidence on what target regimes do to organisations).

## The textbook is more careful than the playbook

Start with a fair reading of the textbook, because a sceptic will. The OpenStax management text gives offsetting uncertainty and change as the first of its reasons for managers to plan. It reports research that planning toward specific goals yields higher performance than setting a general direction alone. But it does not claim that holds everywhere: “Situations in which managers are likely to engage in domain planning include (1) when there is a recognized need for flexibility, (2) when people cannot agree on goals, (3) when an organization’s external environment is unstable and highly uncertain, and (4) when an organization is starting up or is in a transitional period.” It describes Kotter's top-down eight-step model as most useful when the desired change is reasonably predictable, and warns that employees who had no hand in shaping the plans may resist it. Its rule of thumb is that the more complex the change, the greater the need to involve employees in planning and carrying it out. So the quarrel is with a playbook that runs specific quarterly targets and a top-down change programme everywhere, including the parts of the company where conditions are neither stable nor predictable.


## Cascade the purpose, not the target

The playbook's cascade sends targets down and brings individual scores back up. The management text describes the method: “Together they identify common goals, define the results expected from each individual, and use these measurements to guide the operation of their unit and to assess individual contributions.61 In this process, the knowledge and skills of many organizational members are used.” The Marine doctrine cascades something else: “The task denotes what is to be done, and sometimes when and where; the intent explains why. Of the two, the intent is pre-dominant. While a situation may change, making the task ob-solete, the intent is more lasting and continues to guide our actions.” MCDP 1 still cascades from the top, but what flows down is intent, which it says gives subordinates the context to use their initiative from the bottom up. It holds that the most a commander can hope for is a general framework of order over the disorder, influencing the flow of action rather than controlling each event. And it says the further ahead a plan looks, the less precision it should try to impose. For a 300-person company the change is small and cheap. Write every quarterly target with its purpose beside it, and tell people that when the target goes stale before the quarter ends, the purpose governs. A KPI with no stated purpose cannot be departed from intelligently; it can only be hit or missed.


## When the number becomes the mission

Here is what one date did to the shuttle programme, in the Columbia board's words: “These charts illustrate that both the Space Shuttle and Space Station Programs were being managed to a particular launch date — February 19, 2004. Days of margin in that schedule were one of the principle metrics by which both programs came to be judged.” “No one at NASA wants to be the one to stand up and say, "We can't make that date."” It concludes that, little by little, NASA accepted more and more risk to stay on schedule. “Working evenings and weekends just to meet the International Space Station Node 2 deadline sent a signal to employees that schedule is important. When paired with the "faster, better, cheaper" NASA motto of the 1990s and cuts that dramatically decreased safety personnel, efficiency becomes a strong signal and safety a weak one.” Measuring is not the problem; grading with the measure is. The Army's guide to after-action reviews draws that line: “The danger lies in statistics for statistics' sake. Chart after chart of ratios, bar graphs, and tables quickly obscures any meaning and lends itself to a "grading" of unit performance. This stifles discussion and degrades the AAR's value.” The DOE tools volume warns that things are often counted because they can be while saying little about how the organisation works now, and that a results indicator only lags. The Columbia board does not condemn targets: it calls aggressive schedules often a sign of a healthy institution, while warning that schedule pressure on other goals must be watched. The board also found that where status was missing, numbers were the great equaliser, so data is how a voice without rank gets weighed. So keep the KPIs, stop grading people with them in the room where you most need the truth, and give the right to say a number is wrong to someone who does not own the target. The Columbia board calls it crucial to separate programme managers, who must mind cost and schedule, from the owners of technical requirements, who mind safety and rigour.


## The performance review aims at the wrong unit

The review cycle assumes results are mostly individual. The nuclear handbook starts from the other end: “The organization is perfectly tuned to get the performance it receives from the workforce.” It holds that no counselling, training or motivation can change a person's fallibility. Even the management text records that GE and others dropped forced ranking because it bred internal competition and undermined collaboration. The Army's AAR guide says the review grades neither success nor failure, because there are always weaknesses to improve and strengths to sustain. Mixing learning with judgement costs you information. “Punishing or blaming employees involved in an accident is the surest way to kill a learning culture.” It requires a firewall between its learning analysis and any administrative action against the employee, calling information the lifeblood of safety. The Columbia board took privileged statements and left any accounting of personal responsibility to a separate investigation. Where to draw the line for sanction is a real choice, not a settled one. “These traits carried to excess can lead to rashness, but we must realize that errors by junior leaders stemming from overboldness are a neces-sary part of learning.6 We should deal with such errors leni-ently; there must be no "zero defects" mentality. Abolishing "zero defects" means that we do not stifle boldness or initia-tive through the threat of punishment.” It deals severely instead with inaction and timidity, and will not accept a lack of orders as an excuse. The DOE handbook faults organisations that name, blame, shame and retrain people for the consequence of an act rather than its intent. A review built on these would ask two questions the quarterly score cannot answer: did this person act sensibly on what they knew at the time, and did they duck a decision that was theirs to make?


## Speed or proof

How fast to act on incomplete information has no single answer. The Marine doctrine: “Thus, as a basis for action, any decision is generally better than no decision.” It warns that waiting for certainty surrenders the initiative, and that excessive control over subordinates slows tempo and inhibits initiative. The nuclear handbook sets the opposite default: “The bias is set on proving work activities are safe before proceeding, rather than proving them unsafe before halting. Personnel do not proceed and do not allow others to proceed when safety is uncertain.” The Columbia board says high-risk operations must be proved safe rather than proved unsafe, and that NASA inverted this burden of proof. MCDP 1 itself says decisions should not be rushed where time is not the limit, as in deliberate planning. The working rule for a company is to sort decisions by what a mistake costs and whether it can be undone: act at Marine speed on reversible calls, and require proof of safety before anything that can hurt a customer, an employee or the balance sheet beyond repair. The steering committee is where the second kind goes wrong. The Columbia board found that the higher information travels in a hierarchy, the more it is rolled up, abbreviated and simplified, and that weak signals drop out. A committee that hears only good news can borrow one question from the shuttle report: “Imagine the difference if any Shuttle manager had simply asked, "Prove to me that Columbia has not been harmed."” The DOE handbook has members of the senior management team sit on a Human Performance Steering Committee of their own. The committee is not the problem; the question it asks is.


## After a failure: fix the cause, or stop naming one

After something goes wrong, you have to pick a method, and the two on offer are not compatible. The DOE handbook keeps root cause analysis, but points it at what could have prevented an event rather than at who caused it. “Events will continue as long as event investigations stop prematurely at the active human error. The true causes (typically organizational weaknesses) will not be discovered—will remain latent or hidden—and errors and events will persist.” The Forest Service guide calls finding and fixing the root cause the old paradigm, outcome-dependent and problematic in complex systems, treats an accident as a single data point, and bars causal statements from its analyses. “In many circumstances, recommendations actually interfere with learning. Often recommendations, when implemented, add complexity to the workplace, which has the paradoxical effect of increasing risk.” The Columbia board, which did issue recommendations, warns that structural changes that make an organisation more complex may create new ways for it to fail. It also holds that NASA's problems could not be solved by retirements, resignations or transfers. For a change programme the Forest Service guide's rule is direct: any new rule, policy or procedure that comes out of a review is created with the field and vetted by the people who do the work before it is adopted.


## Where the contradiction matters at 300 people

None of this makes your company a nuclear plant. Even the Forest Service guide says its agency exists not to be safe but to accomplish its mission efficiently, knowing that much of the work is hazardous. Most of a quarter's work is reversible, and targets and reviews do tolerably there. The contradictions bite in three places: where a target can quietly trade away something you cannot buy back, where a review punishes the honest report you need, and where the executive team cannot see how work is really done. The Forest Service guide's diagnostic question is how the work was actually done against how managers expected it to be done. “Our natural intuition (our common sense) is to let outcomes draw the line between success and failure and to base safety programs on outcomes. This is shortsighted and eventually dangerous.” The Columbia board's principle is that leaders create culture and that changing it is their responsibility. That puts the work with the executive team itself rather than with the change programme it oversees.
