## 1. The army also cascades, but it cascades purpose, not numbers

MCDP 1 splits a mission into a task (what to do) and an intent (why), and states: "Of the two, the intent is predominant" [mcdp1-warfighting]. It says intent lets subordinates "depart from the original plan when the unforeseen occurs" [mcdp1-warfighting]. It describes a "top-down flow of intent" and expects subordinates to understand intent "at least two levels up" [mcdp1-warfighting]. Under mission tactics, "They inform the commander of what they have done, but they do not wait for permission" [mcdp1-warfighting]. The doctrine also names a single "main effort" that gets "priority for support of any kind," which means "exercising strict economy elsewhere" [mcdp1-warfighting].

This is not a licence for everyone to do as they like. The doctrine warns that decentralised initiative without some means of providing unity would "dissipate our strength" [mcdp1-warfighting].

My inference: the contradiction with your playbook is not "cascade versus no cascade" but what gets cascaded, because a KPI cascade passes down tasks, and tasks are the part the doctrine expects to go stale.

My inference: a quick test is whether a team lead two levels below you could say why their targets exist, and which other team they should sacrifice for this quarter.

## 2. The shuttle record: targets bend the criteria, not just the effort

Feynman wrote that "The acceptance and success of these flights is taken as evidence of safety," when in fact erosion was a warning "that something is wrong" [caib-report-vol1]. On schedule pressure, he wrote that "subtly, and often with apparently logical arguments, the criteria are altered so that flights may still be certified in time" [caib-report-vol1]. The Rogers Commission found "a relentless pressure on NASA to increase the flight rate" [caib-report-vol1]. It also found that NASA had required "a contractor to prove that it was not safe to launch, rather than proving it was safe" [caib-report-vol1].

My inference: the danger of hard quarterly targets is less open cheating than a quiet redefinition of "done" that looks reasonable each time.

My inference: in your steering committee, ask who carries the burden of proof, the person who wants to proceed or the person who wants to stop.

## 3. Dashboards remove weak signals, and following the process is not the same as being safe

The Columbia Board writes that "the higher information is transmitted in a hierarchy, the more it gets 'rolled-up,' abbreviated, and simplified" [caib-report-vol1]. It also warns that "The same conclusions, repeated over time, can result in problems eventually being deemed non-problems" [caib-report-vol1]. A key omission was "not technically a violation of NASA's own procedures," yet it showed "just how easily the flight preparation process can be compromised" [caib-report-vol1].

My inference: a green dashboard rolled up through quarterly reviews may record that nothing new was asked, not that nothing is wrong, so governance that mainly checks process compliance can approve accumulating risk.

## 4. Accountability means an honest account, not a rating

The wildfire guide says: "Accountability means to give an honest account" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. It describes workers as "inheritors of the tools, trainings, procedures, the production incentives and the safety-vs.-production values of the workplace" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Its learning reviews assure participants "that there shall be no punitive actions" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. The guide also records that, after one fire, employees refused to talk to investigators because they did not expect fair treatment [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. The DOE handbook likewise endorses a culture where "people who make honest errors and mistakes are not blamed" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].

My inference: this is where the contradiction with your playbook bites hardest, because if a post-mortem can feed into someone's appraisal, you should expect a less candid account of how they actually traded speed against quality.

## 5. The surprise: the hazard tradition keeps the change programme but demotes the committee

The DOE handbook's change process includes "consider a steering committee." However, it calls such a committee "a temporary organization established in an advisory capacity" and says the need for one "is dependent upon the significance of the change" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2]. It frames change management as error control: "Effective change management reduces the potential for error by managers when they modify their way of doing business" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2]. It also argues that "most daily management activity involves some degree of change," such as changes to crew composition or schedules [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2].

These sources also do not reject metrics. The DOE handbook lists "Failure to establish performance indicators and track trends on the effectiveness of the corrective actions and lessons learned" as an at-risk practice [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2]. The marketing textbook prefers to "identify a handful of the most useful KPIs" over a "laundry list of 300 KPIs" [openstax-principles-marketing].

My inference: the high-reliability material in this notebook does not contradict KPIs. It redirects some of them toward whether fixes actually worked, and it treats your own managerial changes as a risk to control.

## Where the sources disagree with each other

**Rules versus judgement.** The DOE handbook ties safety to "the rigor with which people follow and perform work activities according to correctly written procedures, expectations, and standards" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. The Columbia Board instead calls the ability to operate centrally or decentrally "when appropriate" "the hallmark of a high-reliability organization" [caib-report-vol1]. MCDP 1 says subordinates act without waiting for permission [mcdp1-warfighting].

My inference: no source here sets the military and nuclear positions against each other directly, but read together they imply that you must decide domain by domain which decisions sit under a tight rule and which sit with the person closest to the problem.

**Can failure be engineered out?** The Board sets out two opposing theories. High Reliability Theory says good design "can compensate for inevitable human shortcomings." Normal Accident Theory says failure-free aspirations are "inevitably doomed to fail" [caib-report-vol1]. The DOE handbook is more optimistic, describing HROs as "almost never experiencing an unwanted event" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].

**Fixes can become the next blind spot.** After Challenger, the Rogers Commission recommended an independent safety office [caib-report-vol1]. The Columbia Board then found that "in-family," a category "introduced post-Challenger," meant "by definition the problems that were shifted into the lesser 'in-family' category got less attention" [caib-report-vol1].

My inference: a new governance layer added after a failure deserves the same suspicion as the failure itself.

**Who owns improvement.** The DOE model relies on a management champion with authority to devote resources [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2]. The Scrum Guide gives the team itself the job of identifying improvements, saying "The most impactful improvements are addressed as soon as possible" [scrum-guide-2020].

**Within the wildfire guide.** People-focused reviews produce lessons "without recommendations." Meanwhile, the guide's equipment reviews lead directly to improvements and updated training [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].

## Where it matters at 300 people

My inference: the contradictions matter most in four situations:
- when a plan goes stale mid-quarter
- when a cross-team trade-off has to be made without escalating
- when your approval loop is slower than events
- when a failure review touches someone's rating

My inference: the contradictions matter least for stable, routine work. They also matter less for the few areas where a hidden error would be severe and irreversible, such as cash, data, regulatory exposure or safety. In those areas, the shuttle and DOE material argues for tighter discipline, not looser.
