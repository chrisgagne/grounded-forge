## Question

Our executive team runs on the standard management playbook: strategy cascaded into KPIs, quarterly targets, performance reviews, a change programme with a steering committee. I've been reading things written for very different worlds, armies at war, shuttle launches, nuclear plants, wildfire crews, and I keep feeling they'd run a company differently. What surprising insights come from reading those traditions against the management textbooks? Where do they flatly contradict what the textbooks teach, and where does the contradiction actually matter for someone running a 300-person company?

Write it for a sceptical CEO, about two pages. Say where the sources disagree with each other, not only with the textbook.

# Reading the high-stakes traditions against the management playbook

**What this notebook can support.** The notebook holds the following sources:

- **Military:** the Marine Corps doctrine *Warfighting*.
- **Shuttle launches:** the Columbia Accident Investigation Board (CAIB) report, which also summarises the Challenger findings.
- **Nuclear and energy:** two volumes of a Department of Energy (DOE) human-performance handbook.
- **Wildfire:** a wildfire learning-review guide.
- **Practice:** an Open Practice Library entry.
- **The textbook side:** several management, organisational-behaviour, accounting and marketing textbooks.

No source in this notebook covers Mann Gulch, "drop your tools," Goodhart's law, gaming of targets, critiques of performance appraisal, or quarterly targets as such. Nothing below claims evidence that these practices work in companies.

## 1. The surprise: none of these traditions is anti-planning

*Warfighting* says the planning process applies broadly. "This systematic process of identifying the objective and planning a course to obtain it applies to all areas and levels of preparations" [mcdp1-warfighting]. CAIB says "schedule deadlines are an important management tool" [caib-report-vol1]. DOE recommends that you "Establish metrics to measure program performance and evaluate the effectiveness of actions implemented from lessons learned" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2].

My inference: if you expected these worlds to reject objectives, deadlines and metrics, they don't. The contradictions are narrower and sit in specific places.

## 2. Where they contradict the textbook

**The purpose outranks the target.** The accounting textbook defines a performance measurement system as one that "evaluates management in a way that will link the goals of the corporation with those of the manager" [openstax-accounting-vol2]. *Warfighting* splits every mission into the task and the intent, and says "Of the two, the intent is predominant" [mcdp1-warfighting]. It adds that a changing situation may make the task obsolete while the intent continues to guide action [mcdp1-warfighting]. It also says a subordinate "who is not given a clear purpose for the assigned mission should ask for one" [mcdp1-warfighting].

My inference: in this vocabulary a quarterly KPI is a task. The doctrine expects people to drop it when circumstances change, whereas a pay-for-the-number system rewards holding to it.

**Permission versus reporting.** The textbook change process builds a "guiding coalition" of influential people who act as ambassadors [openstax-principles-management]. *Warfighting* says subordinates "inform the commander of what they have done, but they do not wait for permission" [mcdp1-warfighting]. It also says "The senior intervenes in a subordinate's execution only by exception" [mcdp1-warfighting]. DOE's own change guidance says only "Consider a Steering Committee," calling it "a temporary organization established in an advisory capacity" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2].

My inference: a permanent steering committee that approves changes in advance has no counterpart in either source.

**Your dashboard filters out what you most need.** CAIB says that as information moves up a hierarchy, "weak signals drop from memos, problem identification systems, and formal presentations" [caib-report-vol1]. It also says "the same conclusions, repeated over time, can result in problems eventually being deemed non-problems" [caib-report-vol1].

My inference: a target-versus-actual review is a roll-up of this kind. Recurring anomalies that never breach a threshold are the ones most likely to disappear.

**Stretching a target adds risk nobody books.** CAIB says that pushing schedules beyond what engineers think reasonable adds risk in small increments until managers lose sight of the total. In its words, "Little by little, NASA was accepting more and more risk in order to stay on schedule" [caib-report-vol1]. The Rogers Commission criticised NASA for requiring "a contractor to prove that it was not safe to launch, rather than proving it was safe" [caib-report-vol1].

My inference: the question for your forecast reviews is who has to prove what when someone says "we shouldn't commit to this."

**Fix the system, not the person.** DOE rejects the belief that removing "a few bad performers" fixes things. It says "people cannot perform better than the organization supporting them" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. It describes a just culture in which "people who make honest errors and mistakes are not blamed" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. The wildfire guide says that, for many in the fire community, "'accountability' had become synonymous with 'punitive actions'" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Its learning reviews now carry an assurance of "no punitive actions" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].

My inference: a review system that explains results mainly by individual effort sits badly with both sources. No source in this notebook directly critiques performance appraisal, though.

**Change programmes can succeed on paper.** The textbook opens change by trying to "establish a sense of urgency" and to "create a burning platform" [openstax-principles-management]. CAIB found that after Challenger, "The fact that many changes had been made supported a belief in the safety of the system." It also found that dangerous aspects of the 1986 culture "remained unchanged" [caib-report-vol1]. DOE describes its approach as "not so much a program as it is a distinct way of thinking" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. It notes that "most daily management activity involves some degree of change" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2].

My inference: counting delivered initiatives is the reassurance CAIB warns against. Small, routine reorganisations may deserve more care than they get.

**A good track record is not proof of control.** DOE warns against relying on experience to maintain control. It says "Competence does not guarantee positive control" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].

My inference: a clean record in performance reviews can reflect skill or simply luck so far.

## 3. Where the traditions disagree with each other

**Rules versus judgement.** DOE says people in highly reliable organisations "practice error-prevention rigorously, regardless of their perception of a task's risk and simplicity, how routine it is, and how competent the performer" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. *Warfighting* calls doctrine "authoritative" but "not prescriptive" [mcdp1-warfighting]. It says method should be prescribed "only to the degree that is essential for coordination" [mcdp1-warfighting]. CAIB holds both positions at once:

- It says that in unprecedented conditions "flexibility and democratic process should take priority over bureaucratic response."
- In the same passage it also calls for "new structuring, rules, and management training" [caib-report-vol1].
- It contrasts the submarine programme's rigour with NASA's, saying "NASA requirements are clearly documented but are also more easily waived" [caib-report-vol1].

My inference: the split seems to follow the kind of work. Procedure dominates in repeatable, hazardous operations, and judgement dominates in fluid, novel situations. Your company probably has both kinds of work under one review cadence.

**Who can say no.** DOE ties its practices to "line management responsibility for safety" [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. CAIB praises the Navy's "separate compliance verification organization." It faults the Shuttle Program for controlling both engineering and safety [caib-report-vol1]. *Warfighting* has seniors intervene only by exception [mcdp1-warfighting].

**The person or the network.** DOE puts its emphasis on the individual keeping control and resisting overconfidence [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. The wildfire guide describes moving away from "judging actions or decisions as right or wrong and people as good or bad." It moves toward understanding "a network of influences, or performance shaping factors" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].

**Recommendations or none.** CAIB issues recommendations. The wildfire learning review develops lessons "without recommendations" [lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].

My inference: your post-mortems currently end in owners and actions, which is CAIB's form. The wildfire tradition would argue that this format discourages candour.

**Even the military isn't decentralised without limit.** *Warfighting* says "we cannot allow decentralized initiative without some means of providing unity, or focus, to the various efforts" [mcdp1-warfighting]. It names a main effort: "This is how I will achieve a decision; everything else is secondary" [mcdp1-warfighting].

My inference: a dozen KPIs of equal weight differ from this more than a cascade does.

## 4. Where it matters for a 300-person company

My inference, in priority order:

1. **Let people abandon a target when its reason has changed.** Make them state the intent, and treat a missing intent as a leadership failure, not a junior one.
2. **Build a channel for weak signals that bypasses the KPI roll-up.** DOE's suggestions to "Designate a devil's advocate" and call "timeouts" are cheap to adopt [doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].
3. **Price every stretch of a target in risk, and flip the burden of proof in go/no-go meetings.**
4. **Separate blameless learning reviews from appraisal.** Distinguish honest error from wilful violation, not hits from misses.
5. **Shrink the steering committee to a temporary advisory group, independent of the people it judges.** Judge it by changed behaviour, not by initiatives delivered.

My inference: at 300 people, the long-standing, face-to-face relationships that *Warfighting* relies on are actually achievable. That makes decentralisation less risky for you than for a larger firm.

One closing note for the sceptic: DOE lists ignoring operating experience from "other industries" as an at-risk practice [doe-hpi-handbook-vol2].
