# K01A-1

What is the normalization of deviance, and how did it show up in NASA's decisions about foam loss?

Normalization of deviance is a concept coined by sociologist Diane Vaughan in her analysis of the 1986 Challenger disaster[1:OUT:caib-report-vol1][2:IN:caib-report-vol1]. It describes an organizational process where technical anomalies, unexpected events, or violations of original design requirements—which should be treated as warnings or safety threats—are repeatedly accepted because they do not immediately result in catastrophic failure[2:IN:caib-report-vol1][3:OUT:caib-report-vol1][4:OUT:caib-report-vol1][5:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Over time, as flights return safely despite these flaws, the organization reinterprets the deviations as normal, routine, and acceptable, gradually expanding its "experience base" to tolerate higher levels of risk[5:OUT:caib-report-vol1][6:OUT:caib-report-vol1][7:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
How Normalization of Deviance Showed Up in NASA's Foam Loss Decisions
NASA’s handling of External Tank foam shedding leading up to and during the Columbia (STS-107) mission provides a clear example of this phenomenon:
Violation of Original Design Requirements
The Space Shuttle was originally engineered under strict design specifications stating that the External Tank should shed no debris and that the Orbiter's Thermal Protection System (TPS) should not be subjected to significant debris impacts[5:OUT:caib-report-vol1][8:IN:caib-report-vol1][9:IN:caib-report-vol1]. When foam began breaking off as early as STS-1 and STS-7, it constituted an explicit failure to meet design specifications[5:OUT:caib-report-vol1][8:IN:caib-report-vol1][10:IN:caib-report-vol1].
Reclassifying Safety Hazards as Maintenance Concerns
Because the Shuttle successfully landed after early flights despite receiving tile dings, NASA managers and engineers stopped viewing foam shedding as a safety-of-flight threat[9:IN:caib-report-vol1][11:IN:caib-report-vol1][12:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Instead, they downgraded foam strikes to a routine "turnaround" or maintenance issue—viewing tile damage as an expected operational cost to be repaired between launches rather than a signal of latent danger[6:OUT:caib-report-vol1][9:IN:caib-report-vol1][11:IN:caib-report-vol1][12:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
Defining Anomalies as "In-Family"
NASA routinely designated recurring foam-shedding incidents as "in-family"—a formal classification meaning the problem was within the agency's established experience base[6:OUT:caib-report-vol1][13:IN:caib-report-vol1][14:IN:caib-report-vol1]. This experience base acted like an "elastic waistband," continually expanding to absorb larger and more serious deviations without triggering a fundamental re-evaluation of the hazard[5:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
Downgrading Problem Severity Under Schedule Pressure
Just months before Columbia launched, the STS-112 mission (October 2002) experienced a major bipod foam loss that damaged a Solid Rocket Booster attachment ring[15:IN:caib-report-vol1][16:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Rather than declaring an official In-Flight Anomaly (which would require resolving the issue prior to the next launch), Space Shuttle Program management downgraded the event to an "action item" and authorized two subsequent launches—including STS-107—without fixing the root cause[12:OUT:caib-report-vol1][15:IN:caib-report-vol1].
Decision-Making During Columbia's Final Flight (STS-107)
When a 20- to 27-inch piece of foam struck Columbia's left wing 81.7 seconds after liftoff, mission management's decision-making was anchored in years of normalized deviance[17:OUT:caib-report-vol1][18:OUT:caib-report-vol1][19:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Managers rationalized that foam strikes were "in-family" and "not a safety-of-flight issue"[19:OUT:caib-report-vol1][20:OUT:caib-report-vol1][21:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. This ingrained belief led leadership to dismiss requests for on-orbit satellite imagery to inspect the left wing and to rely on flawed analytical tools (such as the Crater model) to conclude the vehicle was safe[18:OUT:caib-report-vol1][21:OUT:caib-report-vol1][22:OUT:caib-report-vol1][23:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
