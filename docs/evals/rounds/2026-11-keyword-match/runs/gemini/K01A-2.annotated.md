What is the normalization of deviance, and how did it show up in NASA's decisions about foam loss?

Normalization of deviance is a term coined by sociologist Diane Vaughan to describe an organizational phenomenon where technical deviations or unexpected anomalies gradually come to be accepted as normal and acceptable[1:IN:caib-report-vol1]. When a system repeatedly deviates from its original design specifications without resulting in an immediate catastrophic failure, decision-makers begin to reinterpret evidence of unexpected performance as a non-deviant, acceptable baseline rather than a warning signal of danger[2:OUT:caib-report-vol1][3:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
How Normalization of Deviance Showed Up in NASA's Foam Loss Decisions
In the Space Shuttle Program, the normalization of deviance surrounding External Tank foam loss developed over two decades through a series of incremental decisions and reclassifications:
Precedent of Violating Design Requirements: Original Space Shuttle design specifications required that the External Tank must not shed debris and that the Orbiter must not sustain debris impacts exceeding a trivial amount of force[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1][4:IN:caib-report-vol1]. Early foam-shedding events directly violated these design specifications[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. However, after early flights returned safely despite foam strikes, engineering analyses repeatedly concluded that the vehicle could tolerate the damage, establishing a precedent of accepting technical deviations rather than eliminating their root cause[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
Stretching the "Elastic Waistband" of Risk: Because previous missions landed safely after experiencing foam loss, NASA managers treated successful outcomes as valid engineering data proving that continued flights were safe[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1][5:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. The agency's "engineering experience base" acted as an elastic waistband, expanding incrementally to absorb larger and more serious foam strikes[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Worsening anomalies were interpreted not as signs of impending catastrophe, but as proof that the design was operating as predicted[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
Rebranding Hazards as "In-Family" and "Turnaround" Issues: Over the course of 113 missions, foam shedding evolved in the minds of managers from a critical safety threat into a routine maintenance concern[4:IN:caib-report-vol1][6:IN:caib-report-vol1][7:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. NASA formally categorized recurring foam losses as "in-family"—a label signifying that an anomaly fell within historical experience[8:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. This classification oversimplified the hazard, reducing tile damage to an expected post-flight turnaround repair cost rather than a potential threat to crew and vehicle safety[7:OUT:caib-report-vol1][8:OUT:caib-report-vol1][9:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
Downgrading Safety Classifications (The STS-112 Precedent): Historically, major bipod ramp foam-shedding events were designated as In-Flight Anomalies (IFAs), which required formal resolution before launching the next mission[9:OUT:caib-report-vol1][10:IN:caib-report-vol1][11:IN:caib-report-vol1]. However, when a large piece of bipod foam shed during STS-112 in October 2002, Shuttle managers departed from precedent and downgraded the event to an "action item"[9:OUT:caib-report-vol1][11:IN:caib-report-vol1]. This downgrade allowed NASA to fly two subsequent missions—including STS-107 (Columbia)—without resolving the root cause of the foam shedding[11:IN:caib-report-vol1].
Conflating "Accepted Risk" with "Not a Safety-of-Flight Issue": Over time, management confused the operational decision to accept a known risk with the premise that foam shedding posed no safety threat at all[4:IN:caib-report-vol1][12:IN:caib-report-vol1][13:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. This entrenched logic carried directly into Columbia's final mission, leading managers to treat the ascent foam strike as a minor turnaround repair issue after landing and to repeatedly deny requests for on-orbit imagery to inspect the left wing[7:OUT:caib-report-vol1][13:OUT:caib-report-vol1][14:IN:caib-report-vol1][15:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
