# K01B-1

Our pipeline throws the same warning on every deploy. Years ago it scared us; now nobody blinks, since nothing ever broke. How does a team drift into treating a red flag as fine?

Drifting into treating a red flag as fine is a well-documented organizational pattern driven by predictable cognitive biases, habituation, and systemic dynamics:
1. Normalization of Deviance
Sociologist Diane Vaughan coined the term normalization of deviance to describe how organizations incrementally accept technical deviations or unexpected anomalies until they are treated as standard, acceptable baseline behavior[1:IN:caib-report-vol1]. When a pipeline warning occurs repeatedly without causing an outage, the team reclassifies the alert from an "out-of-family" signal of potential danger into a routine, "in-family" operational nuisance[2:OUT:caib-report-vol1][3:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Because the anomaly is encountered constantly during normal operations, it loses its power as an alarm[3:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
2. The "Bald Tire" Effect (Past Success Justification)
Human performance frameworks identify a mindset known as the "Bald Tire" effect—the belief that past success justifies not changing an existing condition or practice ("I've driven 60,000 miles on these tires and haven't had a flat yet")[4:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. When a system repeatedly triggers a warning but nothing breaks, team members fall into a "Pollyanna" bias, assuming everything is inherently normal and ordered[4:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. Over time, lack of immediate negative consequences is misconstrued as proof of safety and system robustness[4:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][5:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
3. Alarm Fatigue and Cognitive Habituation
In software engineering, continuous exposure to non-fatal or false-positive signals creates nuisance alarms[6:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][7:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. Studies on developer behavior show that as the rate of non-actionable or routine warnings increases, developers mentally default to assuming the warning is benign and stop processing the alert altogether[8:OTHER:jones-evidence-based-sweng][9:OTHER:jones-evidence-based-sweng]. Because deliberate mental effort requires significant cognitive resources, human attention naturally habituates and ignores persistent background noise that does not require an immediate, forced response[8:OTHER:jones-evidence-based-sweng][10:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][11:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].
4. Practical Drift & Reversed Burden of Proof
As teams adapt to daily productivity pressures, they undergo practical drift—a gradual, unexamined departure from baseline rules toward routine workarounds[12:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][13:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][14:OTHER:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. A subtle but dangerous shift occurs in the team's culture: the burden of proof is reversed[15:OUT:caib-report-vol1][16:OUT:caib-report-vol1]. Instead of requiring engineers to prove that a deployment is completely clean and safe before releasing, the system implicitly demands that someone prove the warning is an active emergency before anyone invests the effort to fix it[15:OUT:caib-report-vol1][16:OUT:caib-report-vol1].
How High-Reliability Teams Reset the Baseline
High-Reliability Organizations (HROs) avoid this trap by maintaining a preoccupation with failure[17:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. They treat any persistent anomaly or lapse as a symptom of potential systemic weakness rather than proof that the system is resilient[17:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. To restore clear signals, high-reliability teams maintain an "uneasy respect" for human fallibility and actively clean up or fix nuisance alerts so that when a true red flag appears, it demands immediate attention[7:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][18:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][19:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][20:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].
