# K02B-1

Our office relocation looked straightforward on paper, yet each step turned into a slog: a late truck, a missing key, rain, a nervous crew. Is there a name for that drag that turns plain jobs hard?

That drag is formally known as friction[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
Originally articulated by military theorist Carl von Clausewitz and detailed in operational doctrine such as MCDP 1 Warfighting, friction is defined as "the force that makes the apparently easy so difficult"[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting]. Clausewitz observed that "everything in war is simple, but the simplest thing is difficult. The difficulties accumulate and end by producing a kind of friction..."[2:OUT:mcdp1-warfighting].
Friction is the force that resists all action, saps energy, and turns straightforward tasks into grueling efforts[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting]. In an undertaking like an office move, several types of friction interact and accumulate:
External Friction and Chance: Unforeseen situational or environmental obstacles over which you have little control, such as bad weather (rain), late transportation, or a missing key[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting][4:OUT:mcdp1-warfighting].
Mental and Human Friction: The psychological dimension of work, including indecision, anxiety, or stress among team members (such as a nervous crew)[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
Self-Induced Friction: Misalignments that arise during execution due to complex coordination, unclear plans, or subtle lapses in timing[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
While a plan may look simple on paper, real-world execution inevitably generates friction as minor friction points compound, creating the constant drag you experienced[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting][2:OUT:mcdp1-warfighting][3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
