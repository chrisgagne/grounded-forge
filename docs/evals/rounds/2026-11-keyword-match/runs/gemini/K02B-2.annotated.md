Our office relocation looked straightforward on paper, yet each step turned into a slog: a late truck, a missing key, rain, a nervous crew. Is there a name for that drag that turns plain jobs hard?

That phenomenon is known as friction[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
The concept was famously defined by military theorist Carl von Clausewitz, who observed that "everything in war is simple, but the simplest thing is difficult"[2:OUT:mcdp1-warfighting]. He described friction as "the force that makes the apparently easy so difficult"—an unseen drag that resists all action, saps energy, and turns straightforward jobs into an exhausting slog[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
Friction is rarely caused by a single major catastrophe; instead, it is the accumulation of minor, unexpected obstacles[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting][2:OUT:mcdp1-warfighting]. The doctrine in your sources breaks friction down into several categories that map directly onto your relocation experience[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting]:
Physical and External Friction: Uncontrollable environmental or logistical factors, such as bad weather or rain[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
Mental and Psychological Friction: Human factors like stress, hesitation, or a nervous crew[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
Self-Induced Friction: Internal operational snags, such as a late truck, a missing key, or gaps in coordination[3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
While the term originated in military strategy, it is widely used across organizational management and systems theory to explain why plans that look simple on paper routinely run into real-world resistance[1:IN:mcdp1-warfighting][3:IN:mcdp1-warfighting].
