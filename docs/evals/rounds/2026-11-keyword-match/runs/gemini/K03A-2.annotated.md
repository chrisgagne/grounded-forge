# K03A-2

Why should a learning review avoid counterfactual arguments?

In learning reviews and Facilitated Learning Analyses (FLAs), counterfactual arguments—statements asserting that "If person X had done action Y, then outcome Z would not have occurred"[1:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]—are explicitly avoided for several key reasons:
It fails to explain why actions made sense in the moment: Counterfactual arguments focus on what people did not do rather than understanding why they took the actions they actually did[2:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][3:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. The core goal of a learning review is to uncover how the situation, environment, and conditions made their choices seem logical and appropriate at the time[2:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][3:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][4:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol2].
It relies on hindsight bias and presumes an unknowable outcome: Counterfactual thinking uses post-event knowledge that was unavailable to the individuals when making decisions[2:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][5:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Furthermore, counterfactuals assume that investigators can accurately predict an alternate future, whereas it is actually unknowable what would have transpired had a different action been taken[6:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][7:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
It promotes simplistic blame over systemic understanding: Counterfactual reasoning often leads to the flawed conclusion that an event occurred simply because workers failed to follow rules or pay sufficient attention[8:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. In reality, discrete shortcuts or omissions occur regularly in normal, successful work[9:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]; accidents usually result from unexpected combinations of normal performance variability and system conditions rather than individual blunders[10:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
It damages trust and psychological safety: Analyzing events through "should have" or "could have" arguments creates judgment and professional embarrassment[1:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][11:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. When employees feel judged rather than understood, trust between the workforce and leadership breaks down, destroying the willingness of participants to share open, honest accounts necessary for organizational learning[12:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][13:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
