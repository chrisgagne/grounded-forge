# K03B-2

Writing up last month's crane incident, my reviewers keep saying "had Sam checked the brake, we'd be fine." Is that a helpful way to frame the write-up?

Framing your write-up around "had Sam checked the brake, we'd be fine" is not a helpful approach for an incident report[1:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][2:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
In formal learning and human performance frameworks, statements like this are classified as counterfactual arguments ("contrary to the facts")[1:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][2:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Here is why this framing hinders incident investigation and organizational learning:
1. Counterfactuals rely on hindsight bias, not reality
Counterfactual thinking focuses on what people did not do rather than why they took the actions they did[1:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Saying what someone should or could have done presumes you can predict an alternate future, but it explains nothing about the actual conditions present during the event[2:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][3:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][4:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol2]. Hindsight bias predisposes reviewers to judge past actions based on the known outcome, whereas effective incident write-ups seek to understand why Sam's decisions and actions made sense to them in real time and in context[3:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][4:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol2][5:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
2. It creates the illusion that the problem is solved
Attributing an event primarily to an individual's missed step leads to the simplistic—and inaccurate—assumption that safety is restored once the "rule breaker" is retrained or reminded to follow protocol[6:OTHER:caib-report-vol1][7:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][8:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Stopping an investigation at active human error leaves underlying organizational weaknesses untouched, ensuring that the same event can happen again with a different operator[7:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][9:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][10:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1]. Furthermore, blaming individuals damages organizational trust and stops workers from openly sharing critical safety details in future reviews[11:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][12:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
3. Complex systems fail in complex ways
Accidents in high-risk environments are rarely caused by a single isolated mistake; they result from rare combinations of normal performance variability, error precursors (such as poor display layouts, fatigue, competing production pressures, or interruptions), and latent organizational weaknesses[9:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][10:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][13:OTHER:caib-report-vol1][14:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024]. Focusing exclusively on the missing brake check obscures the systemic conditions and organizational vulnerabilities that allowed the check to be missed or failed to mitigate the outcome[9:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][13:OTHER:caib-report-vol1][15:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
How to reframe the write-up instead
Focus on "Work as Accomplished": Document how the task was actually being performed versus "work as imagined" by management, highlighting any operational pressures, unexpected equipment conditions, or procedural ambiguities[15:IN:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024][16:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
Explain how actions made sense at the time: Describe the environmental cues, conflicting priorities, or past practices that led to Sam's mindset leading up to the incident[17:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol2][18:OUT:lfuo-learning-review-guide-2024].
Examine systemic controls: Analyze why existing safeguards or redundant barriers failed to prevent or catch the error before it resulted in an incident[9:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1][10:OTHER:doe-hpi-handbook-vol1].
