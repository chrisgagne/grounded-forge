What does the diathesis-stress model say about how a psychological disorder develops?

The diathesis-stress model posits that a psychological disorder develops from the combined interaction of an underlying vulnerability (a diathesis) and adverse environmental or psychological events (stress)[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e][2:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Key Principles of the Model
Diathesis (Vulnerability): A predisposition toward a disorder[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e]. This vulnerability is not always biological (such as genetic factors or gene variations)[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e][3:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]; it can also be psychological, such as a pessimistic thinking style or a negative cognitive schema[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e][4:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Stress (Triggers): Environmental or psychological stressors that act as precipitating events, such as childhood maltreatment, trauma, severe loss, or a disturbed family environment[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e][5:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][6:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Necessity and Inverse Relationship: The central assumption of the model is that both factors are necessary for a disorder to manifest[6:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][7:IN:openstax-psychology-2e]. The amount of stress required to trigger the disorder is inversely proportional to the level of the diathesis[7:IN:openstax-psychology-2e]: a person with a strong predisposition requires less stress to develop a disorder, whereas someone with low vulnerability would need a significantly higher level of stress to trigger it[7:IN:openstax-psychology-2e].
Examples in Specific Psychological Disorders
Schizophrenia: An adoption study showed that adoptees with a high genetic risk (biological diathesis) were significantly more likely to develop schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder if raised in a disturbed family environment (36.8%) compared to those raised in a healthy environment (5.8%)[6:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][8:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Adoptees with low genetic risk had low rates of schizophrenia regardless of whether they were raised in a disturbed (5.3%) or healthy (4.8%) environment, demonstrating that genes alone do not tell the full story[6:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][8:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Major Depressive Disorder: Research on gene-environment interactions found that individuals carrying one or two short versions of the serotonin-regulating 5-HTTLPR gene (a genetic diathesis) were much more likely to experience major chronic depression when exposed to childhood maltreatment or stressful life events than those carrying long versions of the gene[3:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][9:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Similarly, cognitive theories suggest that early-formed depressive schemas (a psychological diathesis) remain dormant until activated by negative life events[4:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD): The interaction between genetic factors (such as short versions of the 5-HTTLPR gene) and traumatic experiences or childhood adversity elevated the risk of developing PTSD, reflecting a diathesis-stress process[10:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
