# K07B-1

Brothers Tom and Ian went through their parents' awful divorce; Tom sank into deep depression and Ian coped. Why might a rough patch tip one sibling over but not the other?

Even when two siblings experience the exact same event—such as a distressing parental divorce—their psychological outcomes can differ dramatically. Psychological research explains this divergence through an interplay of genetic vulnerability, cognitive interpretation, coping styles, and social buffers.
1. Genetic and Biological Vulnerability (The Diathesis-Stress Model)
The diathesis-stress model posits that psychological disorders originate from a combination of an underlying predisposition (a biological or psychological diathesis) and environmental stress[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e][2:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. While both brothers faced the same environmental trigger (the divorce[3:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]), they may have carried different underlying vulnerabilities:
Genetic Polymorphisms: Variations in specific genes, such as the serotonin-transporter gene (5-HTTLPR), strongly moderate how people respond to stress[4:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][5:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Individuals carrying short alleles of this gene are significantly more likely to develop major depression when exposed to severe life stress, whereas those carrying long alleles demonstrate resilience under identical adverse conditions[4:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][5:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Temperament and Neuroticism: Inherent biological dimensions of temperament—such as reactivity (the intensity of one's reaction to challenging stimuli) and self-regulation—differ between individuals[6:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Individuals high in neuroticism (a personality trait characterized by emotional instability, moodiness, and high anxiety) have an overactive sympathetic nervous system that triggers fight-or-flight reactions even under moderate stress, elevating their risk for depression[7:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][8:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][9:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
2. Cognitive Appraisals and Attributional Styles
Stress is not solely defined by the event itself, but by how an individual appraises and assigns meaning to the event[10:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][11:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]:
Primary and Secondary Appraisals: One sibling may evaluate the divorce as an overwhelming threat with no effective way to cope (negative primary and secondary appraisals)[10:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][12:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Conversely, the coping sibling may possess a higher level of perceived control—the belief in their personal capacity to influence and shape life outcomes—which buffers emotional and physiological reactivity to daily stressors[13:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][14:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Explanatory Style and Hopelessness: According to hopelessness theory, individuals who attribute negative life events to internal ("it's my fault"), stable ("things will never get better"), and global ("this ruins every part of my life") causes are particularly vulnerable to developing depression[15:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][16:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][17:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Depressive Schemas and Rumination: Pre-existing depressive schemas—mental frameworks centered on themes of loss, rejection, or inadequacy—can lie dormant until activated by a major life stressor[18:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Once triggered, a habit of rumination (repetitively and passively dwelling on depressed symptoms and feelings rather than taking problem-solving action) prolongs and intensifies depressive episodes[19:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][20:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
3. Hardiness, Coping Strategies, and Social Support
Differences in internal resilience factors and external support networks also shape individual responses:
Personality Hardiness: Individuals exhibiting hardiness possess a clear sense of personal commitment, an internal locus of control, and a tendency to view potential stressors as challenges rather than threats, allowing them to remain resilient under prolonged pressure[21:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][22:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior].
Coping Mechanisms: Actively using problem-focused coping or positive cognitive reappraisal helps individuals adaptively navigate stressful life transitions, whereas passivity or learned helplessness leads to psychological exhaustion[23:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][24:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][25:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Social Buffers: The availability and active use of external social support (emotional comfort, encouragement, and guidance from peers or family) serves as a critical buffer, mitigating the damaging psychological impact of severe family disruption[26:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][27:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][28:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][29:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
