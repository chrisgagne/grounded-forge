Brothers Tom and Ian went through their parents' awful divorce; Tom sank into deep depression and Ian coped. Why might a rough patch tip one sibling over but not the other?

When two siblings experience the exact same life stressor—such as a difficult parental divorce—their psychological outcomes can differ dramatically due to an interplay of biological vulnerabilities, cognitive interpretations, personality traits, and coping mechanisms.
1. Biological and Genetic Vulnerability (The Diathesis-Stress Model)
Psychological disorders like major depressive disorder are best understood through the diathesis-stress model, which posits that a disorder develops when an underlying predisposition or vulnerability (diathesis) interacts with environmental stress[1:IN:openstax-psychology-2e][2:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Genetic Differences: Genetic variations significantly influence how individuals react to life stress[3:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. For instance, individuals carrying short versions of the serotonin transporter gene (5-HTTLPR) are at a markedly higher risk of developing depression when exposed to stressful life events or family turmoil compared to those with long versions of the gene[3:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][4:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Innate Temperament: Biological differences in early temperament—specifically reactivity (how strongly one responds to environmental stimuli) and self-regulation (the ability to control that emotional response)—shape individual stress tolerance from an early age[5:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][6:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
2. Cognitive Appraisals and Perceived Control
People process and assign meaning to the exact same event in very different ways[7:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][8:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Primary Appraisal: One sibling (Tom) may appraise the divorce as a catastrophic threat or unrecoverable loss, whereas the other (Ian) may appraise the situation as a manageable challenge[9:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][10:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Secondary Appraisal & Perceived Control: Stress intensity is heavily governed by perceived control—an individual's belief in their personal capacity to influence outcomes[11:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][12:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Feeling powerless to change or navigate a situation elevates stress and depressive vulnerability, whereas perceiving effective coping options buffers against emotional collapse[10:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][11:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][13:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
3. Cognitive Vulnerabilities, Attributions, and Rumination
Attributional Styles: According to hopelessness theory, individuals prone to depression tend to make internal, stable, and global attributions for negative life events (e.g., believing the divorce is somehow their fault, will permanently ruin their life, and affects everything they do)[14:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][15:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][16:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e]. Protective cognitive styles, by contrast, attribute negative events to temporary (unstable), specific, or external factors[15:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][17:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Depressive Schemas: Major "exit events" such as divorce can trigger dormant depressive schemas—preexisting cognitive framework themes of rejection, loss, and inadequacy developed earlier in life[18:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][19:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
Rumination: Passively and repetitively dwelling on negative feelings and symptoms (rumination) prolongs and intensifies depressive episodes, whereas actively problem-solving or distracting oneself facilitates recovery[20:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
4. Personality Buffers and Social Support
Hardiness: Individuals who possess hardiness—a cluster of personality characteristics involving a strong sense of commitment, an internal locus of control, and a view of life changes as positive challenges—are far better equipped to transform negative stressors into growth experiences[21:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][22:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][23:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior].
Coping & Social Support: Utilizing problem-focused coping (taking active steps to address challenges) or positive cognitive reappraisal, alongside seeking external social support (friends, relatives, or mentors), provides vital psychological buffers that mitigate the impact of severe familial stress[24:OTHER:openstax-organizational-behavior][25:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][26:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e][27:OUT:openstax-psychology-2e].
