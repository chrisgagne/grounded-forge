# K10B-2

Our board can't settle on a strategy, and nobody knows how the market will react. Is there a tool that plots issues by how sure we are of the facts and how much we see eye to eye?

Yes, the tool you are looking for is the Stacey Matrix (also known as the Agreement/Uncertainty Matrix)[1:OTHER:open-practice-library][2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook]. Developed by Ralph Stacey, this visual decision-making framework helps leadership teams and boards categorize issues by plotting them along two specific dimensions: certainty of facts/outcomes and level of stakeholder agreement[1:OTHER:open-practice-library][2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook].
How the Matrix Works
The matrix uses two axes to visually map out strategic decisions or market opportunities[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][3:OTHER:open-practice-library]:
Horizontal Axis (Degree of Certainty): Ranges from Close to Certainty (high confidence in data, cause-and-effect, and market outcomes) to Far from Certainty (high uncertainty, unknown variables)[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
Vertical Axis (Degree of Agreement): Ranges from Close to Agreement (high consensus among board members on goals and direction) to Far from Agreement (significant differences of opinion or conflicting priorities)[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
The Four Decision Domains
By plotting issues on the grid, the board can identify which strategic approach fits the situation[1:OTHER:open-practice-library][3:OTHER:open-practice-library]:
Simple (High Certainty, High Agreement): The path is clear and outcomes are predictable[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][3:OTHER:open-practice-library]. Standard procedures or command-and-control approaches work best here[3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
Complicated (High Certainty / Low Agreement OR Low Certainty / High Agreement):
High Certainty, Low Agreement: The facts are clear, but opinions differ—requiring consensus-building, negotiation, or formal decision mechanisms[3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
Low Certainty, High Agreement: Everyone agrees on the goal, but the market path is uncertain—requiring expert analysis, adaptive planning, and flexibility[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
Complex (Low Certainty, Low Agreement): The future is unpredictable and the board lacks alignment[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][4:IN:liberating-structures-handbook]. Traditional long-term strategic plans fail in this domain because situations are emergent[4:IN:liberating-structures-handbook]. Instead, the board should adopt an experimentation and learning approach (e.g., running small market probes to gather real-world data before committing major capital)[3:OTHER:open-practice-library][4:IN:liberating-structures-handbook].
Anarchy / Chaos (Far from Certainty, Far from Agreement): High political friction and zero predictability[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook]. These issues must be stabilized or broken down into smaller pieces before strategy can be set[3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
Why It Helps Resolve Board Deadlocks
Diagnoses the Conflict: It clarifies whether the board is stuck due to a lack of information (a certainty issue) or a clash of values/priorities (an agreement issue)[2:IN:liberating-structures-handbook][3:OTHER:open-practice-library].
Matches Strategy to Reality: It prevents the board from forcing rigid 5-year plans or demanding perfect consensus on "Complex" initiatives where market feedback is needed first[4:IN:liberating-structures-handbook].
