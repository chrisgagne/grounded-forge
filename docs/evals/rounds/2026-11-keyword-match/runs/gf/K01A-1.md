## What it is

The Columbia Accident Investigation Board (CAIB, 2003) is the corpus source on this. It takes the term from the sociologist Diane Vaughan's study of the Challenger launch decision. In its own words, normalisation of deviance is "the acceptance of events that are not supposed to happen" (Ch 6). Evidence that a design isn't working as intended gets reinterpreted, one step at a time, as acceptable and normal.

Chapter 8 sets out how this happened, using both the O-ring erosion before Challenger and the foam before Columbia:

1. **The first deviation breaks the specification.** The original design predicted neither O-ring erosion nor foam debris. When each first happened, engineers decided the design could tolerate the damage, put in a temporary fix or accepted the risk, and flew.
2. **That first decision sets a precedent.** "For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations."
3. **Later incidents look like confirmation, not warnings.** They were read "as evidence that the design was now acting as predicted." The experience base "functioned as an elastic waistband, expanding to hold larger deviations from the original design."
4. **Surviving counts as proof of safety.** "Anomalies that did not lead to catastrophic failure were treated as a source of valid engineering data that justified further flights." Meanwhile, "Engineers understood what was happening, but they never understood why."

## How it showed up in the foam decisions

- **Breaking the rules became routine.** The Shuttle's own requirements banned debris shedding that could damage the Orbiter. Even so, foam came off on 65 of the 79 missions that had imagery. The Board's finding F6.1-1 says that "NASA has not followed its own rules and requirements on foam-shedding." Its overall judgement: "With each successful landing, it appears that NASA engineers and managers increasingly regarded the foam-shedding as inevitable, and as either unlikely to jeopardize safety or simply an acceptable risk."
- **Labels were softened over time.** Each bipod foam event had been given In-Flight Anomaly status, which was removed as fixes went in. Later foam events were filed as "in-family" (within past experience), which the Board calls "a strange term indeed for a violation of system requirements." "Accepted risk" and "not a safety-of-flight issue" became blurred. Because tile damage had been budgeted as maintenance from the start, foam damage fit into that category. By the end, "Over the course of 113 missions, foam-shedding and other debris impacts came to be regarded more as a turnaround or maintenance issue, and less as a hazard to the vehicle and crew."
- **STS-112 was the missed warning.** In October 2002, a large piece of bipod foam hit the booster's attach ring. The photo working group recommended an In-Flight Anomaly. Management instead downgraded it to an "action" item, and its due date slipped until after STS-107. "The Space Shuttle Program decided to fly not one but two missions before resolving the STS-112 foam loss." The Board rejects the flight rationale given at the STS-113 Flight Readiness Review: "With no engineering analysis, Shuttle managers used past success as a justification for future flights." Put simply, "After analysis, the biggest foam hit to date was redefined as a weak signal."
- **The result was Columbia.** That rationale gave managers a way to classify the STS-107 foam strike as a maintenance and turnaround concern. The STS-107 Flight Readiness Review never discussed the STS-112 loss at all. The Board also found that NASA did not properly track trends in foam losses (F6.1-10), so each event was judged on its own.

## Why it's hard to see from inside

The Board rejects the idea that NASA knowingly gambled, comparing it to Feynman's "Russian roulette" description of Challenger: "that characterization is only possible in hindsight." There were fixes under way, but neither problem was ever treated as a reason to stop flying, and "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct." Schedule pressure made this worse, especially the February 2004 deadline for the Space Station's Node 2. So did a Flight Readiness process that, in the Board's words, was designed to turn known problems into acceptable flight risks, "and that is precisely what it did."

The Board's proposed remedy is to make warning signals clearer and louder. NASA should "design systems that maximize the clarity of signals, amplify weak signals so they can be tracked, and account for missing signals." It also calls for independent safety representation so managers are not "lulled into complacency by shifting definitions of risk."
