## Normalization of deviance at NASA: how foam loss became routine

**What it is.** The term comes from the sociologist Diane Vaughan's study of the Challenger launch decision. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board (CAIB) borrowed it in its 2003 report to explain how NASA came to live with foam shedding. A normalized deviance is a departure from the design that people see again and again. Each time it fails to cause a disaster, it gets reclassified as acceptable, until no one treats it as a warning. In the Board's words, "engineers and managers conducting risk assessments continually normalized the technical deviations they found," and evidence that the design wasn't working "was reinterpreted as acceptable and non-deviant, which diminished perceptions of risk throughout the agency." Vaughan's own book isn't in the corpus. Everything here is the CAIB's use of her idea, and Vaughan also worked on the Board's staff.

**How it works, according to the Board (Ch 8):**
1. **The first occurrence breaks the rules.** The Shuttle's design didn't allow foam debris (or O-ring erosion) at all, so the first case violated the specification.
2. **The first decision sets a precedent.** The first engineering analysis decided the vehicle could tolerate the damage, and NASA flew. "For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations."
3. **Later cases are read as confirmation.** After that, new strikes counted as evidence the design was "now acting as predicted." The experience base "functioned as an elastic waistband, expanding to hold larger deviations from the original design." Flights that didn't end in catastrophe "were treated as a source of valid engineering data that justified further flights."
4. **The problem is never understood.** "Engineers understood what was happening, but they never understood why."

**How it played out with foam:**
- **Over many flights.** "With each successful landing, it appears that NASA engineers and managers increasingly regarded the foam-shedding as inevitable, and as either unlikely to jeopardize safety or simply an acceptable risk" (Ch 6). The design required that the Orbiter take essentially no debris hits. In practice, foam shed on 65 of the 79 missions with imagery, and the left bipod ramp lost foam on 7 of 113 flights.
- **Softer labels.** Tile damage had been budgeted as routine maintenance from the start, so foam damage slipped into a category that already existed. Foam events were called "in-family," meaning within past experience, which the Board calls "a strange term indeed for a violation of system requirements." Over time, managers also blurred "accepted risk" with "not a safety-of-flight issue."
- **STS-112, the warning that was explained away.** In October 2002, a large piece of bipod foam struck the booster attach ring. Each earlier bipod event had been formally logged as an In-Flight Anomaly. This one was downgraded to an "action" item, and "The Space Shuttle Program decided to fly not one but two missions before resolving the STS-112 foam loss." The Board calls the reasoning given for STS-113 "seriously flawed": "With no engineering analysis, Shuttle managers used past success as a justification for future flights." Accepting that reasoning "cleared the way for Columbia's launch and provided a method for Mission managers to classify the STS-107 foam strike as a maintenance and turnaround concern rather than a safety-of-flight issue." In Chapter 8's summary: "After analysis, the biggest foam hit to date was redefined as a weak signal."
- **During Columbia's last flight (STS-107).** Managers first compared the strike to STS-112 to put it inside past experience, without checking that the impact sites were different. Because the STS-113 reasoning had already been accepted, there was a strong incentive to reach the same judgement again.
- **The Board's overall verdict.** "Over the course of 113 missions, foam-shedding and other debris impacts came to be regarded more as a turnaround or maintenance issue, and less as a hazard to the vehicle and crew."

**Why it's hard to see from the inside.** The Board refuses to explain this with hindsight. It rejects Richard Feynman's comparison to Russian roulette because that "is only possible in hindsight." Several things hid the danger:
- The Shuttle was experimental, so anomalies were expected and didn't stand out as warnings.
- Incidents were looked at one at a time.
- Each scare was followed by flights that went fine.

"Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct." Schedule pressure also played a part. The Board describes NASA taking on more risk "little by little" to stay on schedule.
