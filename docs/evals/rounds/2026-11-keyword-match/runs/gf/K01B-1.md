## How a red flag turns into background noise

The corpus covers this directly. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board report calls it the **normalisation of deviance**, a term it takes from Diane Vaughan. Both sources here come from safety-critical work: NASA, and the U.S. Department of Energy's *Human Performance Improvement Handbook*. Applying them to a CI/CD warning is my own reading, but the fit is close.

### 1. The first decision sets the precedent
The first time something violates expectations, someone looks at it, decides the system can tolerate it, and carries on. The Columbia Board says of both the Challenger O-rings and the Columbia foam: "that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations." Each later occurrence was then read "not ... as signals of danger, but as evidence that the design was now acting as predicted."

In your case, whoever first decided "it's fine, ship it" set the baseline. Every deploy since then has been compared to that decision, not to what the warning actually means.

### 2. "Nothing broke" gets counted as evidence
This is the core mechanism, and it matches your phrase almost exactly. The Board writes: "Anomalies that did not lead to catastrophic failure were treated as a source of valid engineering data that justified further flights." The shared sense of what's acceptable "functioned as an elastic waistband, expanding to hold larger deviations from the original design." With each successful landing, engineers and managers "increasingly regarded the foam-shedding as inevitable."

The DOE handbook calls the matching individual attitude **"bald tire"**: "A belief that past performance is justification for not changing (improving) existing practices or conditions." It adds: "A history of success can promote complacency and overconfidence." And from repetition: "Persistent use of at-risk behaviors builds overconfidence and trust in personal skills and ability."

### 3. The label changes, and with it the attention
Foam strikes ended up labelled "in-family", which meant within normal experience. The Board's comment: "'In-family' was a strange term indeed for a violation of system requirements." Once something carries the normal label, it gets less attention by definition. Over 22 years, foam strikes went from a hazard to a maintenance item.

### 4. Repetition wears the signal down
"The same conclusions, repeated over time, can result in problems eventually being deemed non-problems." Each incident was also judged on its own: "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct." Nobody looked at the trend. The Board says outright that NASA failed to do trend analysis on foam losses. It also found that "no process addresses the need to update a hazard analysis when anomalies occur."

### 5. Delivery pressure is loud and safety is quiet
When the organisation rewards speed, "efficiency becomes a strong signal and safety a weak one." The result: "Little by little, NASA was accepting more and more risk in order to stay on schedule." The DOE handbook explains why prevention loses that contest: deploys are visible and celebrated, while the payoff of prevention is "the quiet non-events."

### 6. The burden of proof flips
By the end, engineers had to prove the situation was unsafe. The Board's standard: "operations must be proved safe, rather than the other way around. NASA inverted this burden of proof." If someone on your team asks "what's this warning about?" and the answer is "it's always there", the burden has already flipped.

## Where the two sources differ
- **The Columbia Board** puts the cause mainly in the organisation: schedule pressure, information getting simplified as it moves up the hierarchy, and a safety function that depended on the programme it was meant to check. It warns that replacing people won't fix the problem.
- **The DOE handbook** spends more time on individual risk perception: people underestimate risk, especially risks that feel routine. Its fix is leadership, because "leadership is a defense."

Both agree that the drift is gradual and that each step looks reasonable at the time.

## What the sources suggest for your warning
- **Bring it back as a real question.** Find out what the warning actually means and record either a fix or an explicit, reasoned decision to accept the risk, with an owner. Don't let it stay "in-family" by default.
- **Treat it as a nuisance alarm.** The DOE handbook lists nuisance alarms among the common flaws in engineered controls. A warning everyone ignores trains people to ignore warnings, so fix it, or suppress it on purpose and document why.
- **Look at the trend, not single deploys.** Is it the same warning with the same cause, or has the content quietly changed?
- **Bring in fresh eyes.** DOE notes that latent conditions "will not self-reveal, they must be discovered," and that outsiders can see degraded conditions insiders have stopped noticing.

One limit: the corpus has no software-specific treatment of alert fatigue or warning hygiene. Everything above comes from aerospace and nuclear safety.
