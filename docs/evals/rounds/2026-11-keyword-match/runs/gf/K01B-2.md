Teams usually get there one reasonable decision at a time, and each problem-free deploy feels like proof the last decision was right. Two sources in this corpus describe this pattern in detail. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board report (CAIB) calls it **normalisation of deviance**, a term it borrows from Diane Vaughan. The US Department of Energy's *Human Performance Improvement Handbook* (DOE) covers it as the "bald tire" attitude and as "drift toward failure."

## How the drift happens

**1. The first "ship it anyway" sets the rule.** Foam coming off the Shuttle's fuel tank broke the design requirements from the very first flight. After the first incident, engineers decided the vehicle could tolerate it, and the Board says "that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations." From then on, each new occurrence looked like the system behaving as expected, not like a warning.

**2. Every quiet outcome counts as evidence.** "With each successful landing," the Board writes, engineers and managers "increasingly regarded the foam-shedding as inevitable." Problems that didn't cause a disaster "were treated as a source of valid engineering data that justified further flights." What they had come to accept "functioned as an elastic waistband, expanding to hold larger deviations from the original design." That matches "nothing ever broke": a run of clean deploys gets read as a safety margin when nobody ever measured one.

**3. A signal that never stops stops meaning anything.** Because foam strikes happened so often, they "became routine signals — a normal part of Shuttle operations, not signals of danger." NASA even had a soothing official label for them, "in-family," and the Board calls it "a strange term indeed for a violation of system requirements." A warning on every deploy is your version of "in-family."

**4. Nobody is ever corrected for ignoring it.** DOE describes shortcuts that rarely lead to an event, and that are "consistently reinforced with convenience, comfort, time savings." Over time, "people will begin to underestimate the risk of hazards and the possibility of error." The handbook's "bald tire" attitude ("I've got 60,000 miles on this set of tires and haven't had a flat yet") holds that past success means nothing needs to change. Its example is Columbia: the foam strikes came to be seen as just a "maintenance" issue.

**5. Prevention can't be seen, but speed can.** DOE says the results of production are obvious, while "the outcomes of prevention activities are the quiet non-events." Stopping to look into a warning delays the deploy you can see, to prevent a failure you can't.

**6. Nobody can point to the bad decision.** The Board says "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed correct." It also notes NASA "understood what was happening, but they never understood why." That's the risky part of your situation: the team knows the warning shows up, but probably can't explain what it's warning about.

## What the sources say pushes back against it

- **Flip who has to prove what.** At NASA, the engineers had to "prove that the situation was unsafe." The Board's alternative is for someone to ask, "Prove to me that Columbia has not been harmed." For you, that means asking someone to show why the warning is harmless, instead of waiting for evidence that it isn't.
- **Don't treat past success as safety.** DOE, drawing on research into organisations that run hazardous operations with very few failures, says "HROs do not take past success as a reason for confidence." It also warns that "the overwhelming human tendency is to respond to weak signals with a weak response."
- **Make signals easy to see.** The Board's recommendation is to "maximize the clarity of signals, amplify weak signals so they can be tracked, and account for missing signals."

A practical step that follows from this, though it's my inference and not something either source says about pipelines: make an explicit decision about the warning. Either fix whatever causes it, or document why it's safe and suppress it on purpose, with an owner and a date to review it. Then a new warning shows up clearly instead of blending into one everybody already tunes out.

The corpus doesn't cover CI/CD warnings or alert fatigue specifically. The mechanism comes from aerospace and nuclear-safety sources, and I've applied it to your pipeline by analogy.
