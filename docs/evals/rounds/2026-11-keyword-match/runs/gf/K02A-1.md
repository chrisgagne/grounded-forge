Friction is everything that gets in the way of carrying out a plan in war. The corpus covers it in one source, the U.S. Marine Corps doctrine *MCDP 1: Warfighting* (1997), which takes the idea from Clausewitz.

**What it is.** MCDP 1 says war looks simple when you picture it as two opposing wills clashing. In practice it becomes very hard "because of the countless factors that impinge on it," and those factors together are called friction. It quotes Clausewitz's definition, "the force that makes the apparently easy so difficult," and adds its own: "Friction is the force that resists all action and saps energy. It makes the simple difficult and the difficult seemingly impossible."

**Why it makes the simple difficult.** The manual gives three reasons:

1. **Friction is built into war.** "The very essence of war as a clash between opposed wills creates friction." The enemy is resisting you, so nothing happens in a frictionless space. The manual treats friction as a basic feature of war, alongside uncertainty, fluidity, disorder and complexity. You can work within it but you can never get rid of it.
2. **It comes from many directions at once.** It can be:
   - **Mental:** indecision over what to do.
   - **Physical:** enemy fire, or terrain that has to be crossed.
   - **External:** caused by the enemy, terrain, weather or chance.
   - **Self-inflicted:** "lack of a clearly defined goal, lack of coordination, unclear or complicated plans, complex task organizations or command relationships, or complicated technologies."

   And "because war is a human enterprise, friction will always have a psychological as well as a physical impact."
3. **The difficulties add up.** The chapter opens with a Clausewitz epigraph: everything in war is simple, but the simplest thing is difficult, and the difficulties pile up until they produce "a kind of friction that is inconceivable unless one has experienced war." Each small obstacle is manageable alone. Together they wear down the effort. The manual also says friction "is the constant enemy of surprise."

**What to do about it.** The doctrine doesn't claim friction can be engineered away:
- Reduce the friction you cause yourselves, but "the greater requirement is to fight effectively despite the existence of friction."
- The main remedy is will: "we prevail over friction through persistent strength of mind and spirit."
- Raise the enemy's friction. Speed and tempo keep the enemy "off balance, thereby increasing his friction."
- Learn it through experience. Training should try to recreate the conditions of war, but "it can never fully duplicate the level of friction of real combat."

Later, in the chapter on how to fight, the manual says that a commander who tries to gain certainty, keep tight control of events at all times, or make events fit the plan is denying the nature of war. The goal is to cope with, and even thrive in, "chaos, uncertainty, constant change, and friction," and to use those conditions as a weapon against an enemy who copes less well.

The corpus has no other source on this, so the answer is how MCDP 1 presents Clausewitz. *On War* itself isn't in the corpus and would be the obvious source to add.
