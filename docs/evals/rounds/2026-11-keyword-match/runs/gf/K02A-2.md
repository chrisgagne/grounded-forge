In the corpus, friction comes from **MCDP 1: *Warfighting*** (U.S. Marine Corps, 1997). It covers the idea in Chapter 1, "The Nature of War," and builds on Clausewitz. No other source in the corpus treats friction in the military sense.

## What friction is

The chapter opens with Clausewitz's line: "Everything in war is simple, but the simplest thing is difficult. The difficulties accumulate and end by producing a kind of friction that is inconceivable unless one has experienced war." The converted text has OCR errors here ("dffi-cult", "dq'/Iculties"). I've written out the intended words.

MCDP-1 starts from a paradox. Seen as a clash between two opposing wills, war looks simple. In practice it becomes "extremely difficult because of the countless factors that impinge on it." Those factors together are called friction, which Clausewitz described as "the force that makes the apparently easy so difficult." The manual then gives its own definition: "Friction is the force that resists all action and saps energy. It makes the simple difficult and the difficult seemingly impossible."

## Why it makes the simple difficult

1. **It comes from what war is.** "The very essence of war as a clash between opposed wills creates friction." The enemy is not something inert you act on. It is an independent will that resists and adapts, so friction is built into war and can't be engineered out.
2. **It comes from everywhere at once.** MCDP-1 names four forms:
   - **Mental:** for example, indecision over a course of action.
   - **Physical:** effective enemy fire, or a terrain obstacle.
   - **External:** imposed by "enemy action, the terrain, weather, or mere chance."
   - **Self-induced:** "lack of a clearly defined goal, lack of coordination, unclear or complicated plans, complex task organizations or command relationships, or complicated technologies."
3. **It piles up.** In Clausewitz's words the difficulties "accumulate." Each one may be small, but together they drag on every action.
4. **It hits the mind as well as the body.** "Because war is a human enterprise, friction will always have a psychological as well as a physical impact."

## What to do about it

- **Cut what you cause yourself, but expect to fight through the rest.** "While we should attempt to minimize self-induced friction, the greater requirement is to fight effectively despite the existence of friction."
- **Rely on will.** "We prevail over friction through persistent strength of mind and spirit."
- **Turn it against the enemy.** Commanders should try "to raise our enemy's friction to a level that weakens his ability to fight."
- **Learn it through experience.** "Only through experience can we come to appreciate the force of will necessary to overcome friction." Training should approximate war, but "it can never fully duplicate the level of friction of real combat."

The manual then deals with **uncertainty** separately, though it notes that uncertainty could be seen as "just one of many sources of friction."

The corpus has only MCDP-1's account. It doesn't hold Clausewitz's *On War* itself, so the Clausewitz lines above are quoted as MCDP-1 presents them.
