The corpus covers this in one source, the *Learning from Unwanted Outcomes* (LFUO) learning review guide (2024). It gives four connected reasons.

**1. Counterfactuals argue about what didn't happen, not what did.** In its Part 9 "Avoid Counterfactual Arguments" section, the guide says teams "must guard against making counterfactual arguments such as: 'If this person had done X, then the outcome would have been Y and the accident would not have occurred.'" Its reasoning is that the review "is only useful when it learns why people did what they actually did (why it made sense to them at the time), rather than why they did not do something that—in hindsight—others might think they should have." A counterfactual swaps an explanation of real behaviour for a judgement about imagined behaviour.

**2. They assume foresight nobody had.** Handout C ("FLA Watch Outs") defines the term: "Counterfactual means 'contrary to the facts.' Engaging in counterfactual thinking presumes that you can predict the future." The guide reminds teams that "if the person involved in the accident had known what the results would be, they probably would have taken a different action. But the future hadn't happened yet, and they couldn't have known the outcome." It also makes an epistemic point: the person "simply did not take the counterfactual action at that time and it is unknowable what would have happened if they had." So a counterfactual claim can't be checked.

**3. They hide the context the review exists to uncover.** The guide warns that what "team members may think should have happened are the most seductive counterfactuals and they will blind the team from understanding the event in context." Its examples are the familiar phrases "If only the firefighter had . . .", "The crew leader failed to . . .", "The supervisor should have . . .". Phrasing like this points at a person's choice and stops the inquiry before it reaches the conditions, goal conflicts and work-as-imagined versus work-as-done gaps that the next step ("Display Misalignments") is meant to bring out.

**4. They're part of a set of biases.** Handout C lists counterfactual thinking alongside hindsight bias, outcome bias, confirmation bias and the fundamental attribution error. The guide notes that hindsight affects participants too. Someone who first talks about what they wish they'd done differently will, over the following weeks, start describing "the certainty of what other people should have done." Its remedies are:
- tell everyone on the team they're allowed to call out bias when they see it;
- interview people as soon as possible;
- keep each person telling the story "solely from their perspective."

In short, a learning review is meant to understand why decisions made sense to the people involved when they made them. Counterfactuals replace that with an unprovable story built from knowing the outcome, and they push the review back toward blame.

Other corpus sources, such as the DOE Human Performance Improvement handbook, cover hindsight bias in general, but only the LFUO guide treats counterfactual arguments as a named concept.
