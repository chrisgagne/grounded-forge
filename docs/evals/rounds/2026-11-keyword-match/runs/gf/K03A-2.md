A learning review exists to understand why people did what they did. A counterfactual argument swaps that question for another one: what *should* they have done? The corpus covers this mainly through the US Forest Service's *Learning from Unintended Outcomes* guide (2024), which covers the Facilitated Learning Analysis (FLA) and Learning Review processes. The DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook*, Vol. 1, backs it up independently.

**1. It answers the wrong question.** The guide says plainly that "FLA teams must guard against making counterfactual arguments such as: 'If this person had done X, then the outcome would have been Y and the accident would not have occurred.'" Its reason is that "the FLA is only useful when it learns why people did what they actually did (why it made sense to them at the time), rather than why they did not do something that—in hindsight—others might think they should have." The DOE handbook reaches the same conclusion from another tradition: "explaining what people could have or should have done explains nothing about why they did what they did."

**2. It assumes knowledge nobody had at the time.** "Counterfactual means 'contrary to the facts.' Engaging in counterfactual thinking presumes that you can predict the future." The guide adds that if the person had known the result, "they probably would have taken a different action. But the future hadn't happened yet, and they couldn't have known the outcome." It also makes a point about evidence: the person being interviewed "simply did not take the counterfactual action at that time and it is unknowable what would have happened if they had." A counterfactual is a guess presented as a finding.

**3. It hides the context, and the context is where the learning is.** The guide calls a team's own ideas about what should have happened "the most seductive counterfactuals," which "will blind the team from understanding the event in context." It quotes the Fire Operations Risk Management Council: "Hindsight bias is the chief saboteur of any accident investigation." The review's real job is to describe how people understood the situation and why their choices made sense to them then. That matters because, as the guide puts it, "in complex situations, people must act based on uncertain, incomplete, and sometimes even contradictory information."

**4. It leads to blame instead of fixes.** Counterfactuals tend to be about a person ("If only the firefighter had…", "The supervisor should have…"). The guide lists them with the fundamental attribution error and outcome bias: blaming the individual instead of the conditions, and judging a decision by how it turned out. The DOE handbook explains the cost. Hindsight pushes analysts to look for an individual's shortcomings, and when analysis "is fixated on individual culpability," it struggles to find effective corrective actions. Its advice is to focus "on what could have prevented the event rather than simply concentrating on who caused an event."

**5. The people involved do it too.** The guide notes that sensemaking carries on after an accident. Interviewees often start out talking about what they wish they had done differently, and over the next weeks their language "changes to the certainty of what other people should have done." Its remedy is to interview early and keep people telling the story from their own point of view. It also tells review teams to explicitly let each other call out biases they spot.

**What to do instead:** Rebuild the local rationality. For each key decision, work out what the person was trying to achieve, what they were paying attention to, and what they knew. Then explain "why, in context, the actions and decisions made sense." The guide also suggests asking how the same decisions could have led to the outcome people expected. That shows the decision wasn't the obvious mistake hindsight makes it look like.
