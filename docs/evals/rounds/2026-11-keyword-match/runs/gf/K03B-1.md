No. The corpus's incident-review sources all treat "had Sam checked the brake, we'd be fine" as a framing to avoid. It points the write-up at a person and away from what you could change.

**1. It's a counterfactual, and the method guides warn against exactly this sentence.** The US Forest Service's *Learning Review* guide says review teams "must guard against making counterfactual arguments such as: 'If this person had done X, then the outcome would have been Y and the accident would not have occurred.'" It says a review is only useful when it learns "why people did what they actually did (why it made sense to them at the time)." It adds that "engaging in counterfactual thinking presumes that you can predict the future." Its examples of tempting phrasings are "If only the firefighter had...", "The crew leader failed to..." and "The supervisor should have...". Your reviewers' line is the same pattern.

**2. It explains nothing about why the check was missed.** The DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook* says that "explaining what people could have or should have done explains nothing about why they did what they did." It also warns that "Hindsight predisposes the analyst to search for data that confirms the apparent shortcomings of the individual(s)." It says "inattention to detail" and "not following procedures" are not root causes, because human error can't be eliminated completely.

**3. Stopping at Sam means it happens again.** The same handbook says "Events will continue as long as event investigations stop prematurely at the active human error." The real causes, usually weaknesses in the organisation, stay hidden. It recommends asking what could have prevented the event, not who caused it.

**4. A single missed check implies a single barrier.** The DOE handbook's defence-in-depth idea is that controls are layered, so "if one fails, remaining controls will function as needed." If one person skipping one brake check was enough to cause the incident, that's a finding about the system's design. It's not only about Sam.

**5. Even on accountability, singling someone out needs tests first.** The NHS *Just Culture Guide* says "Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate - most patient safety issues have deeper causes and require wider action." Before it points at a person, it asks two things:
- Were there agreed procedures that were workable, in routine use, and knowingly departed from?
- Would comparable colleagues have done the same thing in the same situation?

If the answers are no and yes, focusing on the individual is "unlikely to be appropriate."

**What to write instead.** For Sam and anyone else involved, the DOE handbook suggests reconstructing:
- what they were trying to accomplish
- what they were paying attention to
- what they knew at key points

It also says to record which controls worked to keep the outcome from being worse. That leads to questions like:
- Why did skipping the brake check make sense at that moment? Think about time pressure, how the checklist is designed, and whether the step is routinely skipped.
- Why was that check the only thing between normal operation and the incident?

When reviewers raise the line, you could answer: "Agreed, the check didn't happen. The write-up is about why, and why nothing else caught it."
