**No, it isn't a helpful framing.** All three of the sources I checked warn against exactly this kind of sentence. "Had Sam checked the brake, we'd be fine" is a counterfactual: it says what didn't happen, not what did. The missed brake check can still go in the write-up, but as one finding, not the conclusion.

**1. It's the specific pattern incident reviews are told to avoid.** The US Forest Service's *Learning From Unintended Outcomes* guide gives almost your reviewers' sentence as its example of what not to write: "If this person had done X, then the outcome would have been Y and the accident would not have occurred." It says a review is only useful when it learns "why people did what they actually did (why it made sense to them at the time)". It also warns that counterfactuals "blind the team from understanding the event in context." A counterfactual assumes you know what would have happened on a path nobody took. That can't be known.

**2. It's hindsight talking.** The DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook* (drawing on Dekker) says investigations get distorted by facts the analyst knows now but the people involved didn't know then. That pushes reviewers to hunt for the individual's shortcomings. Listing what someone should have done doesn't explain why they did what they did. The handbook's advice is to "focus on what could have prevented the event rather than simply concentrating on who caused an event."

**3. It treats one check as the whole system.** The DOE handbook says an accident happens only when one or more controls failed or were missing, and that how bad it gets depends on how many controls there were and how weak they were. If one skipped brake check was all that stood between normal work and a crane incident, that is a finding about the system. It treats human error as "the effect or symptom of deeper trouble in the system," not the cause. Questions the framing skips:
- Was the brake check workable, and was it actually done routinely?
- Was there time pressure, or a goal that competed with it?
- Were there other barriers, like inspections, interlocks or supervision? Why didn't they catch it?

**4. It costs you the next report.** The DOE handbook says "A just culture is a prerequisite for a reporting culture." The Forest Service guide quotes Dekker: blaming people makes them "tell fewer accounts." If the official write-up says "Sam caused this," people learn to stay quiet next time.

**5. Dropping this framing doesn't mean nobody is accountable.** Dekker again: "Blame-free or no-fault systems are not accountability-free systems." If your reviewers want to judge Sam's own conduct, do it separately with explicit tests, not through the narrative. The NHS *Just Culture Guide* (now superseded, but its logic still holds) asks:
- **Foresight:** was there a workable brake-check procedure in routine use, and did Sam knowingly skip it?
- **Substitution:** would comparable colleagues have done the same in the same conditions?
- **Training and supervision:** did Sam miss training their peers got, or supervision they should have had?

If the procedure wasn't workable, or peers would have done the same, the guide says singling out the individual is unlikely to be appropriate. Its starting position is that "Action singling out an individual is rarely appropriate."

**What to suggest to your reviewers:**
- Rewrite the claim as a question about the moment: *"How did it make sense, at the time, to operate without the brake check?"* The Forest Service guide's question set helps here: what was planned, what information people had, what was routine, what surprised them.
- Compare how the brake check is supposed to happen with how it actually happens day to day on that crane.
- List every control that failed or was missing, not just the last one.
- Take Sam's name out of the narrative. The Forest Service guide recommends role titles or neutral placeholder names in reports.
- Keep any judgement about Sam's conduct out of the learning write-up and handle it through a just-culture process.

One caveat: I don't know the facts of your incident. The brake check may well have mattered a lot. The point is that it opens the analysis rather than closing it.
