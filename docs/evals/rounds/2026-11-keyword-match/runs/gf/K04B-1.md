Your team needs a **Definition of Done**: one written standard for "finished" that every ticket has to meet, agreed by the whole group, testers included. Right now each developer is using their own private definition. The corpus covers this mainly through the Scrum Guide, with support from three other sources.

## 1. Write it down once, for everyone

Schwaber and Sutherland's Scrum Guide calls the Definition of Done "a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product." Its purpose is to give everyone the same understanding of what work is complete. If several teams work on one product, "they must mutually define and comply with the same Definition of Done." If your organisation already has a standard, that standard is the minimum each team must meet.

## 2. Put the work that every ticket needs into it

The Open Practice Library describes the Definition of Done as "a place where non-functional and functional work that should be performed for each and every work item can be managed." It means things like quality, assurance and documentation, agreed so that expectations line up across stakeholder groups. Compiling on a laptop is one personal check, not a shared one. A list that matches your problem could include:

- code merged and building in CI (not just on a laptop)
- automated tests passing
- peer review done
- tested in an integrated environment
- acceptance criteria met
- documentation updated

*(Those items are my suggestions. The corpus gives the principle but no checklist.)*

## 3. Keep it separate from acceptance criteria

The sources disagree here, and the difference matters for you:

- **Letaw's software-engineering handbook** treats the Definition of Done as a single story's acceptance criteria, written in given-when-then form.
- **The Scrum Guide and Open Practice Library** treat it as a standard that applies to every item. Each item also has its own acceptance criteria. The Open Practice Library's retrospective practice asks whether an improvement belongs in the Definition of Done or in acceptance criteria.

You probably need both. "It only works on my laptop" means the shared standard is missing. "It doesn't do what we asked" means that ticket's acceptance criteria were missing.

## 4. The team owns it and enforces it

The Open Practice Library calls it "Collaboratively created, maintained and enforced criteria by the team." Put testers in the room when you draft it, so it reflects what they actually check. The Scrum Guide makes developers accountable for "Instilling quality by adhering to a Definition of Done." Quality is their job, not something testers catch afterwards.

## 5. Don't count "almost done" as done

This rule is what makes the definition stick. The Scrum Guide says: "If a Product Backlog item does not meet the Definition of Done, it cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review. Instead, it returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration."

## 6. Plan and estimate against it

In Sprint Planning, the Scrum Guide has developers plan the work needed to meet the Definition of Done. Knowing it well makes their forecasts more reliable. In practice, testing and integration time goes into the estimate, not after it.

## 7. Revisit it regularly

The Scrum Guide's retrospective includes inspecting the Definition of Done. Each time a tester finds something half-finished, ask whether it points to a missing line in the definition.

## 8. Check how the team is structured

If testers sit in a separate group, part of the problem is structural. The Org Topologies primer compares a team's ability to deliver without handoffs to "increasing completeness of the Definition of Done." A team that lacks testing skills can't honestly include testing in its definition. Over time, consider bringing testing into the team.
