In the Org Topologies map, the vertical axis shows the **scope of work mandate**: how broad a piece of work an organizational unit is allowed to own. The source is Krivitsky, Larman & Flemm, *Strategic Org Design: The Primer* (2025). The horizontal axis covers a unit's scope of skills. The vertical axis covers the scope of its work, which the authors say "determines how units collaborate, learn, and share work. The higher a unit is on the map, the bigger is its field of play." At the highest levels, the work of different units overlaps. That pushes them to own work together and operate as one larger "Team-of-Teams."

**Economic reading.** The axis "represents the switching costs to work on something different (decreasing from bottom to top), and, therefore how easy it is for the whole organization to switch direction, ideally driven by feedback, innovation, competition, or strategic changes." The authors add: "The larger the scope of work the units own, the closer they are to holistically understanding and solving real problems."

**The five levels, bottom to top:**

1. **Tasks Focus.** The narrowest scope. The unit receives fine-grained tasks that match its expertise, like a sous-chef told to chop onions. The authors stress: "It's an org design decision to limit their mandate, even though they may be capable of learning and doing more."
2. **Capabilities Focus.** The unit owns a full capability, feature or service element within a product or service, such as an insurance claims-processing group. Typical side effects are "silo thinking and local optimizations."
3. **Partial-Business Focus.** The unit owns one business vertical, kept clearly separate from the others, as with Uber Ride versus Uber Eats. Teams at this level collaborate and learn across team lines because they care about the business result. The primer says this "usually leads to organizational simplification."
4. **Whole-Business Focus.** The unit works on whole-business problems and customer value, with no permanent internal divisions. The example is a two-person, Instagram-like startup.
5. **Unbounded.** Units go wherever they want to explore new ideas and unmet customer needs, possibly in unknown markets. The example is the failing games company that kept changing direction until it found Slack.

"Elevating" an organization in the primer's method means moving up and to the right on the map, which widens both mandates. The authors argue that AI makes the upward move easier.
