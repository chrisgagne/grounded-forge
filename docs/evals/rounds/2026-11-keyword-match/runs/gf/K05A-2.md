In Org Topologies, the vertical axis shows how much work an organizational unit is mandated to own, from narrow tasks at the bottom to the whole business and beyond at the top.

The primer defines it as "the scope of work mandate given to organizational units. That determines how units collaborate, learn, and share work. The higher a unit is on the map, the bigger is its field of play." At the top of the axis, different units' work overlaps. That pushes them "to learn to co-own and work as one super unit – 'Team-of-Teams'." (Krivitsky, Larman & Flemm, *Strategic Org Design: The Primer*, 2025, p. 7.)

**Economic reading.** The axis "represents the switching costs to work on something different (decreasing from bottom to top), and, therefore how easy it is for the whole organization to switch direction, ideally driven by feedback, innovation, competition, or strategic changes." The horizontal axis is read in terms of transaction costs; the vertical one is read in terms of switching costs.

**The five levels, bottom to top:**

1. **Tasks Focus.** Units handle the narrowest work and receive small tasks matched to their expertise ("chop onions", "design graphic"). Example: a sous-chef. The authors stress that this limit is an org design choice, not a cap on what people can do: "It's an org design decision to limit their mandate, even though they may be capable of learning and doing more."
2. **Capabilities Focus.** Units own a full capability, feature or service element within a product. Example: an insurance claims-processing group. The usual side effects are "silo thinking and local optimizations."
3. **Partial-Business Focus.** Units own one business area, clearly separated from the others. Example: Uber Ride versus Uber Eats as separate profit centres. Teams at this level work and learn together because they care about business outcomes, and this tends to simplify the organization.
4. **Whole-Business Focus.** Units work together on problems and customer value for the whole business, with no permanent internal divisions. Example: a two-person Instagram-like startup.
5. **Unbounded.** Units go wherever they need to in order to find unmet customer needs, possibly in unknown markets, and learn whatever that takes. Example: the failing games company that kept pivoting until it found Slack.

**How it fits the map.** Combined with the horizontal axis (scope of skills mandate), the vertical axis creates the OT grid. "Elevating" an organization means moving it up and to the right, which widens both mandates. The authors argue that AI makes moving up the vertical axis "ever easier," so the top-right "Driving" archetypes become more achievable.
