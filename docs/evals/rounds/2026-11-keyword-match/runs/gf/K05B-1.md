Org Topologies has a name for this: **scope of work mandate**. It's the vertical axis of the map in Krivitsky, Larman & Flemm's *Strategic Org Design: The Primer* (2025). The authors define it as "the scope of work mandate given to organizational units." The higher a unit sits, "the bigger is its field of play."

**The five rungs, bottom to top:**

| Level | What the unit works on | Primer's example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Tasks** | Small, specific tasks that match its expertise | A sous-chef told to "chop onions"; a designer sketching a wireframe |
| **2. Capabilities** | One whole capability (a feature, function or service element) | Insurance claims-processing group, Web Search Team |
| **3. Partial-Business** | One business area, kept apart from the others | Uber Ride vs Uber Eats, which are separate profit centres |
| **4. Whole-Business** | Problems and customer value across the whole business, with no permanent internal splits | A two-person Instagram-like startup |
| **5. Unbounded** | Units go wherever they need to meet customer needs nobody has found yet | The failing games company that kept pivoting until it found Slack |

**Where your squads sit.** A "tweak this button" ticket is a Tasks-level input. The primer's main point here applies directly: "It's an org design decision to limit their mandate, even though they may be capable of learning and doing more." Being stuck at the bottom says something about how the organisation is designed, not about what the squad can do.

**Why moving up matters.**
- **Cost of changing direction.** In economic terms the axis measures switching costs, which fall as you go up. A higher mandate makes it easier for the organisation to change course.
- **Solving real problems.** "The larger the scope of work the units own, the closer they are to holistically understanding and solving real problems."
- **Outcomes, not just output.** "For an organizational unit to deliver outcomes, it needs to work in a higher-level business context." A squad that only gets button tickets can produce output but can't own an outcome.
- **The middle rung has its own trap.** For Capabilities-level units, "Common consequences include silo thinking and local optimizations."
- **The top rungs change how teams work together.** Units at Partial-Business level and above tend to collaborate across teams because they share in the business's success or failure. At the highest levels their scopes overlap, which pushes them to work as a "Team-of-Teams."

**Naming a squad precisely.** The second axis is **skills mandate**: Functional, Multi-Skill, End-to-End, Expanding, Unbounded. Combining the two axes gives short labels. In the primer's worked example, cross-functional dev teams that only receive broken-down tasks are **TASKS-2**, and the product manager overseeing the whole business is **WHOLE-1**. So you could describe a squad as "TASKS-2 today, aiming for CAPS-3." The authors call moving up and to the right on the map **Elevation**.

**One caveat.** The primer doesn't have a level called "entire product." A whole product roughly matches Partial-Business if it's one of several lines, or Whole-Business if it *is* the business. Capabilities is the feature level.
