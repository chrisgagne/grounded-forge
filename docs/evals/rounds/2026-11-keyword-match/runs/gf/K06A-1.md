## What the paper plant is

The DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook* (Vol. 1, Ch. 1) uses the term to describe two plants that exist side by side:

- **The physical plant** is "the systems, structures, and components used in the production processes."
- **The paper plant** is "the aggregate of all the documentation that helps control the configuration of the physical plant": procedures, instructions, drawings, specifications and the like.

Because the handbook defines the workplace as any location where either plant can be changed, the job site includes the engineer's desk as well as the shop floor. Different people work on each plant. Front-line workers "touch" the physical plant. Engineers and other technical staff "alter the paper plant or modify processes and procedures that direct the activities of workers in the physical plant." Managers shape behaviour through directives and their own example. The handbook says: "The activities of all these individuals need to be controlled."

## How flaws in the paper plant cause errors in the physical plant

The handbook states the link directly: "Error can come from either the industrial plant or the paper plant… Flaws in the paper plant can lie dormant and can lead to undesirable outcomes in the physical plant or even personal injury." The mechanism has several parts:

1. **Flaws can lie dormant.** A mistake in a drawing, specification or procedure does no harm until someone acts on it. Vol. 2 describes knowledge workers (scientists, engineers, procedure writers, trainers) as people "who create and modify the paper plant and who can make errors and mistakes that can enter into the system and later cause events." This is the latent-failure pattern. The handbook cites an NRC/INEEL study that found four latent failures for every active failure. Design and design-change problems were a factor in 81% of the human-error events in that study.

2. **Doing the job correctly can still produce a bad result.** The handbook defines performance as behaviour plus results (P = B + R) because compliant behaviour, such as following an incorrectly written procedure, can still lead to a bad outcome. The worker doesn't make the error. They pass on a flaw that someone wrote into the paper.

3. **Paper errors are hard to catch.** Citing research at one DOE site, the handbook says engineers tend to be critical of others but not of themselves. As a result, their errors can go undetected for years, which reduces the chance to learn from them. For this reason it recommends error-reduction techniques for knowledge workers that target knowledge-based errors.

4. **Error-likely situations occur at both plants.** An error-likely situation arises when the demands of a task exceed what the individual can do at the moment they are "touching" either the physical or the paper plant. Event-free performance therefore depends on reducing error "both where people touch the facility and where they touch the paper (procedures, instructions, drawings, specifications, and the like)." It also depends on keeping the controls against residual errors intact.

In short, the paper plant is the documentation that controls how the physical plant is configured. An error made there can sit unnoticed until a worker carries it out faithfully and an event occurs in the physical plant.
