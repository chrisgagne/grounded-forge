**What the paper plant is.** The idea comes from the DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook*. It splits a facility into two plants. The physical plant is "the systems, structures, and components used in the production processes." The paper plant is "the aggregate of all the documentation that helps control the configuration of the physical plant." In practice that means procedures, instructions, drawings, specifications, policies and design-basis documents. The handbook defines a workplace as anywhere either plant can be changed. Front-line workers "touch" the physical plant. Engineers and other technical staff change the paper plant, or change the processes and procedures that direct workers in the physical plant. The handbook says plainly that "Error can come from either the industrial plant or the paper plant."

**How flaws in it cause error in the physical plant.** The handbook gives four connected reasons:

1. **The flaws sit dormant.** "Flaws in the paper plant can lie dormant and can lead to undesirable outcomes in the physical plant or even personal injury." The handbook calls these latent errors: hidden weaknesses that "go unnoticed at the time they occur and have no immediate apparent outcome." It says "Inaccuracies become embedded in paper-based directives, such as procedures, policies, drawings, and design bases documentation."

2. **Workers then act on bad documents.** A worker who follows an inaccurate document can damage equipment without knowing it. The handbook's examples are installing the wrong gasket, putting a valve in the wrong position, hanging a danger tag on the wrong component, and attaching the wrong label. This is why it defines performance as behaviour plus results (P = B + R). A worker who correctly follows an incorrectly written procedure behaves well and still gets a bad result.

3. **Nothing signals the mistake.** "Usually, there is no immediate feedback that an error has been made." The handbook's example: "Engineers have performed key calculations incorrectly that slipped past subsequent reviews, invalidating the design basis for safety-related equipment." It adds that engineers tend to be critical of others but not of themselves. As a result, their errors "may go undetected for long periods of time, sometimes years," and the engineer may never learn that they made one.

4. **This is common.** The handbook cites an NRC study by INEEL. Human error was a causal factor in 79 percent of licensee events, with four latent failures for every active one. Design and design-change problems were a factor in 81 percent of the human-error events.

**What this means in practice.** Error-prevention work has to cover the people who write and change documents, not just the people who handle equipment. Volume 2 aims its tools at "scientists, engineers, procedure writers, trainers, and other knowledge workers who create and modify the paper plant and who can make errors and mistakes that can enter into the system and later cause events." Volume 1 says event-free performance depends on reducing error "both where people touch the facility and where they touch the paper (procedures, instructions, drawings, specifications, and the like)." It also depends on keeping the defences against the errors that still get through in good working order.
