Yes. The DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook* (Vol. 1) calls it the **"paper plant"**: "the aggregate of all the documentation that helps control the configuration of the physical plant" (Ch. 1, p. 1-11). That covers drawings, procedures, manuals, design-basis documents and so on. The "physical plant" is the actual equipment and structures.

Your scenario matches the handbook's framing closely:

- **Who changes which plant.** Engineers and technical staff "alter the paper plant." Front-line workers "touch" the physical plant as they do their tasks. So the engineer's edit and the technician's injury happened at two different places in the system.
- **Why it sat unnoticed.** The handbook warns that "Flaws in the paper plant can lie dormant and can lead to undesirable outcomes in the physical plant or even personal injury." It also cites research from one DOE site finding that engineers tend to be critical of others but not of themselves, so their errors may go undetected for years.
- **The error type.** The engineer's edit is a **latent error**. Latent errors "go unnoticed at the time they occur and have no immediate apparent outcome," and "Inaccuracies become embedded in paper-based directives, such as procedures, policies, drawings, and design bases documentation" (Ch. 2). The technician following the drawing would be the **active error**: the point where the dormant flaw finally has a visible effect.

So the records are the **paper plant**, and the mistake sitting in them is a **latent error** (the handbook also calls it a latent condition).
