Yes. The DOE *Human Performance Improvement Handbook* calls it the **"paper plant"**: "the aggregate of all the documentation that helps control the configuration of the physical plant" (Vol. 1, Ch. 1, p. 1-11). That covers drawings, manuals, procedures, policies and design-basis documents. The handbook contrasts it with the **physical plant**, meaning the systems, structures and components themselves.

Your scenario matches the handbook's framing:

- **Who changes which plant.** Front-line workers "touch" the physical plant. Engineers and other technical staff "perform activities that alter the paper plant." So the engineer's edit and the technician's work happened in two different "plants."
- **Why the mistake sat unnoticed.** The handbook says, "Flaws in the paper plant can lie dormant and can lead to undesirable outcomes in the physical plant or even personal injury" (p. 1-11).
- **The error type.** The engineer's mistake is a **latent error**. Latent errors "go unnoticed at the time they occur and have no immediate apparent outcome." Among the handbook's examples: "Inaccuracies become embedded in paper-based directives, such as procedures, policies, drawings, and design bases documentation" (Ch. 2). The technician following the drawing is where an active error, or simply the act of touching the plant, sets off the hidden flaw.
- **What follows from that.** Vol. 2 says its error-prevention tools are meant for "knowledge workers who create and modify the paper plant," not only for the people doing hands-on work. Vol. 1 adds that controls for latent errors "are designed more to catch them than to prevent them," because people usually don't know when they've made one.

So the records are the paper plant, and the dormant mistake inside them is a latent error, which the handbook also calls a latent condition.
