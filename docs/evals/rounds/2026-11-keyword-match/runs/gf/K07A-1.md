The corpus covers this in one source, the **OpenStax *Psychology 2e*** textbook, Chapter 15.3, "Perspectives on Psychological Disorders."

**What the model claims**

The textbook calls the diathesis-stress model (Zuckerman, 1999) an approach that "integrates biological and psychosocial factors to predict the likelihood of a disorder." The idea is that people with an underlying predisposition for a disorder (a *diathesis*) are more likely than others to develop it when they face adverse environmental or psychological events (*stress*). Its examples of stress are childhood maltreatment, negative life events and trauma.

The main points:

- **Both factors are needed.** "The key assumption of the diathesis-stress model is that both factors, diathesis and stress, are necessary in the development of a disorder." A vulnerability alone doesn't produce a disorder, and neither does stress alone.
- **The two trade off.** There are several versions of the model, but in general "the level of stress needed to produce the disorder is inversely proportional to the level of diathesis." Someone with high vulnerability may develop a disorder after little stress. Someone with low vulnerability would need much more.
- **A diathesis doesn't have to be biological.** The book says a diathesis "is not always a biological vulnerability". It can be psychological, such as a habit of interpreting life events in a pessimistic, self-defeating way.
- **Why it matters.** The textbook presents the model as a way to combine the biological view (genes, brain structure, neurotransmitters) with the psychosocial view (learning, stress, faulty thinking, environment). Many disorders "develop not from a single cause, but from a delicate fusion between partly biological and partly psychosocial factors."

**How the textbook applies it to specific disorders**

- **Depression:** Stressful life events reliably predict depression, but most people who go through them don't become depressed. The book offers a diathesis-stress reading as the explanation. One candidate vulnerability it names is a variant of a gene that regulates serotonin (5-HTTLPR), from Caspi et al., 2003. Cognitive theories, including Beck's, are framed the same way: a "cognitive vulnerability" (negative, maladaptive thinking) combined with stressful life events triggers depression.
- **PTSD:** People who have one or two short versions of a gene that regulates serotonin face much higher PTSD risk after childhood adversity or adult trauma. The book reads this as a possible diathesis-stress case.
- **Schizophrenia:** In an adoption study, adoptees with high genetic risk mostly developed schizophrenia only if they grew up in a disturbed home: 36.8% did, compared with 5.8% of those raised in healthy homes. The textbook concludes that both genetic vulnerability and environmental stress are needed, and that "genes alone do not show the complete picture."

**In short:** a disorder develops when a predisposition, biological or psychological, meets enough stress to trigger it. The more vulnerable a person is, the less stress it takes.
