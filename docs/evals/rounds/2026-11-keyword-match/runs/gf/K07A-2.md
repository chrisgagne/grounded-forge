The diathesis-stress model says a disorder develops when two things come together: a pre-existing vulnerability (the "diathesis") and a stressful experience. Neither one is usually enough by itself. The only source in the corpus that covers this is the OpenStax *Psychology 2e* textbook (Ch. 15.3, "Perspectives on Psychological Disorders").

**The core claim.** The textbook presents the model (attributed to Zuckerman, 1999) as a way to combine biological and psychosocial factors to predict how likely a disorder is. It says that "people with an underlying predisposition for a disorder (i.e., a diathesis) are more likely than others to develop a disorder when faced with adverse environmental or psychological events (i.e., stress)." Its examples of stress are childhood maltreatment, negative life events and trauma.

**Diathesis doesn't have to mean genes.** A vulnerability can be biological, but "some diatheses may be psychological (e.g., a tendency to think about life events in a pessimistic, self-defeating way)."

**Both factors are needed, and they trade off.** The model's key assumption is that "both factors, diathesis and stress, are necessary in the development of a disorder." The two also scale against each other: "the level of stress needed to produce the disorder is inversely proportional to the level of diathesis." Someone highly vulnerable may develop a disorder after mild stress. Someone with little vulnerability would need much more stress.

**Why the textbook favours it.** The text treats the model as a middle path between purely biological and purely psychosocial explanations. It says many disorders arise "not from a single cause, but from a delicate fusion between partly biological and partly psychosocial factors."

**How the textbook applies it to specific disorders:**
- **Schizophrenia:** In the Tienari et al. (2004) adoption study, adoptees with high genetic risk raised in disturbed families developed schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder at 36.8%. In the other groups the rate was about 5–6%. The text concludes that "both genetic vulnerability and environmental stress are necessary for schizophrenia to develop."
- **Major depression:** Most people who go through stressful life events don't become depressed. The text points to Caspi et al. (2003): people with short versions of the serotonin-regulating 5-HTTLPR gene were much more likely to become depressed after several stressful events, but not if they'd had few stressors. Cognitive theories (e.g., Beck) are described as diathesis-stress models too, with the vulnerability being negative, maladaptive thinking.
- **PTSD:** Childhood adversity or adult trauma raises PTSD risk more in people with short versions of the same gene. The text calls this "a possible diathesis-stress interpretation of PTSD."
