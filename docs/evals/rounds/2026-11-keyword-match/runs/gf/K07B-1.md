The corpus's answer is the **diathesis-stress model**. It's covered in one source, OpenStax *Psychology 2e* (Ch 15.3, 15.7 and 14.4), so this answer draws on a single introductory textbook. The model holds that a disorder needs two things: a pre-existing vulnerability (the *diathesis*) and a stressful event that sets it off. The divorce was the stress for both brothers. They probably differed in the diathesis.

## 1. Stress alone usually doesn't cause depression

The textbook says plainly that stressful life events, including "divorce or separation", often come right before a depressive episode. It then adds: "not everyone who experiences stressful life events or childhood adversities succumbs to depression—indeed, most do not." The model's key assumption is that "both factors, diathesis and stress, are necessary in the development of a disorder." The two also trade off against each other: "the level of stress needed to produce the disorder is inversely proportional to the level of diathesis." So for someone with a higher vulnerability, a smaller blow is enough to tip them over.

## 2. Siblings don't share all their biology

- **Genes.** Brothers share only part of their genes. Even identical twins match on major depression only 50% of the time (38% for fraternal twins). In the Caspi et al. (2003) study, people with short versions of a serotonin-regulating gene (5-HTTLPR) were much more likely to become depressed after several stressful events. People with two long versions were much less affected. With few stressful events, the short versions made little difference.
- **Stress physiology.** Many people with depression have high cortisol, especially those with early trauma such as losing a parent. High cortisol is itself a risk factor for later depression. Depressed people also show stronger amygdala reactions to negative stimuli and weaker prefrontal control to dampen them. So two brothers can feel the same blow but differ in how hard it lands and how well they recover.

## 3. Thinking styles can be the vulnerability

The textbook notes that "a diathesis is not always a biological vulnerability." It describes three cognitive versions:

- **Beck's depressive schemas.** These are built-in tendencies to see things negatively, with themes of loss, rejection and worthlessness. They can form in childhood and "remain dormant until they are activated by stressful or negative life events." A divorce is exactly that kind of event.
- **Hopelessness theory.** This risk comes from blaming bad events on stable, global causes ("It's never going to change", "It's going to affect my whole life") rather than temporary, specific ones. In one study, people with this style who faced uncontrollable stress were 7 times more likely to become depressed within six months.
- **Rumination.** This means dwelling passively on one's low mood instead of distracting oneself or problem-solving. It predicts more symptoms, more episodes and longer ones.

If Tom took the divorce as proof that "families fall apart, and it's about me," while Ian saw it as "this is about Mum and Dad, and it's temporary," the theory predicts what happened.

## 4. "The same divorce" may not have been the same for each brother

- **Exit events and humiliation.** Departures such as a divorce or a parent leaving home are strong triggers. They're especially strong when they leave someone feeling humiliated or devalued. A brother who felt rejected or caught in the middle went through a harsher event than one who didn't.
- **Perceived control.** The textbook quotes Bandura: "the intensity and chronicity of human stress is governed largely by perceived control over the demands of one's life." Ch 14 also connects lack of control to Seligman's learned helplessness.

## 5. What protects people

Social support is one of the strongest predictors of health in this literature, and it softens the body's stress response. Coping style matters too. Problem-focused coping works for stressors you can control. Emotion-focused coping works better for ones you can't, and a parental divorce is largely outside a child's control. If Ian had more people to lean on, or a better fit between his coping style and the situation, that would explain some of the gap.

**In short:** the divorce probably acted as a trigger on different starting vulnerabilities, made up of genes, stress physiology and learned thinking habits. It may also have been experienced differently, and each brother had a different safety net. This doesn't mean Tom was weaker. In this model, vulnerability is not a character flaw. The same textbook reports that cognitive therapy, which targets those negative thinking patterns, has good evidence behind it. If you're asking because of a real situation, Tom's depression is worth taking to a professional.
