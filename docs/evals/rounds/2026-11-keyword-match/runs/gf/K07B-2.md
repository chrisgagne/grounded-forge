The usual explanation is the **diathesis-stress model**: the divorce was the stress, and what differed between the brothers was the vulnerability (the *diathesis*) each one brought to it. On this question the corpus has one source, OpenStax's *Psychology 2e*. It names the model as a key framework for many disorders.

### 1. Same storm, different breaking points
The textbook says people with an underlying predisposition are more likely to develop a disorder "when faced with adverse environmental or psychological events." Its key point is that "the level of stress needed to produce the disorder is inversely proportional to the level of diathesis." Someone with high vulnerability can be tipped over by less. The predisposition doesn't have to be biological. It can also be psychological, such as "a tendency to think about life events in a pessimistic, self-defeating way." The text also says plainly that not everyone who goes through stressful life events "succumbs to depression—indeed, most do not." So Ian coping is the more common outcome, not a sign that Tom was weak.

### 2. Brothers don't share the same genes
Ordinary siblings differ genetically about as much as fraternal twins. Relatives of people with major depression have about twice the risk. Identical twins share major depression more often than fraternal twins (50% vs. 38%), so genes matter, though less than they do for bipolar disorder. The best-known gene × stress finding the book reports is Caspi et al. (2003). People with a short version of a serotonin-regulating gene (5-HTTLPR) were much more likely to become depressed after several stressful events. With few stressors, the same people were unlikely to become depressed. Even identical twins can turn out differently, because the same genes can be expressed differently depending on each twin's experiences.

### 3. How each brother reads the divorce
The textbook describes three cognitive diathesis-stress theories:
- **Beck's depressive schemas.** These are negative mental templates (loss, rejection, worthlessness) that can form in childhood, "remain dormant until they are activated by stressful or negative life events," and then bias attention and memory toward the negative.
- **Hopelessness theory.** People who explain bad events as stable ("It's never going to change") and global ("It's going to affect my whole life") are at higher risk. In one study, people under uncontrollable stress who scored high on this style were 7 times more likely to become depressed over six months.
- **Rumination.** This means dwelling passively on one's low mood instead of distracting oneself or problem-solving. It predicts more episodes and longer-lasting ones.

### 4. The "same" divorce wasn't the same event for both
The book calls a departure such as divorce or separation an **"exit event."** These are especially likely to trigger depression when they "humiliate or devalue the individual." Family turmoil and separation from a parent in childhood also raise the risk of depression later in life. The book doesn't discuss siblings, so this next part is my own application. Tom and Ian may have had quite different divorces: different ages, different time with each parent, one pulled into the conflict or feeling blamed.

### 5. Appraisal, control and support
Drawing on Lazarus, the textbook says stress "is largely in the eye of the beholder: it's not so much what happens to you as it is how you respond." It quotes Bandura that stress "is governed largely by perceived control over the demands of one's life." It also covers learned helplessness, where feeling powerless is linked to depression, and treats social support as a strong buffer. If Ian felt he had more control, or had friends to lean on, that alone could account for much of the gap. The book adds a biological route: high cortisol, which is more common after early-life trauma, is itself a risk factor for later depression.

**Bottom line:** Tom's depression most likely came from a heavy stressor landing on greater vulnerability (genetic, cognitive, or in what the divorce meant to him), with fewer buffers. On the practical side, the book notes that cognitive therapy grew directly out of Beck's theory and targets exactly these thinking patterns.

The corpus only covers this at intro-textbook level. It has nothing on divorce or on why siblings differ. Robert Plomin's work on why children in the same family turn out so differently would be a good candidate to add.
