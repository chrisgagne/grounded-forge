Equity theory says we're motivated by fairness, and we judge fairness by comparing ourselves with others. We compare what we get from work with what we put in, and then compare that ratio with other people's. Adams developed the theory, and the corpus covers it mainly in OpenStax *Organizational Behavior* (Ch 7.3–7.4). OpenStax *Principles of Management* and *Introduction to Business* cover it more briefly and agree.

**Inputs and outcomes are what we perceive them to be**
- **Inputs** are anything we feel we contribute that has value, such as time, effort, performance, education, skills and opportunities we passed up. Only our own view of an input's value counts. People sometimes count things the organisation or the law would call inappropriate, such as age, sex or social status.
- **Outcomes** are anything we feel we get back, such as pay, working conditions, status, a sense of achievement and friendships. Negative outcomes like stress and fatigue count too.

**The comparison with others matters most**
We first ask whether what we got matches what we put in. The more important question, in the textbook's words, is: "Did I get as much for my inputs as my coworkers got for theirs?" The people we compare ourselves with are **referent others**, usually colleagues doing work of similar difficulty. The text says "it is this comparison of the two ratios that has the strongest effect on our equity perceptions." The comparison has three possible results:
1. **Equity:** our ratio matches theirs.
2. **Overreward inequity:** our ratio is higher than theirs.
3. **Underreward inequity:** our ratio is lower than theirs.

**How this affects motivation**
- **Underreward** has the strongest effect. People feel dissatisfied, angry and driven to change the situation or leave it.
- **Overreward** rarely produces much reaction, and the feeling soon fades. In the text's words, "Most people are less sensitive to overreward inequities than they are to underreward inequities."
- The overall claim is that "employees engage in effective behaviors when they perceive equity and ineffective behaviors when they perceive inequity."

**What people do to restore equity**
The textbook illustrates this with John and Mary, two assembly-line workers who do the same work. John earns $500 a week and Mary earns $600. Following Adams, it lists seven responses John could make:
1. Reduce his own effort or the quality of his work.
2. Increase his own outcomes, for example by asking for a raise. The text notes that some people steal from their employer instead.
3. Push the other person to put in more.
4. Try to reduce the other person's outcomes.
5. Change how he sees either person's inputs or outcomes, for example telling himself "Mary does better work than I thought."
6. Compare himself with someone else instead. The text calls this "the simplest and most powerful way to deal with perceived inequity."
7. Leave, through absenteeism, a transfer or quitting. People usually do this only when the inequity feels large.

**Why people often feel unfairly treated**
Two common tendencies create a sense of inequity that isn't based in fact:
- **We overrate our own inputs.** In one study by the authors, more than 600 employees rated their own performance at an average of 6.2 out of 7.
- **We overestimate what others earn.** Pay secrecy makes this worse.

**What this means for managers**
- Measure performance accurately and give the largest raises to the best performers.
- Be open about pay ranges, even without revealing what individuals earn.
- Avoid favouritism. The text says "playing favorites" guarantees that people will see inequity.
- Watch out for "equity sensitives," people who react more strongly to unfairness than most.

*Principles of Management* draws a related point: employees expect rewards proportional to their contribution, not identical rewards. Treating everyone the same regardless of performance demotivates top performers. *Introduction to Business* advises managers to "make an effort to understand an employee's perceptions of fairness and take steps to reduce concerns about inequity."

**Research status**
The theory "continues to receive strong research support." Its main weakness is that each person defines inputs and outcomes differently, so researchers can't know them all. For the main ones, performance and pay, the textbook calls the theory a strong one. Recent work has extended it into **organisational justice**, which covers two things:
- **Distributive justice:** whether the outcomes are fair.
- **Procedural justice:** whether the process used to hand them out is fair. For example, layoffs based on seniority may seem fairer than layoffs based on a supervisor's opinion.
