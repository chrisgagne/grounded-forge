## Equity theory: how comparing yourself with others affects motivation

**The core idea.** The OpenStax *Organizational Behavior* text defines it like this: "Equity theory states that motivation is affected by the outcomes we receive for our inputs compared to the outcomes and inputs of other people." The theory comes from Adams. We ask two questions. One is about ourselves ("Did I get as much out of this as I put into it?"). The more important one is about others ("Did I get as much for my inputs as my coworkers got for theirs?").

**Inputs and outcomes are what we perceive them to be.**
- **Inputs** are anything we feel we contribute that has value, such as time, effort, performance level, education, skills and opportunities we gave up. If we think an input counts, it counts. The text says that whether anyone else agrees "is unimportant to us." This can include factors the organization would call inappropriate, like age or social status.
- **Outcomes** are anything we feel we get back, such as pay, working conditions, job status, a sense of achievement and friendship. Negative outcomes count too: stress, headaches, fatigue.

**The ratio comparison.** We first compare our own outcomes with our own inputs. But the theory's key prediction is that we then compare our ratio with other people's: "It is this comparison of the two ratios that has the strongest effect on our equity perceptions." The people we compare against are called **referent others**. They are usually colleagues doing work of similar difficulty and complexity. The comparison has three possible results:

| Comparison | State | Typical reaction |
|---|---|---|
| My ratio = referent's | Equity | Satisfied |
| My ratio > referent's | Overreward inequity | Rarely acted on. The text says feelings of overreward "are quite transient" |
| My ratio < referent's | Underreward inequity | "dissatisfied, angered, and motivated to change the situation (or escape the situation)" |

**How this drives behaviour.** Underreward inequity creates tension, and people act to reduce it. Adams lists seven ways:
1. Put in less (lower the quantity or quality of your work)
2. Increase your own outcomes (ask for a raise or promotion, or, in the bad case, steal)
3. Push the referent to put in more
4. Reduce the referent's outcomes
5. Change how you see the inputs or outcomes, yours or theirs
6. Pick a different referent. The text calls this "the simplest and most powerful way to deal with perceived inequity"
7. Leave, through absenteeism, transfer or quitting

The text sums up what this means for employers: "employees engage in effective behaviors when they perceive equity and ineffective behaviors when they perceive inequity."

**Why people often feel underrewarded when they aren't.** Two tendencies create inequity that isn't real. First, we overrate our own performance. In one study by the authors, more than 600 employees rated themselves at an average of 6.2 on a 7-point scale. Second, we overestimate other people's pay, especially when pay is kept secret. As a result, "in the vast majority of cases, employees experience (or perceive) underreward inequity rather than overreward."

**What managers do about it.** Employers measure performance as accurately as they can and give the biggest raises to the highest performers. They also share pay ranges for jobs, though not individual salaries, so employees have "a factual basis for judging equity." OpenStax *Principles of Management* makes a related point: an organization that rewards everyone equally regardless of performance tends to demotivate its top performers. OpenStax *Introduction to Business* advises managers to try to understand how employees see fairness and to act on their concerns about inequity.

**Research status.** "Equity theory continues to receive strong research support." The main criticism still stands: inputs and outcomes are poorly defined, because each person decides what counts. Recent work extends the theory into **organizational justice**. That covers distributive justice (whether outcomes are fair) and procedural justice (whether the process for allocating them is fair). The text's example: "layoffs based on seniority may be perceived as more fair than layoffs based on supervisors' opinions."

All three sources are introductory OpenStax textbooks that cover Adams second-hand. Adams's original work isn't in the corpus.
