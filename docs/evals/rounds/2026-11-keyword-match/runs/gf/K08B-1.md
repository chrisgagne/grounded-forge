She's very likely experiencing what the corpus calls **underreward inequity**, from Adams's equity theory. The main source is the OpenStax *Organizational Behavior* text, chapters 7–8.

**1. She compares her deal with his, not just her pay with her work.** Equity theory says people weigh what they get (pay, recognition) against what they put in (effort, experience, loyalty), and then compare that ratio with a "referent other." The text says "It is this comparison of the two ratios that has the strongest effect on our equity perceptions." A referent other is usually someone doing similar work nearby. A new hire at her own desk earning more is about as direct a comparison as you can get. Lawler's research, quoted in Chapter 8, makes the same point: satisfaction with pay "is influenced by comparisons with what happens to others, especially one's coworkers."

**2. Losing drive is a predictable reaction.** When people believe "others unfairly make more than we do," the text says they become "dissatisfied, angered, and motivated to change the situation (or escape the situation)." Adams lists the ways people try to restore a sense of fairness:
- **Cut their own effort** ("decreasing the quantity or quality of our performance"). This is probably what's happening to her.
- **Push for better outcomes**, such as asking for a raise.
- **Change how they see things**, either by reinterpreting the situation or by comparing themselves with someone else.
- **Leave**, through absenteeism, a transfer or quitting.

**3. The pay she has stops feeling like a reward.** The text adds that when outcomes feel unfair next to a referent other, "we will associate a lower or even negative valence with those outcomes." In other words, the same salary now feels less valuable to her. Herzberg's theory, also in the corpus, points the same way: pay is a "hygiene" factor. Getting it right doesn't motivate anyone much, but getting it wrong causes real dissatisfaction.

**4. The process matters as much as the number.** The text separates *distributive justice* (is the outcome fair?) from *procedural justice* (was the way it was decided fair?). If she can't see why a newcomer was put above her, it may look like effort and loyalty don't count. That hits motivation harder than the pay gap alone.

**Two caveats from the same source.** Most people overrate their own performance, so some perceived unfairness doesn't hold up when checked. In her case, though, she knows the real figure. Separately, if the gap follows gender, the text notes that the US Equal Pay Act of 1963 requires equal pay for equal work. It also reports that women still earn about 80 cents per dollar men earn in similar jobs. That turns the issue into a legal one, not just a motivational one.

**What helps.** Of the options equity theory lists, the constructive one is to act on the outcome rather than on her effort. She could ask her manager how pay was set for her role and for his (procedural justice) and make a case for an adjustment. The theory predicts that cutting effort or quietly disengaging tends to make things worse for both her and the employer.

The corpus doesn't say why new hires often get more than existing staff (for example, hiring at current market rates). It explains her reaction, not why the gap exists.
