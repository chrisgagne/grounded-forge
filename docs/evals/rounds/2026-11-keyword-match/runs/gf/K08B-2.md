Your colleague's reaction fits a well-established idea in the corpus: **equity theory** (Adams), as explained in OpenStax *Principles of Management* (Ch 14.3) and *Organizational Behavior* (Ch 7.3).

## Why the drive drops

**1. People judge pay by comparison, not by the amount alone.** In equity theory, we weigh what we put into a job (effort, skill, experience) against what we get out of it (pay, recognition). Then we compare that balance with a "referent other": someone doing similar work. A new hire at the same desk is about as direct a comparison as there is. If he gets more for what looks like the same work, or less, she is in what the theory calls **underreward inequity**. The result is dissatisfaction, whatever her salary was worth to her the day before.

**2. Cutting effort is one of the main ways people respond.** Adams listed several things people do to ease the tension of feeling unfairly paid. The first is to change their own inputs, "by decreasing the quantity or quality of our performance." The others are asking for a raise, trying to change the other person's inputs or pay, reinterpreting the situation, picking someone else to compare with, or leaving (through absence, transfer or quitting). Losing drive is the first response, and it's often not deliberate. The textbook's bottom line: "employees engage in effective behaviors when they perceive equity and ineffective behaviors when they perceive inequity."

**3. Unfairness can make her own pay feel worth less.** The management text links equity theory to expectancy theory (the idea that people put in effort when they believe effort leads to results they value). If outcomes feel unfair compared with a peer's, we attach "a lower or even negative valence with those outcomes." So her salary didn't change, but it now feels worth less to her, and the effort to earn it seems less worthwhile.

**4. Pay doesn't motivate much, but unfair pay demotivates a lot.** In Herzberg's motivator-hygiene theory, pay is a "hygiene" factor: getting it right doesn't drive people, but getting it wrong causes real dissatisfaction. Recognition, achievement and growth are what actually motivate. The text warns that "Intense forms of dissatisfaction distract employees from important work-related activities and tend to be demotivating," and says managers must fix hygiene problems before motivators can work. So even a job she enjoys can feel flat while this is unresolved.

**5. How the pay was decided matters too.** Later research extends equity theory into **organisational justice**. That covers distributive justice (whether outcomes are fair) and procedural justice (whether the process for deciding them is fair). If nobody can explain why a newcomer earns more, the gap feels worse than one with a clear reason.

## Two caveats worth considering

- **The gap may have a reason.** The same chapter notes that people tend to rate their own performance higher than it is. Maybe he negotiated harder, brings scarcer skills, or was hired when the market rate was higher. Those reasons may not make it feel fair, but they're worth knowing.
- **It may be a gender pay issue.** OpenStax *Business Ethics* (Ch 6.2) reports that women earned about 80% of men's wages as of 2016, and that a small difference in starting salary grows over a career. In the US, the Equal Pay Act requires equal pay for jobs demanding "equal skill, effort, and responsibility" under similar working conditions. Seniority, merit and production-based pay systems are allowed defences (OpenStax *Business Law*, Ch 9.3). If the work really is the same, this deserves a closer look rather than being written off as low morale.

## What tends to help

The theory points to actually fixing the inequity, rather than just encouraging her to compare herself with someone else. That means asking her manager what the pay is based on, and asking for an adjustment if the work is equivalent. The textbook tells employers to "do everything they can to prevent feelings of inequity," which makes it a reasonable conversation for her to start.
