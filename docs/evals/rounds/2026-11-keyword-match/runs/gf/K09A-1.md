**Fixes that fail** is a system archetype: a common feedback pattern where a quick fix makes a problem better for a while, then makes it worse. The corpus's main treatment is the Social System Design Lab's *Methods Brief 1.07: System Archetypes* (Branz, Farrell, Hu, Liem & Ballard, 2021). It defines the archetype this way:

> "The 'fixes that fail' archetype describes attempts to address a problem through a short-term fix. The fix might solve the problem in the short run, but it also generates unintended consequences that actually make the original problem worse."

**How it works.** Two loops act together. The fix relieves the symptom quickly. It also triggers a side effect that builds up slowly and feeds back into the original problem. The early relief makes the fix look like it's working, so people keep using it while the side effect grows.

**The brief's example: exclusionary discipline.** A child has a hard morning, skips breakfast, arrives late and is disruptive in class. The teacher sends her to time-out, suspension or the principal's office. That is a short-term fix: it does remove "the disruption from the classroom." Over time, though, the child "becomes even more disengaged from school" and feels that no adults there care about her. Her behaviour gets worse. (The last line of the example is cut off in the converted text.)

An earlier brief in the same series (1.05) has a second case. A district brings in an intensive new curriculum to cut dropouts. The dropout rate falls in year one. But the extra teacher workload leads to burnout and weaker teacher–student relationships, and after three years the rate is higher than when they started.

**Where it comes from.** The SSDL brief adapts its archetypes from Daniel Kim's *Systems Archetypes I* (Pegasus, 1993). It presents fixes that fail as the first of five, alongside success to the successful, shifting the burden, drifting goals and limits to growth. Barbrook-Johnson's *Systems Mapping* lists it among Kim's eight archetypes, which it suggests as starting templates for causal loop diagrams.

**A close relative: shifting the burden.** This also starts with a quick fix, but the harm works differently. The quick fix takes time and energy away from the fundamental solution, so people come to rely on quick fixes. In fixes that fail, the fix's side effects make the original problem itself worse.

**How to use it.** The brief cautions that archetypes "should not be used prescriptively." Jumping to an archetype label can oversimplify a situation. In a workshop it can also let the modeller's expertise outweigh what participants know from experience. Treat fixes that fail as a starting idea to test, not a diagnosis.
