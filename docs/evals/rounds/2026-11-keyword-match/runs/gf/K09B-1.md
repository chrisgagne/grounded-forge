This is the **"fixes that fail"** pattern, one of the standard system archetypes. The corpus covers it in the Social System Design Lab's *Systems Thinking Foundations* briefs (Farrell et al., 2021), which adapt it from Daniel Kim's *Systems Archetypes I*. Barbrook-Johnson's *Systems Mapping* also lists it among Kim's eight core archetypes.

### How the pattern works

The brief defines it like this: a short-term fix that "might solve the problem in the short run, but it also generates unintended consequences that actually make the original problem worse."

In your case it has two loops:

- **The loop you intended:** the backlog grows, you add temps, more complaints get answered, and the backlog shrinks.
- **The loop you didn't intend:** temps give sloppy answers, customers come back or complain again, and the backlog grows.

There's usually a delay before the second loop kicks in, so the fix looks like it's working at first. The brief's school dropout example follows the same path. A district brought in an intensive new curriculum, and dropouts fell in Year 1. But the curriculum overloaded teachers, and by Year 3 the dropout rate was higher than when they started.

### Why the backlog didn't shrink

The briefs' material on accumulations explains this. A backlog behaves like a bathtub: it only goes down when complaints are resolved faster than new ones arrive. A sloppy answer looks like a resolution but doesn't stay one. It sends the complaint back into the backlog, often with an angrier customer, so the temps were adding to the backlog while they emptied it. The brief makes the same point about a therapy waiting list: "The system will react to the accumulation, not only the input." It also notes that raising inflow, without fixing why people leave, just "speed[s] up how quickly teachers are moving through the system."

### A related pattern to rule out: "shifting the burden"

Check whether you're also caught in **"shifting the burden."** Here the quick fix "may actually impede our ability to invest time and energy to the fundamental solution," and the quick fix starts to look like the only option. That would apply if relying on temps has used up the budget or attention needed for the underlying cause. That cause might be why so many complaints come in, or why the permanent team can't keep up. If the next proposal is "more temps," you're probably in this pattern.

### What to do about it

The feedback approach says to change the system's structure, not just push harder on the fix. In this situation that means:

- **Weaken the unintended loop.** Measure whether complaints are resolved the first time, not how many get answered. Give temps training, scripts, quality checks and a way to escalate.
- **Work on what feeds the backlog.** Find out what causes the complaints in the first place.
- **Watch the delay.** Track repeat complaints for a few weeks after any fix, because that's when the unintended loop shows up.

The brief also warns that archetypes should not be "used prescriptively." Treat the label as a hypothesis to test with the people who handle the complaints, not as the final diagnosis.
