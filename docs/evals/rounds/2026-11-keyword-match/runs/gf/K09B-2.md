This is the **"fixes that fail"** pattern, one of the five system archetypes in the Social System Design Lab's *Systems Thinking Foundations* briefs (Farrell et al., 2021, adapted from Daniel Kim's *Systems Archetypes I*, 1993). The brief defines it like this:

> "The 'fixes that fail' archetype describes attempts to address a problem through a short-term fix. The fix might solve the problem in the short run, but it also generates unintended consequences that actually make the original problem worse."

Your case fits that structure:

- **The problem:** a backlog of complaints.
- **The short-term fix:** hire temps. The backlog goes down for a while.
- **The unintended consequence:** the temps' sloppy answers create new complaints.
- **The loop closes:** the new complaints refill the backlog the temps were hired to clear. That puts pressure on you to add more of the same fix.

The briefs' examples come from schools, but they say the same structures turn up "in businesses" too. Applying it to a complaints queue is my reading, but it's a direct fit. Their closest example is a district that brought in a new curriculum to cut dropouts. Dropouts fell in Year 1, but the extra workload burned out teachers, and by Year 3 the rate was "even higher than where it started."

**The backlog view.** The briefs also treat a backlog as an *accumulation*, a stock that fills and drains like a bathtub. They warn that "the system will react to the accumulation, not only the input." Speeding up the outflow doesn't help when the outflow feeds back into the inflow. Their teacher-attrition example says something similar: if you ignore the outflow, "you won't solve the problem – you will just speed up how quickly teachers are moving through the system." Here, each sloppy answer moves a complaint out of the queue and puts one or more back in.

**A related archetype to watch for: "shifting the burden."** If temps become your standard response to complaint spikes, you may be drifting into this one. The brief warns that "the quick-fix to the symptoms may actually impede our ability to invest time and energy to the fundamental solution." Here the fundamental solution would be fixing whatever generates the complaints, or building lasting capacity to answer them well. The quick fix keeps winning because it's faster.

**What the briefs suggest doing about it:**
- Map the loops explicitly, ideally with the people involved (agents, temps, customers' representatives).
- Then change the structure. You can remove the reinforcing link, for example by adding a quality check before a temp's answer goes out. Or you can weaken it, for example by giving temps scripts or training, or routing only simple cases to them.

**One caution from the source:** archetypes are meant as hypotheses, not labels. "System archetypes are not meant to erase the uniqueness of a situation." It's worth confirming that the new complaints really come from the temps' answers, not from something else that changed at the same time.
