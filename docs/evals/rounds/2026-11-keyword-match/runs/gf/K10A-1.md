The *Engaging Everyone with Liberating Structures Handbook* (Heft & Pattillo, 2010) credits the matrix's two-axis framing to Ralph Stacey. In the handbook's words, Stacey distinguished:

> "two axes – the degree to which a group is certain about what's going on and the degree to which there is agreement about what to do or about the meaning of different aspects of the situation."

So the two axes are:

1. **Certainty:** how certain the group is about what's going on.
2. **Agreement:** how much the group agrees about what to do, or about what different parts of the situation mean.

The handbook uses the matrix to tell simple, complicated and complex situations apart. It borrows Brenda Zimmerman's examples: a recipe is simple, building an airplane is complicated, and raising a child is complex. The handbook says this matters because most strategies for working on problems are built for simple or complicated situations, and they don't work well in complex ones, which are "emergent and unpredictable." To run the exercise, you introduce the matrix and ask the group where their current work would sit on it.
