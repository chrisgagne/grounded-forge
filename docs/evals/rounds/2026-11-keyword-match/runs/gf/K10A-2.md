The matrix comes from Ralph Stacey, and its two axes are:

1. **Certainty:** "the degree to which a group is certain about what's going on."
2. **Agreement:** "the degree to which there is agreement about what to do or about the meaning of different aspects of the situation."

That's how the *Engaging Everyone with Liberating Structures Handbook* (Heft & Pattillo, 2010) describes it. The handbook uses the two axes to separate situations that are **simple, complicated, or complex**. It borrows Brenda Zimmerman's examples: following a recipe is simple, building an airplane is complicated, and raising a child is complex. It also warns that "many of our strategies and heuristics for working on problems are designed for simple or complicated situations," and that those strategies don't work well when a situation is complex, because complex situations are emergent and unpredictable. To use the matrix, you explain it and then ask the group where the things they're working on fall on the diagram.

The *Open Practice Library* has the same tool under the name "Stacey Matrix," with the same two axes: how certain you are about outcomes, and how much stakeholders agree. It links each position on the matrix to a way of managing. For example, high certainty with high agreement calls for command and control, and high certainty with low agreement calls for a consensus-based approach.
