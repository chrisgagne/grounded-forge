Yes. The tool you're describing is the **Agreement/Uncertainty Matrix**. In this corpus it comes from Heft and Pattillo's *Engaging Everyone with Liberating Structures Handbook* (Group Jazz, 2010).

**What it plots.** The handbook credits Ralph Stacey with the two axes: "the degree to which a group is certain about what's going on and the degree to which there is agreement about what to do or about the meaning of different aspects of the situation." Your situation lands on both axes. The board doesn't agree on a strategy, and nobody is sure how the market will react.

**Why it helps.** The matrix separates problems that are simple, complicated or complex. The handbook uses Brenda Zimmerman's examples: "a recipe is simple; building an airplane is complicated… But raising a child is complex." The distinction matters because "Many of our strategies and heuristics for working on problems are designed for simple or complicated situations. These strategies don't work well when things are complex because the situation is emergent and unpredictable." A board can stay stuck by applying a method built for complicated problems, such as more analysis or a better forecast, to an issue that is actually complex.

**How to run it.** The handbook keeps the exercise short: "Introduce the idea of the Agreement/Uncertainty Matrix and invite the group to think about where the things they're working on might be mapped in this diagram." For your board, that could look like this:

1. Draw the two axes, certainty and agreement, on a wall or whiteboard.
2. Have directors write each open strategic issue on its own sticky note, for example "pricing response if a competitor enters" or "which segment to prioritise."
3. Place each note where the group thinks it belongs, and talk through any note people disagree about.
4. Treat the notes that end up low on both axes differently from the rest. Further analysis is unlikely to resolve them.

**What the corpus doesn't cover.** The handbook gives only the two axes, the simple/complicated/complex distinction and the mapping exercise. It doesn't say what approach to use in each region of the matrix. Stacey's own work would be the place for that, but it isn't in this corpus, so I haven't drawn on it.

**Related tool.** The same handbook's *Six Words* exercise is pitched at topics like "missions, vision, purpose, or strategy." It asks a group to compress an idea into a few words, which might help your board find where its disagreement really lies.
