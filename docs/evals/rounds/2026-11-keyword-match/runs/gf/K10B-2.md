Yes. It's called the **Agreement/Uncertainty Matrix**, and it comes from Ralph Stacey. The corpus covers it in the *Liberating Structures* handbook (groupjazz.com).

**What it plots**

The handbook says Stacey separated situations along "two axes – the degree to which a group is certain about what’s going on and the degree to which there is agreement about what to do or about the meaning of different aspects of the situation." That fits your board's situation:

- **Certainty axis:** nobody knows how the market will react, so you're low on certainty.
- **Agreement axis:** the board can't settle on a strategy, so you're low on agreement.

**Why it matters**

The matrix sorts work into simple, complicated and complex. The handbook uses Brenda Zimmerman's examples: "a recipe is simple; building an airplane is complicated," because if you understand the engineering you can be "pretty certain it will fly. But raising a child is complex." Then it gives the reason to bother: "Many of our strategies and heuristics for working on problems are designed for simple or complicated situations. These strategies don’t work well when things are complex because the situation is emergent and unpredictable."

For your board, that means a decision that is low on both certainty and agreement probably won't be settled by more analysis or a better plan. Those methods suit airplane-type problems, not child-raising ones.

**How to use it with the board**

The handbook's instructions are short: introduce the matrix, then "invite the group to think about where the things they’re working on might be mapped in this diagram." In practice:

1. Draw the two axes on a wall or whiteboard.
2. Write each strategic issue on its own sticky note. Split the strategy into parts, such as pricing, market entry and timing.
3. Have the board place each issue. Disagreement about where an issue goes is useful information in itself.
4. Pick an approach for each zone. Settle the clear issues quickly, and handle the complex ones with small experiments and regular review instead of one big decision.

**What the corpus doesn't cover:** the handbook gives the matrix one page. It shows a diagram but doesn't describe each zone or say what to do in each one. Stacey's original work isn't in the corpus, so the zone-by-zone advice in step 4 is my own reading, not something the handbook says.
