# Answer

Question: Please draw some interesting parallels between US Marines war-fighting doctrine and modern "textbook" business administration.

The demo corpus holds one military source, MCDP 1 itself, and general business textbooks, so every parallel here is drawn by setting the two side by side; no source in the corpus applies mission command, intent or manoeuvre thinking to business directly, and none speaks for the textbook tradition beyond OpenStax. The management textbook's deep reference leaves out its exercise apparatus, and only the chapters on decisions, management history, environments, strategy, structure and planning were read for this answer. Candidates that would let the corpus answer this question directly: Stephen Bungay's The Art of Action (mission command applied to business strategy and execution), Peter Drucker's The Practice of Management (the primary source for management by objectives), and Stanley McChrystal and colleagues' Team of Teams (decentralised command carried from the military into large organisations).

## Both start from not knowing

Both traditions begin with the same problem: how to act when nobody can know what will happen next. MCDP 1 sets out its starting point: “Because we can never eliminate uncertainty, we must learn to fight effectively despite it. We can do this by developing simple, flexible plans; planning for likely contingencies; de-veloping standing operating procedures; and fostering initia-tive among subordinates.” [1] The management textbook comes at the same problem through planning: The management textbook lists offsetting uncertainty and change as the first of five reasons managers plan, ahead of focus, a road map, efficiency and control. [2] For a manager, the practical question is whether initiative sits inside the answer to uncertainty or outside it. Once the environment turns rough, the textbook moves the work down to where it happens: “As organizations confront increasing environmental or technology-induced uncertainty, rapid environmental change, and turbulence, planning and controlling move closer to the point in the organization where the plans are implemented and carried out on a day-to-day basis.” [3] Put plainly, the rougher the market, the more a business has to be run the way a Marine unit is run.

## Commander's intent and the mission statement

The closest match in vocabulary is between commander's intent and the business mission statement, and the two even share a document. In MCDP 1, intent travels with the mission: MCDP 1 places the commander's intent inside the mission statement a commander hands to a subordinate. [4] The textbook gives its mission statement a similar job: The management textbook describes the mission statement as taking the reason behind a firm's vision and sketching broadly how the firm will pursue it. [5] The difference lies in what flows down the hierarchy after that. The textbook's version is a cascade of goals: “In the process, managers must make sure that lower-level goals combine to achieve higher-level goals.” [6] MCDP 1 on the two parts of every mission: “The task describes the action to be taken while the intent describes the purpose of the action. The task denotes what is to be done, and sometimes when and where; the intent explains why. Of the two, the intent is pre-dominant. While a situation may change, making the task ob-solete, the intent is more lasting and continues to guide our actions.” [7] For a business, this is the sharpest lesson in the whole comparison. A cascaded target goes stale the moment the market moves, while a stated reason behind the target still tells a team what to do next.

## How far to push decisions down

Where decisions get made is where the comparison turns most practical. MCDP 1 commits to one answer: “First and foremost, in order to generate the tempo of operations we desire and to best cope with the un- certainty, disorder, and fluidity of combat, command and con-trol must be decentralized. That is, subordinate commanders must make decisions on their own initiative, based on their understanding of their senior's intent, rather than passing in-formation up the chain of command and waiting for the deci-sion to be passed down.” [8] The management textbook describes organic structures this way: “As Exhibit 4.4 shows, these organizational forms work best in unstable, complex, changing environments. Their structures are flatter, with participatory communication and decision-making flowing in different directions.” [9] That sits inside its wider stance: The management textbook presents the contingency school's view that management has no universal rules, because each situation calls for its own response. [10] There is less conflict here than it first seems: war sits at the unstable, complex end of that scale, so a fixed rule and a contingent one give the same answer there. The Columbia board adds the move a business most needs: “Ability to Operate in Both a Centralized and Decentralized Manner: The ability to operate in a centralized manner when appropriate, and to operate in a decentralized manner when appropriate, is the hallmark of a high-reliability organization.” [11] Most firms run a stable core and an unstable edge at once, so the skill to build is switching between the two, not picking a side.

## Fast and slow decisions, paired differently

Decision-making holds the most surprising contrast. MCDP 1 assigns its two modes like this: “Decisionmaking may be an intuitive process based on ex-perience. This will likely be the case at lower levels and in fluid, uncertain situations. Alternatively, decisionmaking may be a more analytical process based on comparing several op- tions. This will more likely be the case at higher levels or in deliberate planning situations.” [12] The textbook's two systems look much the same at first: The management textbook contrasts a reflective system that is logical and deliberate with a reactive system that is quick, intuitive and driven by emotion or habit. [13] Its default pairing, though, runs the other way: “Nonprogrammed decisions will generally need to be processed via the reflective system in our brains in order for us to reach a good decision. But with programmed decisions, heuristics can allow decision makers to switch to the quick, reactive system and then move along quickly to other issues.” [14] The textbook does leave a door open: The management textbook grants that the reactive system can save lives when time is short and works far better when it rests on experience and expertise. [15] Put together, a novel and fast-moving situation is where the two would most often send a manager in opposite directions. Time shows the same split: The management textbook lists time pressure among the barriers to good decisions, since little time to gather and weigh information makes a good novel decision much less likely. [16] MCDP 1 holds that failing to decide hands the initiative to the enemy, so as a basis for action almost any decision beats none. [17] For a business in a fast market, treating decision speed as a capability to build, rather than a pressure to endure, is the more useful stance.

## Mistakes, blame and the cost of waiting

Treatment of error is where a business reader may be most surprised. MCDP 1 on the mistakes of junior leaders: “We should deal with such errors leni-ently; there must be no "zero defects" mentality. Abolishing "zero defects" means that we do not stifle boldness or initia-tive through the threat of punishment.” [18] It is just as firm in the other direction: MCDP 1 tells leaders to deal severely with inaction and timidity and refuses to accept a lack of orders as an excuse for doing nothing. [19] The textbook's nearest match is its involvement-oriented organisation: “Control in high-involvement organizations is diffused through many groups and is commonly focused on task accomplishment and overcoming obstacles, with a de-emphasis on fixing blame with a particular individual for performance failures. In many control-oriented management systems, the reins of control are firmly held by the hierarchy, and the activities of individuals are carefully controlled.” [20] On sunk effort, the textbook warns: The management textbook asks managers to see that pressing on down a wrong path is not progress, and to be ready to rethink a decision and change direction. [21] MCDP 1 gives the field version: MCDP 1 tells commanders who shift their main effort to move it toward what is working, not to pour more into what is failing. [22] The business lesson is that blame-free control works only if waiting carries a cost too; without that, it turns into permission to do nothing.

## Doctrine is not a framework

The last parallel concerns what a body of rules is for. MCDP 1 states its own standing: “Our doctrine does not consist of procedures to be applied in specific situations so much as it sets forth general guidance that requires judg-ment in application. Therefore, while authoritative, doctrine is not prescriptive.” [23] Merton, as the management textbook quotes him, saw what happens when rules lose that standing: “(2) Such devotion to the rules leads to their transformation into absolutes; they are no longer conceived as relative to a set of purposes. (3) This interferes with ready adaptation under special conditions not clearly envisaged by those who drew up the general rules.” [24] The Org Topologies primer turns the same worry on management frameworks: The Org Topologies primer calls the off-the-shelf management framework a consultancy pitch that tempts firms to follow the crowd rather than understand and design their own organisation. [25] For a business, the test of any playbook is whether it still lets people act well in a situation its authors did not foresee.
